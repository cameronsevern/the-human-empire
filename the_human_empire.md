# The Human Empire
## How Technology, Resources, and Wars Over Ideas Made One Intertwined World

**Book plan · Eight parts · 93 chapters**

## Preface

I never paid attention to history in school. It was boring. All they did was make us memorize dates, and I never understood the why.

It interests me now. Conflicts and wars have shaped the world as we know it, and I imagine that goes all the way back to the beginning of human history. I'm fascinated by empires, how they form and how they fail. I suspect technology has a lot to do with these conflicts, and resources as well. It all makes much more sense to me when I can understand the perspectives of every party involved. I think being driven to war shows that each side believes something so strongly that its people are willing to die for it. There is a war over ideas in there too.

The idea for this book started with a narrower question: why is Israel so important to America? That question is no longer the focus. What really interests me is the path we took to get here, how the world became so intertwined, and why people are so impassioned by its conflicts.

I am not a soldier, a military person, or a historian. I'm a scientist and technologist who is curious how we got here, and I want to hear the story from the beginning. I want to understand why we got into these conflicts and what drives people. I want to understand how philosophy, science, technology, and human civilization evolved together.

This book is my way of understanding all of human history through a lens I personally care about. In some ways it is a retrospective on humanity, written at a time when many people are concerned that we are near our end. The rise and fall of empires is a key part of the story. Then it zooms out to look at the rise of humans as a whole and what our eventual downfall could look like. That is the human empire.

I wrote it for myself first, and I have made it free to read. After this preface I step out of the way, and the history is told without me as narrator.

This book was written with AI. I see AI as one of the many technologies that have caused great change in human civilization. One thing it allows is a book like this, made by one person, with a level of rigor, depth, breadth, and scope that was not possible before. The story of AI is the story of humans, and here AI is used to tell that story.

## The vision

**What the book is.** A single history of humanity from the first hearths to the present, told through the rise and fall of empires. It ends by treating humanity itself as an empire and asking how that one might end.

**What it explains.** Three things, in every period:

- How the world became intertwined, so that a person's life came to depend on more and more distant strangers.
- Why people fought, and what they believed strongly enough to die for.
- How philosophy, science, technology, and civilization changed one another.

**Who it is for.** A curious reader with no background in history and some comfort with science and technology. Every historical term is explained when it first appears. Mechanisms, whether of a steam engine or a tax system, are explained clearly enough that the reader could explain them to someone else.

**How it is told.** As a story, by a confident narrator who states ideas plainly and shows each with one vivid example. There is no "I" after the preface, and "we" means humans in general. The style sheet (`style.md`) sets the voice. Every chapter sets out to answer one question, whether or not its title asks it. The order is roughly chronological and steps out of sequence where that makes the story clearer. Every conflict is told from the position of each party involved, including the people who had no say in it.

**What it shows.** Maps wherever the argument depends on where something was, diagrams wherever it depends on how something worked, and pictures of the surviving evidence. Images are a normal part of every chapter.

**What it values.** Clarity first. The book is long because the subject is, no chapter has a fixed length, and none is cut for length alone. A chapter is finished when a reader can say why things happened, not only what happened.

**Where it stops.** The account runs to the end of 2025. Anything after that date is labeled as a possible future.

## The argument

**Thesis.** Each jump in the scale of human cooperation was made possible by a technology, held together by an idea, and paid for by people who had no say in it. That is now true at the scale of the planet.

**The running dispute.** Do resources and technology drive history, or do ideas? The preface suspects both. Each chapter supplies evidence, and the final chapter weighs it.

**The title.** "The human empire" has two meanings. It means the empires humans built, whose rise and fall fill Parts II through VII. It also means humanity as a single expanding power, introduced in Part I and examined in Part VIII with the questions asked of every empire before it.

**The shape.** Each part widens the circle of people an ordinary person depends on.

## The eight questions

These run through the whole book. Each chapter takes up the ones its subject raises.

1. What **resources** did people need?
2. What did their **technology** make possible?
3. How did they **organize** strangers to work together?
4. Which **ideas** made sacrifice meaningful?
5. Who held **power** over the decision?
6. What sustained the arrangement, and when did its **stability** give way?
7. **Why did they fight?** The preface proposes that people go to war for beliefs they would die for. Each war chapter tests that against the alternatives: pay, loot, loyalty to kin, compulsion, or the lack of any other choice.
8. **What did people know?** What could they explain about nature at the time, and who was allowed to say what was true?

## The chapters

Each chapter has a brief and a *working answer*. The working answer is the hypothesis the chapter starts from, and the finished chapter says whether it held. This list tracks what the book must say. The order, the titles and where one chapter ends and the next begins are settled as the chapters are written, so the list will drift from the finished book and is brought back into line from time to time. Five indexes after the chapter list show where each technology, conflict, empire, and idea is covered, and where each running thread is picked up. ## Part I — Origins
*Scale: the band and its neighbors.*

### 1. What is a human?
*Drafted: `chapters/01-what-is-a-human/chapter.md`.*

Answer the title in the first line: a human is an ape. Explain why this ape is odd, weak alone and strong in a group, and trace how that came about: walking upright, the helpless child raised by many hands, the stone flake, fire and cooking, language and gossip, and knowledge that piles up across generations, shown by the Franklin expedition dying where Inuit families lived. Then spend a day and a year in a foraging band: hunger, danger, dead children, shared meat, no chief, reputation, quarrels. End on what that life left in us: the craving for sugar and fat, old fears, the pain of being left out, the sense of fairness, us and them, and status.

*Working answer:* A human is an ape that lives by other humans, by what they share, teach and remember. Our instincts were shaped by a very long time in small bands, and everything later in the book has to work with them.

**Still to say in Part I.** These were planned for chapter 1 and moved out, or came up while writing it. They belong somewhere in the next chapters:

- The other kinds of human, including Neanderthals and Denisovans, and why only we are left. A drafted passage is in `chapters/held/the-others.md`.
- The spread out of Africa into every habitable continent.
- The large animals that disappeared soon after people arrived, and how far people caused it.
- Whether early humans made war.
- The first art, burials and signs of belief.
- Ties, gifts and trade between bands.

### 2. Who shares the last meal?
Open at a seasonal gathering. Use the movement of stone tools and ornaments to investigate how mobile communities kept ties across distance. Ask what sharing food did for a family that might need shelter next winter, and how far objects can take us toward knowing anyone's motives.

*Working answer:* Sharing worked as insurance. Materials moved hundreds of kilometers, which shows the ties existed. The motives are inferred.

### 3. Why stay for the harvest?
Open with the interval between sowing and eating. What had to be stored, tended, or given up to make waiting worthwhile? Compare early cultivation in Southwest Asia with an independent path in East Asia. Weigh stored food against the work of guarding it and the cost of leaving a planted field.

*Working answer:* Farming was a slow commitment over many generations, not a single decision. Each investment made leaving more costly, even where skeletons suggest early farmers were less healthy than foragers.

### 4. Who owns the grain?
Enter the city of Uruk through a ration record. Trace the barley from field to storehouse to worker, as bread and as beer, which was part of the ration. Explain writing as a technology: what the marks recorded, who could read them, and what they could not yet say. Ask what recourse a worker had if the official counted wrong.

*Working answer:* Writing in Mesopotamia grew out of accounting. A record let officials provision strangers, and it gave power to whoever could read it. This chapter sets up a question the book returns to in chapter 88.

### 5. What makes a king necessary?
Set Egypt's royal claims beside the practical work of feeding laborers along the Nile. Compare the authority of a household, a temple, and a palace during a poor harvest. End with the question neighboring cities faced when one ruler could feed an army away from home.

*Working answer:* The ability to store and redistribute grain became the ability to command labor and soldiers. Whether early rulers solved a coordination problem or captured a surplus is the central disagreement.

## Part II — Conquest
*Scale: the city and the first empires.*

### 6. What changes when one city rules many?
Begin with Sargon of Akkad, who brought the rival cities of Mesopotamia under one ruler. Ask what he had to invent to hold them: appointed governors, garrisons, a permanent force fed from royal stores, and a daughter installed as high priestess in a conquered city. Follow the empire's breakup within a few generations and the argument over whether drought, invasion, or revolt ended it. Then move to Babylon under Hammurabi and the laws he had carved in stone. Read several of them to see what they assume about property, debt, slaves, and rank. Ask whether the laws protected the weak, as the king claimed, or advertised his justice.

*Working answer:* Ruling many cities required new tools: governors loyal to the king, soldiers who served year-round, and a claim to rule that reached beyond any one city's god. Hammurabi's laws show a ruler presenting himself as the source of justice. How far they were applied in actual courts is uncertain.

### 7. The cargo that held kingdoms together
Use the Uluburun shipwreck, a Bronze Age trading vessel, to reconstruct the many hands behind a bronze tool. Explain the wheel, the domestication of the horse on the steppe, and the war chariot that joined them, and what a chariot force cost a palace to keep. Move forward to the breakdown of several eastern Mediterranean powers, among them the Hittite empire, the Mycenaean Greek palaces, and Egypt's empire in the Levant. Test what interrupted trade can explain alongside local conflict and environmental stress, including why some ports survived.

*Working answer:* Bronze needed copper and tin from far-apart sources, and chariots needed horses, craftsmen, and trained crews, so palaces depended on supplies none of them controlled. The collapse had several causes and historians do not agree on their order.

**The wheel needs more room than this entry gives it.** It is a subject in its own right, not a step towards the chariot, and may become its own chapter beside the horse and the steppe, under a question such as "Why did it take so long to invent the wheel?". Chapter 6 mentions the potter's wheel only in passing. To say, wherever it lands:

- The potter's wheel and the first wheeled carts both appear about 5,500 years ago, in or near Mesopotamia.
- The hard part is the axle and its fit, which probably needed metal tools.
- Wheeled carts spread across Europe and the Middle East within a few centuries, so the invention was made once or close to it.
- The Americas had wheeled toys but no carts, since there was no animal to pull one.
- Carts let people live on the grasslands with their herds, which sets up the horse peoples of the steppe.

### 8. The price of defying Assyria
Reconstruct the siege of Lachish from the Assyrian palace reliefs and the excavated city. Explain how iron is smelted and worked, why its ores are far more common than tin, and what that meant for the size of an army. Compare what the reliefs show of siegecraft and deportation with the traces in the ground. Ask who was meant to see the images and what the king wanted the next city to do.

*Working answer:* Iron made it possible to arm far more men than bronze had. Assyria advertised its violence so that surrender would look cheaper than resistance, and it used deportation as policy to break up rebellious populations and move labor.

### 9. How Persia governed at a distance
Begin with a travel ration issued on the road to Persepolis. Trace what made an imperial authorization good for food far from the capital: officials, translators, tax arrangements, local authorities. Explain the religion of the Persian kings, founded on the teaching of Zoroaster, in which a good creator and the truth are opposed by a destructive spirit and the Lie, and show how the kings used it to describe rebels. Ask when keeping a local elite in office served a conqueror and when it gave rebellion room to grow.

*Working answer:* Persia ruled cheaply by standardizing procedures and leaving local elites in place. The same arrangement left potential rebels with their resources intact.

### 10. How did a defeated people keep their god?
Begin with Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem and its temple, and the deportation of Judah's leading families, the same policy Assyria used in chapter 8. In the ancient world a conquered people usually concluded that its god had lost. Follow the exiles who concluded the opposite: that their god was the only god and had used Babylon to punish them. Trace how older laws, histories, and prophecies were gathered and edited into scripture during and after the exile, so that a people came to be defined by a text and a law that could be carried anywhere. Follow the return that Persia permitted and the rebuilding of the temple. Set the biblical account beside archaeology and Babylonian and Persian records, and show where scholars disagree about when the texts were written.

*Working answer:* Exile turned the religion of a small kingdom into a faith that did not depend on a king, a territory, or a temple. That is why it survived the loss of all three, and why Christianity and Islam could later grow from it. When belief in a single god became firm, and when the texts took their shape, are debated.

### 11. Why would a stranger accept a piece of metal?
Begin with one of the first coins, struck in the kingdom of Lydia in western Anatolia. Explain what a stamp on a lump of metal guaranteed and who stood behind the guarantee. Go back to the older system of debts recorded on clay in Mesopotamia, and across to the bronze coinage that arose separately in China. Follow a coin from a mint to a soldier's pay to a market stall and back to a tax collector. This chapter starts a thread that later chapters continue with paper money, banks, companies owned by shareholders, and government debt, and that ends with payment by phone in chapter 83.

*Working answer:* Coins let strangers trade without knowing or trusting each other, because both trusted the issuer. Rulers had their own reason to mint them, since coins made it easier to pay soldiers and collect taxes. Whether trade or the needs of states came first is debated.

### 12. What do you owe the station you were born into?
Begin with a hymn from the Vedas, composed in an early form of Sanskrit and memorized word for word by families of priests for centuries before it was written down. Explain the sacrifices the hymns accompanied and the priests who controlled them. Trace the idea of four ranked orders of society, with priests at the top, and the people placed below all four. Follow the later teachers who asked what lay behind the ritual and arrived at a set of linked ideas: a self that is reborn, actions that carry consequences across lives, duty according to one's place, and release from the cycle. Ask who gained from the ranking and how strictly it was lived. Note that "Hinduism" as a single name for these many traditions is much later.

*Working answer:* These traditions gave Indian societies a shared account of duty, rebirth, and rank that held together without one state or one church behind it. Ranking by birth proved to be among the most durable social orders in history. How rigid it was before modern times, and how far British rule hardened it, is debated.

### 13. How should one live when states are at war?
Set four settings side by side: the Greek cities, China's Warring States, the Ganges plain, and the kingdom of Judah in exile. Follow Confucians, Mohists, and Legalists competing for a ruler's ear, and the Daoists who advised walking away from rulers altogether. Follow Greek thinkers proposing that nature could be explained without the gods. Follow the Buddha's community, which broke with the priestly religion of chapter 12, and return briefly to the exiles of chapter 10. Ask why so many lasting traditions of argument date from centuries of fragmentation and war.

*Working answer:* Where many states competed, rulers needed advisers and thinkers could find rival patrons. Grouping these traditions into a single "Axial Age" is a modern idea and is contested.

### 14. Why did the cities that fought an empire build one?
Begin with the Greek cities' refusal to submit to Persia and the wars that followed. Explain the oared warship that won the sea battles and the citizen rowers who pulled it. Explain Athenian democracy, in which citizen men voted directly on war and law while women, foreigners, and slaves had no vote. Trace how Athens turned a defensive alliance into an empire of its own, collecting tribute from cities that had joined as equals, and how that led to a long war with Sparta that ruined both. Ask what a Greek rower, a Spartan subject laborer, a city that tried to leave the alliance, and a Persian governor each had at stake. Note that nearly all the narrative sources are Greek, and show what Persian records add.

*Working answer:* The Greeks fought Persia in the name of freedom and then fought one another over who would dominate. Athenian democracy and Athenian empire grew together, since the fleet that held the empire was rowed by the poorer citizens who voted for it.

### 15. What did Alexander take over?
Begin in Macedon, where King Philip built a professional army around a long pike and brought the Greek cities under his control. Follow his son Alexander as he conquered the Persian empire of chapter 9 in a single decade and marched as far as the Indus. Show how he kept Persian governors, roads, and treasuries, and how his own Macedonians resented it. Follow his generals as they divided the conquests into rival kingdoms that spread Greek language and city life from Egypt to Afghanistan. Go to Alexandria, where kings paid for a library and for scholars who systematized geometry and measured the size of the Earth. Ask what a Macedonian foot soldier, a Persian noble, and an Egyptian priest each made of the new rulers. Note that the surviving accounts were written centuries later.

*Working answer:* Alexander did not replace Persian government so much as take it over. The kingdoms that followed him made Greek the common language of the eastern Mediterranean for centuries, which the chapters on Rome and Christianity depend on. What his soldiers and his new subjects made of his aims is hard to recover.

### 16. Can conquest change a conqueror?
Begin in the empire that rose in northern India after Alexander's army withdrew. Read its emperor Ashoka's account of the suffering he caused in Kalinga beside the orders he addressed to his officers. Ask what a conquered family could expect to change once the emperor publicly expressed remorse. Investigate the distance between an inscription's promises and the reach of an official.

*Working answer:* The inscriptions show a ruler using moral language, drawn from the Buddhist teaching of chapter 13, as a tool of government. Almost all the evidence is his own words, so what changed for his subjects is largely unknown.

### 17. What did a unified China cost?
Follow a measure of grain across a former state boundary after the Qin conquests. Trace how common measures, a single coinage, written orders, roads, and compulsory labor reached a farming family. Show the Legalist ideas of chapter 13 put into practice. Investigate why the dynasty fell so quickly and what its successor, the Han, retained.

*Working answer:* Standardization and conscription won the wars. The same demands helped provoke the revolts. The Han kept the machinery and changed how it was justified.

### 18. Why build a wall against people on horseback?
Begin on the northern frontier of Han China, facing the Xiongnu, a confederation of mounted herders that was an empire in its own right. Explain the composite bow and why a mounted archer was so hard for a farming state to defeat. Explain also why the herders wanted the grain, silk, and metal goods the farmers made. Follow the Han court's long argument between buying peace with gifts and royal brides and paying for war. Trace the walls, garrisons, and envoys the Han pushed westward, and the caravan routes later called the Silk Roads that grew along them. Ask what a conscript on the wall, a Xiongnu herder, and a Central Asian merchant each wanted. Note that almost every written source is Chinese.

*Working answer:* The grassland and the farmland needed each other and could not govern each other. Their relationship swung among trade, tribute, and raiding for many centuries, down to the events of chapter 40, and long-distance trade across Asia grew partly out of it.

## Part III — Faith
*Scale: communities that outlast the governments around them.*

### 19. How Rome recruited its rivals
Begin with an Italian ally obliged to supply soldiers. Trace the relationship among service, land, plunder, and citizenship through the wars of expansion. Give the wars against Carthage their full weight: how Rome built a fleet from nothing, survived Hannibal's invasion of Italy by drawing on allied manpower, and ended by destroying the rival city. Then follow the Italian allies' revolt. Explain Roman law and its idea of the citizen as a holder of rights, and the Stoic philosophy that many of Rome's rulers professed. Ask why fighting Rome could become a way to demand a place inside it.

*Working answer:* Rome grew by turning defeated communities into sources of soldiers and by granting degrees of membership. That reserve of manpower is what let it outlast Carthage. Whether the rebel allies later wanted citizenship or independence is debated.

### 20. How did an executed preacher's followers outlast the empire that killed him?
Begin in Roman-ruled Judea with a Jewish teacher from Galilee, crucified by the Roman governor. Explain what can be known about Jesus from accounts written decades later by believers. Follow Paul, who carried the message to non-Jews in the cities of the eastern Mediterranean and argued that they did not need to adopt Jewish law. Set this beside the Jewish revolts against Rome and the destruction of the Temple, after which Judaism was rebuilt around teachers and the study of the law. Ask why people joined the new movement: the promise of resurrection, a community that fed its poor and buried its dead, and a welcome that extended to slaves and women. Follow the periods of persecution and the emperor Constantine's decision to favor the faith.

*Working answer:* Christianity spread along the roads, cities, and common languages of the empire, among people of every rank, without state backing for nearly three centuries. Why it grew so steadily is debated, with explanations ranging from its message to its care for members during epidemics. Its separation from Judaism was gradual and left a hostility that returns in later chapters.

### 21. Why join a community of believers?
Compare the support available to travelers on a Buddhist trade route with the care a late Roman Christian community offered strangers. Examine patronage, hospitality, burial, and belief. Ask how obligations changed when rulers became patrons and dissent acquired political consequences.

*Working answer:* Religious communities offered trust and welfare that traveled with a person. Once rulers sponsored them, belonging and dissent became political acts.

### 22. When an emperor could no longer pay
Build two accounts of the Roman fifth century, one from a western government losing its North African revenue and one from Constantinople, which also had to hold a frontier against Persia under the Parthians and then the Sasanians, a rival of equal rank. Follow soldiers, taxpayers, and landowners to explain how imperial government vanished in one region and endured in another.

*Working answer:* An army is a payroll. When the West lost its richest tax base, soldiers and landowners made local arrangements. The East kept its revenue and its government.

### 23. What did the first believers hear?
Begin in Mecca with the message itself: one God, a coming judgment, and duties to the poor and the orphan. Follow the community to Medina, where belonging came to rest on belief more than clan. Explain that most narrative sources were written generations later, and show what the earliest documents can and cannot confirm.

*Working answer:* The new community offered a bond that cut across kinship, which let rival clans act together. The evidence for its first decades is thinner than the traditional accounts suggest.

### 24. After the Arab conquests
Begin with the long war between the Roman and Sasanian Persian empires, which left both exhausted on the eve of the conquests. Then go to Egypt and ask who would collect the next tax payment. Trace the roles of conquerors, existing officials, and Christian communities under early Muslim rule. Explain the dispute over who should succeed Muhammad, which became the lasting division between Sunni and Shia. Separate the pace of military conquest from the much longer histories of language change and conversion.

*Working answer:* Two worn-out empires faced a new and united opponent. The conquerors kept the existing administrators and tax records. Conquest took decades. Arabic and Islam took centuries to become the majority language and religion.

### 25. Who kept the world's knowledge?
Follow a Greek text on geometry into Arabic in Baghdad under the Abbasid caliphs, written on paper, a technique that had come from China. Show the numerals and the zero that came from Indian mathematicians working under the Gupta kings and their successors. Explain what algebra, the study of optics, and hospital medicine added to what was inherited. Ask who paid for translation and learning, and what they wanted from it. Follow the same books onward to Spain and, in Latin translation, to the universities of chapter 35.

*Working answer:* Knowledge accumulated where cheap writing material, wealthy patrons, and large cities met. It was extended there, not merely preserved for a later Europe.

### 26. What did the examination hall build?
Go to China under the Tang and Song dynasties. Follow a candidate into the examination that chose officials by written test, and ask why a government would recruit that way. Explain woodblock printing, gunpowder, the magnetic compass, and the first government paper money, and what each was first used for. Trace the canal that carried southern rice to northern capitals, and the iron and coal output of Song workshops. End with the Song court losing northern China to mounted invaders despite its wealth and its gunpowder weapons.

*Working answer:* Song China was by most measures the richest and most technically inventive society of its time, and the examinations tied its educated families to the state. Wealth and invention did not make it safe from its mounted neighbors. Why this did not become an industrial revolution is a question chapter 48 returns to.

### 27. Who rules a sea lane?
Begin in the Strait of Malacca, the narrow passage that every ship sailing between India and China had to use. Explain the monsoon winds that set the sailing calendar for the whole Indian Ocean. Show how Srivijaya, based on Sumatra, grew rich by controlling the strait and its ports, with little territory. Follow the Chola kingdom of southern India as it sent a fleet across the Bay of Bengal to raid Srivijaya. Then go inland to the Khmer capital at Angkor, where reservoirs and canals supported one of the largest cities of its time. Follow the same winds west to the East African coast, where Swahili cities such as Kilwa sold gold and ivory from the interior, some of it from the stone-walled capital of Great Zimbabwe. Trace how Hindu and Buddhist ideas, and later Islam, traveled these routes with merchants and teachers. Weigh the evidence, which comes from inscriptions and from Chinese and Arab travelers.

*Working answer:* Power at sea came from controlling narrow passages and ports, and a rival with a fleet could take it away. Religions and writing systems spread across the region largely through trade and the choices of local rulers, with little conquest. How that happened is debated.

### 27a. Who built the statues on Easter Island?
*Added after Part IV was drafted. Written: `chapters/34-pacific/chapter.md`.*

The Polynesians as one people across a triangle of ocean. How a navigator found an island without instruments. What the canoes carried, and what Rapa Nui was like. The quarry, the statues and how they were moved. The story of self-inflicted collapse set against the evidence of rats, rock gardens and old DNA. What the same people built on Hawaii, Tonga and New Zealand. Disease, the slave raid of 1862 and the script nobody can read.

*Working answer:* The islanders built them, and the famous collapse did not happen until outsiders arrived. The same settlers with the same kit built very different societies on different islands.

### 27b. Who were the Vikings?
*Added after Part IV was drafted. Written: `chapters/35-vikings/chapter.md`.*

Lindisfarne. The ship that made the raids possible. Chiefs who needed silver. Paying raiders to go away. The Swedes on the Russian rivers, trading furs and slaves for Arab silver. Settlement in England and Normandy. Iceland's assembly. Greenland and Newfoundland. Kings, conversion and the end of raiding. 1066.

*Working answer:* "Viking" was a job. The raids ended when the targets fortified and the chiefs became Christian kings. Those who stayed abroad became the English, the Russians and the Normans.

### 27c. Why are there so many castles in Europe?
*Added after Part IV was drafted. Written: `chapters/36-castles/chapter.md`.*

The castle at Hastings. The broken Frankish empire and raiders from three sides. The mound and the tower. What a knight cost and how he was paid in land. The serf's bargain and Domesday Book. The heavy plough, the horse collar, three fields and mills. Towns, charters and the first universities. Gothic cathedrals. Magna Carta and the first parliaments.

*Working answer:* Weak kings left defence and government to whoever held a fortified house. Because the lords kept their walls, kings had to bargain with them, and consent to taxation came out of the bargaining.

### 28. Why march to Jerusalem?
Set the scene in western Europe after Rome, where a pope had crowned Charlemagne emperor in 800 and popes and emperors had competed for authority ever since. Then take the pope's call in 1095 and the promise attached to it. Follow a knight who mortgaged his land to go, a Jewish community in the Rhineland attacked by crusaders on their way east, the Byzantine emperor who had asked for help and received an army he could not control, and the Muslim rulers of Syria who at first saw one more invader among many. Trace the capture of Jerusalem, its recapture by Saladin, and the crusade that ended by sacking Christian Constantinople. Ask what each party believed it was fighting for.

*Working answer:* Many crusaders went at great personal cost for the sake of their souls, which makes this one of the clearest cases of war for belief. Land, status, trade, and papal authority were also at stake, and the two sides did not understand the conflict in the same terms.

### 29. Who could cross the Sahara?
Follow salt south and gold north through the markets taxed in turn by three empires: Ghana, Mali, and Songhai. Ask what caravan organizers, miners, rulers, and scholars each contributed and who could levy a toll. Use the pilgrimage of Mali's ruler Mansa Musa to show how access to wealth became relationships far beyond his borders. End with a Moroccan army crossing the desert with firearms to defeat Songhai.

*Working answer:* Power lay in controlling routes and markets more than mines. The pilgrimage converted gold into reputation and scholarly ties.

### 30. A hemisphere apart
Begin in a Maya city with a carved stone recording one king's capture of another, in the most fully developed writing system of the Americas. Explain the maize, beans, and squash that fed these cities, the calendar and the zero their scribes worked out independently, and what it meant to build without draft animals, wheeled transport, or iron. Follow the abandonment of the southern Maya cities and the argument over drought, war, and exhausted land. Move to the Andes, where states stored freeze-dried food, moved goods by llama, and kept accounts on knotted cords with no writing at all. Add Cahokia, the earthwork city on the Mississippi. Ask what the absence of horses, cattle, and the diseases that came with them meant, which chapters 36 and 37 return to.

*Working answer:* The Americas produced cities, states, writing, and mathematics with no contact with the rest of the world, which shows these were not accidents of one region. A different set of animals shaped transport, war, and disease. When the Maya cities were abandoned, the Maya people and their languages carried on.

## Part IV — Expansion
*Scale: connected hemispheres.*

### 31. The army that moved with its horses
Reconstruct a Mongol campaign from the demands of spare horses, pasture, scouting, and siege expertise. Consider a city's decision to surrender with the information its inhabitants had. Follow merchants and displaced artisans into the trade networks conquest reshaped.

*Working answer:* Mongol armies won on mobility and on engineers taken from conquered peoples. The same conquests that destroyed cities joined the trade of Eurasia more closely than before.

### 32. After the plague, who could bargain?
Follow the Black Death along commercial routes, then compare surviving workers and landholders in England and Egypt. Investigate how land tenure, labor rules, and irrigation affected who gained bargaining power when hands became scarce.

*Working answer:* The same shock had opposite effects. Where workers could move and landlords competed for them, wages rose. Where farming depended on centrally maintained irrigation, the loss of labor damaged the system itself. The Egyptian side of the comparison is contested.

### 33. A city behind walls, a fleet beyond them
Begin at Constantinople in 1453. Explain how a large bronze cannon was cast, moved, and fired, and what it did to a wall built for an earlier kind of war. Reconstruct the Ottoman need to feed besiegers alongside the defenders' efforts to repair breaches and get relief by sea. Examine how repopulating the city turned a prize into a capital, and what resettlement meant for those compelled to move.

*Working answer:* Cannon mattered, and so did supply and control of the water. The conquest became permanent through forced resettlement and restored markets.

### 33a. What was the Renaissance?
*Added after Part IV was drafted. Written: `chapters/43-renaissance/chapter.md`.*

Brunelleschi's dome. Why Florence: cloth, the florin, banks, schooling and no king. Petrarch, the book hunters and the return of Greek. Perspective and the rise of the artist. Why bankers and popes paid. Leonardo's letter and notebooks. The French invasion of 1494, Machiavelli, the sack of Rome and the bill for St Peter's.

*Working answer:* A movement of a few thousand people in rich, kingless, competing cities, who set out to equal the ancients. It left the study of the past as a different world, painting from observation and the cult of talent.

### 34. How do you cross an ocean and come back?
Begin with a Portuguese captain off West Africa who must sail far out into the Atlantic to catch a wind home. Explain the ship, its sails, the compass, how latitude was found from the sun and stars, and why longitude could not yet be found at all. Set this beside three other traditions: the monsoon sailing of the Indian Ocean, the vast Chinese fleets of the early 1400s, and the Polynesian navigators who settled the Pacific without instruments. Ask why Portuguese and Spanish courts paid for these voyages and why the Chinese court stopped paying for its own. End with the first voyages to India and the Caribbean, and with guns mounted on ships.

*Working answer:* Europeans were not the only or the best ocean sailors. What set them apart was a strong reason to bypass the merchants who controlled the trade in gold and spices, rival courts willing to fund the attempt, and a hard-won understanding of the Atlantic winds.

### 35. Why could one monk not be silenced?
Set the scene with the universities of medieval Europe, where Aristotle had been reconciled with Christian teaching, and with the humanist scholars who went back to ancient texts in their original languages. Then follow a pamphlet by Martin Luther from a Wittenberg press to a tavern reading. Explain movable type, what a print run cost, and who could afford one. Show that the press carried slander, forgery, and rumor as readily as argument. Trace how an argument about salvation became a peasants' revolt, a prince's opportunity, and more than a century of religious war ending in the Thirty Years' War. Ask what people were killing and dying for, and what else was at stake in church land and princely independence.

*Working answer:* Printing let dissent spread faster than it could be suppressed, and rulers had their own reasons to protect it. Sincere belief and material interest were bound together, and sorting them is the chapter's work.

### 36. Why fight alongside the newcomers?
Approach the siege of the Mexica capital Tenochtitlan through the decisions of Tlaxcalan allies, Mexica defenders, and Spanish expedition leaders. Explain the Mexica belief that the sun had to be fed with human blood, and how taking captives for sacrifice shaped both their wars and their neighbors' resentment. Trace rivalries, epidemic disease, the causeways, and control of the lake. Ask what allied communities expected to win and how they later pressed their claims.

*Working answer:* The fall of Tenochtitlan was in large part a war among indigenous powers, fought alongside a small Spanish force and a smallpox epidemic. The allies' later petitions show what they believed they had been promised.

### 37. What could a mountain of silver buy?
Begin with the Spanish seizure of the Inca empire during a war of succession that followed an epidemic. Follow the conquerors to the silver mountain of Potosí. Trace the silver through imperial taxation and across the Pacific to markets in Asia. Explain how a banker's written promise let money move without moving metal, and how a king borrowed against silver not yet mined. Reconstruct a conscripted worker's obligations alongside a merchant's calculation. Ask why a crown with enormous mineral wealth still depended on creditors.

*Working answer:* The silver passed through Spain to its lenders and on to China, where silver was in high demand. For the first time a single commodity linked the economies of four continents.

### 38. How do you rule people who do not share your faith?
Set three Muslim-ruled empires of the 1500s and 1600s side by side: the Ottomans under Suleiman, Safavid Iran, and Mughal India. Explain what cannon and muskets cost and why only a large treasury could field them. Compare three answers to religious difference. The Ottomans gave Christian and Jewish communities their own courts and leaders, and took Christian boys to be raised as the sultan's soldiers and officials. The Safavids imposed Shia Islam on a largely Sunni population, and fought the Sunni Ottomans along a border that still divides the two traditions. The Mughal emperor Akbar allied with Hindu princes and lifted the tax on non-Muslims, and a later emperor restored it. Note the Sufi teachers who carried Islam through much of India and Southeast Asia, and the Sikh faith that arose in Punjab under Mughal rule and took up arms after its leaders were executed. Follow the Mughal land tax from a village to the treasury, since this is the system the East India Company takes over in chapter 44. Ask what a Hindu prince, a soldier taken as a boy from a Christian village, and an Armenian merchant in the Safavid capital each gained and gave up.

*Working answer:* Each empire ruled large populations whose faith or sect differed from the ruler's, and each found a different answer: managed autonomy, enforced conversion, or alliance. By most estimates Mughal India was among the richest states in the world. The common label "gunpowder empires" is debated.

### 39. Why would a country close its doors?
Begin with Portuguese firearms reaching Japan in 1543, in the middle of a century of war among regional lords, and with how quickly Japanese smiths copied them. Follow the three commanders who unified the country, and the invasion of Korea that the second of them launched as a first step toward conquering China. Explain the Korean kingdom he attacked: a state run on Confucian lines by examined officials, with an alphabet designed at court so that ordinary people could read. Follow the Korean admiral whose fleet cut the invaders' supplies, the Chinese army that intervened, and the ruin left behind. Then explain the settlement of the Tokugawa rulers: peasants disarmed, lords' families kept in the capital as hostages, Christianity banned, and foreign trade confined to a few ports. Ask what a lord, a farmer, a Japanese Christian, and a Korean villager each faced.

*Working answer:* The Tokugawa limited foreign contact to remove threats to their own control, namely missionaries and lords grown rich on trade, and not out of ignorance of the world. The result was more than two centuries of internal peace, growing cities, and wide literacy, which matters in chapter 53. "Closed country" is a later label that overstates the isolation.

### 40. Who closed the steppe?
Begin with Russian fur traders and Cossacks crossing the Ural mountains and following the rivers east to the Pacific in about sixty years, taking furs as tribute from the peoples of Siberia. Turn to the Manchus, from the lands northeast of China, who conquered Ming China and founded the Qing dynasty. Follow the two expanding empires to their meeting on the Amur river and to a border treaty negotiated through Jesuit interpreters. Then follow the Qing campaigns that destroyed the Zunghars, the last independent empire of the steppe, and brought Mongolia, Tibet, and the far west under Qing rule. Explain why firearms, forts, and wagonloads of grain ended the horseman's advantage. Tell it from the positions of a Siberian hunter, a Zunghar herder, a Cossack, and a Qing soldier.

*Working answer:* Two farming empires with guns, forts, and bureaucracies divided between them the grasslands that mounted peoples had dominated since chapter 7. The borders they drew largely define Russia and China today. The Zunghars were destroyed so completely that some historians call it genocide.

### 41. Who gets to say what is true about nature?
Begin with a telescope pointed at Jupiter and the argument over whether to trust it. Explain what Copernicus proposed, what Galileo saw, and what Newton's mathematics joined together. Trace the instruments, societies, and journals that let strangers check one another's claims. Show the debt to earlier astronomers writing in Arabic, and the appetite of navies and gunners for better mathematics.

*Working answer:* Authority over nature shifted toward claims that could be measured and checked by others. The shift was gradual, it was built on older work, and the label "Scientific Revolution" is itself debated.

## Part V — Wealth
*Scale: an industrial globe.*

### 42. The kingdoms on the other shore
Begin with a letter of 1526 in which the Christian king of Kongo protests to the king of Portugal that traders are carrying off his people. Survey the states Europeans met on the Atlantic coast of Africa, among them Benin, Kongo, and later Oyo, Dahomey, and Asante. Explain why Europeans stayed in coastal forts for centuries: disease killed them inland, and African states were strong enough to set the terms. Follow how captives were taken, through war, raiding, court sentences, and debt, and what they were sold for. Ask how European demand changed the wars. Tell it from the positions of a ruler who sold captives, a ruler who tried to stop, a coastal merchant, and a person captured and marched to the sea.

*Working answer:* African states were neither passive victims nor equal partners. They controlled the supply on the coast for three centuries, while European buyers controlled the ocean and the demand. The trade enriched some rulers and merchants and made war and kidnapping pay across wide regions. How deeply it damaged the societies it drew on is debated.

### 43. The price of sweetness
Work backward from sugar at a European table to a Caribbean mill and the Atlantic slave trade. Set sugar beside rum, tobacco, coffee, and tea: mild drugs were among the first goods traded around the whole world. Trace land seizure, credit, forced labor, and the timetable imposed by cut cane. Reconstruct how enslaved people maintained families, shared knowledge, and resisted within that timetable.

*Working answer:* The plantation joined land, credit, and enslaved labor in a rhythm close to a factory's, generations before the factory. It tied the daily habits of Europeans to violence they did not have to see.

### 44. When a company collected the taxes
Begin with the Dutch East India Company, the first to sell shares to the public, and the spice islands it seized by force. Explain why a company owned by shareholders could raise more money than any merchant. Then follow a revenue payment in Bengal, a province of the weakening Mughal empire, after the British East India Company acquired the right to collect it. Explain the musket and the drill that made infantry effective, and how the Company trained Indian soldiers in both. Trace how Indian bankers, soldiers, officials, and cultivators made company rule possible and contested it. End with the great rebellion of 1857, when many of those soldiers turned on the Company, and with the British crown taking direct control.

*Working answer:* Bengal's land revenue paid for the army that took more territory. Company rule rested on Indian credit and Indian soldiers throughout, which is why their revolt nearly ended it.

### 45. Who are "the people"?
Start with the Seven Years' War, fought by Britain and France on three continents, and explain how governments paid for it by borrowing from their own public. Follow a tax dispute in the American colonies and a fiscal crisis in France as each turned into an argument about consent. Explain what Enlightenment writers had proposed about rights and government. Ask who each founding document included, and set the American republic's promises beside its protection of slavery.

*Working answer:* Quarrels over money were argued in the language of rights. Once written down, that language could be claimed by people its authors had left out.

### 46. Who counted as free?
Follow news of the French Revolution to the sugar colony of Saint-Domingue. Place the demands of enslaved insurgents, free people of color, planters, and French officials against one another as alliances shifted. End with Haitian independence and the struggle for recognition.

*Working answer:* The insurgents forced abolition, and France then ratified it. Independence was won in war and then charged for, through a payment to France that burdened Haiti for generations.

### 47. What happens when the king is captured?
Begin in 1808, when Napoleon removed the king of Spain and every colony had to decide whom it now obeyed. Explain the colonial order: people ranked by birth and ancestry, and Spaniards born in the Americas shut out of the highest offices. Go back to the great Andean rebellion of the 1780s, which had shown those colonial elites what a rising from below could look like. Follow Bolívar in the north and San Martín in the south, and Mexico's different path from a priest's popular rising to an independence led by conservatives. Set Brazil beside them, where the Portuguese court fled to Rio and a prince declared independence as emperor, with slavery intact. Follow what came after: Bolívar's union breaking apart, rule by strongmen, British loans and trade, and a United States declaration that Europe should keep out. Ask what an American-born landowner, an indigenous villager, an enslaved man offered freedom for fighting, and a royalist each wanted.

*Working answer:* Independence began as a crisis of authority in Spain more than as a popular rising. It was led largely by colonial elites who wanted to rule without Madrid and who feared the people below them, so political independence changed little about who owned the land. Why Spanish America became many republics while Brazil held together is debated.

### 48. Why did the factory run on coal?
Begin with water entering a British mine and the cost of pumping it out. Explain why an engine that burned large quantities of coal could pay for itself at a mine, and what had to change for steam to make sense elsewhere. Follow steam into textiles, tracing links to cotton growers, enslaved laborers, mechanics, and mill workers. Compare what cheap power meant to an owner with what a powered loom meant to a weaver.

*Working answer:* Early engines were wasteful but ran where coal was almost free. Why industrialization began in Britain is one of the most argued questions in economic history, and the chapter lays out the main explanations.

### 49. Who should set the price?
Begin with a pin factory, the example Adam Smith used to show how dividing a task among workers multiplies what they make. Explain his argument that people pursuing their own gain in a competitive market can serve the general good, and what he said governments must still do. Set it against the older view that a country grows rich by selling more than it buys and guarding its trade with tariffs and warships, the view behind the companies of chapter 44. Follow Britain's long argument over taxing imported grain and its turn to free trade. Then follow the critics: workers who broke the machines that replaced them, early socialists who founded cooperative communities, and those who argued that a wage agreed between a mill owner and a hungry worker was not a free bargain. Ask what a mill owner, a weaver, a landowner, and an Indian cloth maker facing British imports each made of the new doctrine.

*Working answer:* The idea that markets should set prices, wages, and trade became the governing idea of the first industrial power, and the main objections to it arose at the same moment. The argument between them has shaped politics ever since, including the revolution of chapter 59, the Depression of chapter 62, and the turn back to markets in chapter 78.

### 50. Why die for a nation?
Open with France's mass conscription of 1793 and the claim that every citizen owed the country military service. Follow Napoleon's armies across Europe and the national feeling they provoked in the countries they occupied. Trace how schools, newspapers, railways, and armies taught people who had never met to feel like kin. Follow the idea into Germany and Italy, then to peoples inside the Habsburg and Ottoman empires. Treat Zionism as one national movement among these.

*Working answer:* Nations were made by institutions as much as inherited. How much they drew on older identities is disputed among scholars of nationalism.

### 51. Whose land was the West?
Begin with the Cherokee, who had a written constitution and a newspaper, won their case in the United States Supreme Court, and were removed from their land anyway. Trace how the republic grew: a vast purchase from Napoleon, who sold after the Haitian Revolution of chapter 46 had wrecked his plans for the Americas; a war that took the northern half of Mexico; and treaties made and broken with Native nations. Show Native powers such as the Comanche and the Lakota as states with territories and strategies of their own. Explain what the railroad and the destruction of the bison herds did to the plains. Ask what settlers, Native nations, Mexicans who found themselves inside a new country, and enslaved people marched west each faced. End with the question every new territory raised: would slavery be allowed there?

*Working answer:* The United States was an empire across a continent before it was a power overseas, and it grew by purchase, war, treaty, and removal. Many settlers believed they were filling empty land with a mission from God, and the nations already living there experienced an invasion. The fight over slavery in the new lands led directly to the civil war.

### 52. What is worth a civil war?
Pair two wars of the same years. In the United States, follow the dispute over slavery's expansion to secession and four years of fighting. In China, follow a failed examination candidate who came to believe he was the younger brother of Jesus and raised a movement that held much of the country for more than a decade. Explain the rifle, the railway, and the telegraph, and what they did to the scale of killing. Ask what an enslaved person fleeing to Union lines, a Confederate conscript, a Taiping convert, and a provincial official defending the dynasty each believed was at stake.

*Working answer:* Both wars were fought over what kind of society would exist afterward, one over slavery and one over a religious vision of a remade China. The Taiping war was by most estimates the deadliest conflict of its century, and it is barely known outside China.

### 53. Who could refuse an industrial empire?
Pair Chinese officials of the Qing dynasty confronting British demands in the Opium Wars with Japanese leaders debating foreign warships and unequal treaties. Examine the fiscal and political choices behind industrial and military reform, including what the Taiping war had cost the Qing. Follow Japan's emergence as an imperial power into Korea through Korean accounts of lost sovereignty.

*Working answer:* The outcome turned on a government's capacity to tax and reorganize more than on awareness of the threat. Japan's reformers then used that capacity to take an empire of their own.

### 54. When did science become an industry?
Follow nitrogen from the air into a German factory. Explain the Haber–Bosch process and why the same chemistry produced fertilizer and explosives. Widen to electricity, synthetic dyes, the telegraph cable, vaccination, and the germ theory of disease. Include cholera in the industrial city, and the sewers and clean water that ended it. Ask who paid for laboratories and what they expected in return, and how Germany and the United States overtook Britain.

*Working answer:* Companies and governments began to fund research as a routine investment. From here on, scientific discovery fed directly into food supply, public health, and military power.

### 55. Who decided that some people were lesser?
Begin with a naturalist sorting humans into varieties at a time when European wealth depended on enslaved Africans. Trace how older prejudices about religion and ancestry were recast as claims about biology, with skulls measured and peoples ranked. Explain what Darwin actually proposed about natural selection, and how others stretched it into the claim that nations and races compete and the weak deserve to lose. Follow these ideas into the defense of slavery, the claim that conquest was a duty to civilize, bans on immigration, and forced sterilization. Include those who argued back at the time, among them Frederick Douglass and the Haitian scholar Antenor Firmin. Explain what genetics has since shown: human variation is continuous, and most of it lies within groups, not between them.

*Working answer:* Racial ranking was largely built after the fact to justify slavery and empire, and it borrowed the authority of science to do it. It was contested from the start, including by the people it ranked lowest. It leads directly to the conquests of the next chapter and the genocide of chapter 66.

### 56. How was a continent taken in thirty years?
Begin with the conference in Berlin where European governments set rules for claiming African territory, with no African ruler present. Explain the three technologies that changed the odds: quinine against malaria, the river steamboat, and the machine gun. Follow a treaty from the ruler who signed it to the meaning Europeans later gave it. Reconstruct the forced rubber collection of the Congo under the Belgian king. Then follow Ethiopia, a Christian kingdom since the fourth century, in its defeat of an Italian army, to show what a state with firearms, diplomacy, and unity could still do.

*Working answer:* In 1880 Europeans controlled a small fraction of Africa, and by 1914 nearly all of it. Disease had kept them out and firepower let them in. African states made choices throughout, including alliance, resistance, and negotiation, with far worse options than their conquerors had.

### 57. Who was left out of "the people"?
Begin with two women who answered the revolutionary declarations of chapter 45: one in France who wrote a declaration of the rights of woman and was sent to the guillotine, and one in England who argued that women seemed lesser only because they were denied an education. Explain the law most women lived under, in which a wife's property and earnings belonged to her husband. Follow the movement's roots in the campaign against slavery, the first conventions, and the split over whether Black men should receive the vote before women. Follow the first national vote for women in New Zealand, the militant campaign in Britain, and the part played by women's factory work. Widen to reformers in Egypt, India, China, and Japan. Ask what a mill worker, a wealthy campaigner, and the women who opposed the vote each wanted.

*Working answer:* The revolutions that declared all men equal left out half the population, and women used the same words to claim their place. The vote came first at the edges of the English-speaking world and spread after the war of the next chapter, when governments needed women's labor. Equality in law and equality in practice arrived at very different speeds.

## Part VI — War
*Scale: whole populations organized for war.*

### 58. Why did a regional crisis become a world war?
Reconstruct the crisis of July 1914 as a series of decisions whose outcomes were still open. Follow what ministers knew about mobilization and guessed about allies. Ask why millions of men reported when called. Explain what the machine gun and heavy artillery did to an attack across open ground, and why the war in the west stopped moving. Then trace how empires carried a European war into African communities through recruitment, requisitions, and forced carrying of supplies.

*Working answer:* Leaders accepted risks expecting someone else to back down or the war to be short. How to divide responsibility among them remains disputed.

### 59. Who inherited the ruins?
Enter the Russian Revolution through demands for bread, land, and an end to war, and explain what Marxism promised those who made it. Then trace a claim for Arab independence from a petition in former Ottoman territory to the authorities empowered to hear it. Compare the institutions that decided whose claims were heard as empires dissolved and the victors divided their territories. Include the influenza of 1918, which killed more people than the war and left far less trace in memory.

*Working answer:* The war destroyed four empires. Who received a state afterward depended on who had a seat where the decisions were made.

### 60. What replaces a dynasty?
Begin in 1900 with foreign armies in Beijing after an uprising against foreigners failed. Follow the Qing court's last reforms, including the end of the examinations of chapter 26, which cut the tie between educated families and the throne. Follow the revolution of 1911, the republic that replaced the dynasty, and its slide into rule by regional commanders. Follow the students who protested in 1919 when the peace conference handed German holdings in China to Japan, and their argument over science, democracy, Confucius, and Marx. Trace the Nationalist party's alliance with the Communists, its massacre of them in 1927, the Communists' retreat to the countryside, and Mao's turn to the peasants. End with Japan's seizure of Manchuria and its full invasion in 1937. Tell it from the positions of a student, a commander's conscript, a villager, and a merchant in a foreign-run port.

*Working answer:* The dynasty fell when it lost the provincial elites and the army after decades of defeats. No successor could command both. The ideas that competed to fill the gap, nationalism, democracy, and communism, came from the same foreign powers China was trying to resist.

### 61. Can you win without killing?
Begin with Gandhi's march to the sea to make salt in defiance of a British tax. Explain his method: break an unjust law openly, accept the punishment, and make the ruler's violence visible to everyone watching. Trace where it came from, in Hindu and Jain teaching on doing no harm, in writers he read abroad, and in his years organizing Indians in South Africa. Go back to the massacre at Amritsar in 1919 and follow the Indian National Congress as it became a mass movement. Include those who disagreed: revolutionaries who chose the bomb and the pistol, the leader who raised an army with Japanese help, Ambedkar, born among the people ranked below all four orders of chapter 12, who argued that independence would change little without an end to caste, and Jinnah, who feared what Hindu majority rule would mean for Muslims. Ask when the method worked and when it failed.

*Working answer:* These movements show people prepared to die for a belief and not to kill for it, which tests the preface's idea from an unexpected side. The method worked against a government that depended on Indian cooperation and cared how it looked to the world. How much independence owed to it, and how much to Britain's exhaustion after the Second World War, is debated.

### 62. Why did the money stop?
Begin with a crowd outside a closed bank in 1931. Explain the gold standard, under which a currency was a promise to pay gold, and the loop of debts left by the war: American loans to Germany, German payments to Britain and France, and their repayments to America. Follow the crash of 1929, the bank failures, the tariffs, and the collapse of trade, until roughly one worker in four was unemployed in the United States and Germany. Explain the argument of John Maynard Keynes that an economy can settle into mass unemployment and that a government must then spend. Follow the American New Deal, and show why Soviet planning and fascist promises of work looked attractive at that moment. Follow the idea forward to the welfare states built after 1945. Ask what a farmer, a banker, an unemployed worker, and a finance minister each thought was happening.

*Working answer:* Governments made the Depression far worse by defending gold and balanced budgets, and countries recovered roughly in the order they gave up gold. The slump discredited the self-regulating market of chapter 49 for two generations and handed arguments to communists and fascists. Economists still argue about its causes.

### 63. What did building socialism cost?
Begin in a Ukrainian village in 1930 as officials arrive to merge its farms into a collective. Explain the problem the Bolsheviks faced: a revolution made in the name of industrial workers in a country of peasants. Follow the argument after Lenin's death and Stalin's answer, which was to take grain from the countryside to pay for factories. Follow the collectives, the deportation of better-off peasants, and the famine of 1932 and 1933 in Ukraine, Kazakhstan, and elsewhere. Follow the five-year plans and the new steel cities, then the terror of 1937 and 1938 and the labor camps. Ask what a party activist, a farmer, an engineer, a camp prisoner, and an admiring foreign visitor each believed. Note what the archives opened after 1991 have settled and what they have not.

*Working answer:* The Soviet Union industrialized within a decade at the cost of millions of lives, and that industry supplied the armies of chapter 65. Many who carried the policies out believed they were building a just society, which makes this a hard test of what people will do for an idea. Whether the famine in Ukraine was a genocide, and whether industrialization required any of it, are debated.

### 64. How does a democracy surrender power?
Reconstruct the choices that brought Hitler into office and the measures that dismantled opposition. Explain how radio and film reached millions of people at once, and what the regime did once it controlled both. Include the forged *Protocols of the Elders of Zion*, exposed as a fake in 1921 and believed anyway. Examine the mass unemployment of chapter 62, organized violence, antisemitism, and elite bargaining through the positions of voters, conservative politicians, trade unionists, and people the regime targeted.

*Working answer:* The Nazi party never won a majority in a free national election. Hitler was appointed by conservatives who believed they could control him, and the dictatorship was built within months through emergency powers and violence.

### 65. What made a war global?
Begin in China under Japanese invasion. Follow the German invasion of the Soviet Union, where most of the German soldiers killed in the war died. Then connect the Asian and European wars through oil, shipping, industrial production, and alliance decisions. Explain the internal combustion engine and what it made possible in tanks, trucks, and aircraft, including the bombing of cities. Follow penicillin from a laboratory to mass production. Use one supply route to anchor the scale of the fighting. Follow an occupied community to show how access to food became an instrument of power.

*Working answer:* Engines made armies depend on oil, oil and shipping tied separate wars into one, and factory output decided it. This is also where American industrial power becomes the central fact of world politics.

### 66. Who made mass murder possible?
Trace a deportation from an order, through property seizures and railway schedules, to its human consequences. Pair administrative records with Jewish testimony on hiding, mutual aid, and resistance. Examine the decisions that made the Holocaust possible and the gaps left by murdered witnesses.

*Working answer:* Genocide on this scale needed the routine work of many ordinary institutions across a continent. The idea alone was not enough, and neither was the machinery.

### 67. When did physicists become a strategic resource?
Begin with the discovery of nuclear fission in 1938 and the letter that warned an American president. Explain how a chain reaction works and why building a bomb took an industrial effort. Follow the scientists who petitioned over its use, the officials who decided, and survivors' accounts from Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

*Working answer:* A state turned a laboratory finding into a weapon in under seven years. The scientists who made it possible had little say over how it was used.

## Part VII — Dominion
*Scale: one economic and strategic system.*

### 68. What does independence mean in the same town?
Pair two partitions carried out as Britain withdrew. Begin in a district of Punjab in 1947 and a neighborhood of British-ruled Palestine before the United Nations vote on partition. Trace the rival national aspirations, the Holocaust's aftermath, the proposals, and the wars and killings that followed. Set India's and Pakistan's independence beside mass displacement, and Israel's independence beside the Palestinian Nakba, on the same local maps. Note that Gandhi, of chapter 61, was shot dead by a Hindu nationalist months after independence. Follow what statehood, expulsion, flight, and the prevention of return meant for particular households.

*Working answer:* Where a departing empire left rival national claims to the same ground, new states were born alongside mass displacement. The same year is remembered as a founding and as a catastrophe.

### 69. Who wrote the rules after 1945?
Begin at the conference that set up the postwar monetary system. Explain how the dollar, American loans, and American bases shaped the order that followed. Include the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the convention against genocide, and ask who could enforce them. Then follow newly independent states into that order through Ghana's independence, Algeria's war against France, and the Bandung conference of Asian and African governments. Explain the Pan-African and Pan-Arab movements, which argued that peoples divided by colonial borders belonged together. Ask which rules they could change and which they had to accept.

*Working answer:* The United States designed the institutions at the moment of its greatest relative strength. New states used those institutions to press claims their designers had not anticipated.

### 70. Why did the Cold War turn hot in Asia?
Begin with a village in northern China where the Communist party redistributed land. Follow the civil war to the Communist victory of 1949. Then trace how Korea, divided by the victors of 1945, became a war that drew in the United States and China, and how Vietnam's fight against French rule became a twenty-year American war. Tell each from several positions: a Chinese peasant, a Korean family split by the dividing line, a Vietnamese nationalist deciding which side to join, an American draftee, and the cabinets that sent them. Note the part television played in how Americans saw Vietnam.

*Working answer:* In Asia the contest between the superpowers was laid over older struggles about land, foreign rule, and who would govern a newly independent country. People who fought mostly did so for those local causes, while Washington and Moscow read the same wars as moves against each other.

### 71. What did the revolution do next?
Begin in 1958 with a village melting its tools in a backyard furnace to make steel. Follow the first years of Communist rule: land taken from landlords, many of whom were killed, and then farms merged into communes. Follow the Great Leap Forward and the famine of 1959 to 1961, and explain why officials reported harvests that did not exist. Then follow the Cultural Revolution, in which Mao called on students to attack his own party, teachers were denounced by their pupils, and millions of young people were sent to the countryside. Include the break with the Soviet Union, China's own bomb, and the American president's visit. Set the gains in literacy and life expectancy beside the deaths. Tell it from the positions of a village official, a farmer, a student Red Guard, and a teacher.

*Working answer:* A government that could mobilize a whole population, and that no one dared to give bad news, produced the deadliest famine on record through its own policies. The reformers of chapter 77 were people who had lived through it. Estimates of the deaths vary widely, and most Chinese archives remain closed.

### 72. Does the method travel?
Follow the method of chapter 61 to the American South, with its bus boycott, its sit-ins, its churches as organizers, and its television pictures of police dogs set on children. Follow it to South Africa, where the African National Congress protested peacefully for decades, turned to sabotage after police shot demonstrators, and won through resistance at home, sanctions from abroad, and negotiation. Include those who rejected the method, among them Malcolm X and Nelson Mandela in his own case for armed struggle. Ask when it worked and when it failed.

*Working answer:* Non-violence worked best against governments that depended on the cooperation of the people resisting them and that cared how they looked to others. Whether it could have succeeded without the threat of more violent alternatives is debated.

### 73. Who had room to maneuver in the Cold War?
Explain the rocket that could carry a warhead across an ocean and the satellite that could photograph a missile site. Reconstruct the Cuban Missile Crisis with Cuban security fears inside the account alongside Soviet and American calculations. Widen through India's policy of siding with neither superpower. Then turn to Latin America, where the United States organized the overthrow of an elected government in Guatemala in 1954 and worked against another in Chile before the army's coup of 1973. Ask how nuclear deterrence looked from a smaller country's cabinet and what choices remained under superpower pressure.

*Working answer:* Smaller states had more leverage than a two-sided picture suggests, and they used it at great risk. Cuba was left out of the settlement of its own crisis.

### 74. Who controls the oil?
Begin with the car and the mass ownership that made whole economies run on gasoline. Follow a barrel of oil from a Gulf concession held by a Western company to the embargo of 1973. Trace how producing states took control of their own reserves. Compare Washington's decisions during the Suez crisis of 1956 and the October war of 1973, and read each from Washington, Israel, Palestinian communities, and neighboring Arab capitals. Treat the American–Israeli relationship as one of several American commitments in the region, alongside those to Saudi Arabia and Iran.

*Working answer:* American involvement in the Middle East formed around oil, Cold War rivalry, and domestic politics together. No single one of them explains it.

### 75. What happens to land taken in six days?
Step back to six days in June 1967, when Israel took the West Bank, Gaza, East Jerusalem, Sinai, and the Golan. Follow what military occupation meant for the people living under it and for Israeli politics. Explain the Palestinian national movement that formed in exile and under occupation. Explain religious Zionism and the settler movement, and evangelical Christian support for Israel in the United States. Follow the peace between Egypt and Israel, in which land was returned for recognition, then the war in Lebanon, the first Palestinian uprising, the Oslo agreements, the murder of the Israeli prime minister who signed them, the second uprising, and the rise of Hamas in Gaza. Tell each from the positions of Palestinian and Israeli civilians as well as those who decided.

*Working answer:* The 1967 war turned a conflict between states into a conflict over land and the people living on it. Peace was reached where land could be handed back to a state, and not where two national movements claimed the same ground. Each side explains the failure of the negotiations differently, and the chapter sets out both accounts.

### 76. Can a revolution be made in God's name?
Begin in Iran, where a shah restored to power in 1953 with American and British help ruled with oil money and a secret police. Follow the coalition that overthrew him in 1979 and how the clerics around Khomeini came out on top. Explain the movements that argued Islam should be the basis of government, from the Muslim Brotherhood in Egypt to the teaching promoted by Saudi Arabia to Khomeini's doctrine of rule by clerics, and how they differed. Follow the eight-year war between Iran and Iraq. Then go to Afghanistan, invaded by the Soviet Union in the same year, and to the foreign fighters the war drew, armed with American, Saudi, and Pakistani help. Tell it from the positions of Iranian revolutionaries of different kinds, Afghan villagers, and a Soviet conscript.

*Working answer:* After the defeat of 1967 discredited secular Arab nationalism, religion became a governing program across the region. The movements grouped together as "political Islam" disagreed with one another and often fought. The Afghan war helped exhaust the Soviet Union and trained the men behind the attacks of chapter 80.

### 77. Why did the Soviet Union fall and China rise?
Set Soviet reformers of the 1980s beside Chinese reformers after 1978. Include the Soviet hold on Eastern Europe and how it was let go in 1989. Explain what each tried to change and what each refused to give up. Follow a shipping container from a Chinese factory to an American store to show how manufacturing became a chain across many countries.

*Working answer:* One government lost political control while trying to reform its economy. The other opened its economy to export manufacturing and kept political control. Why the outcomes differed is still argued.

### 78. The market's return
Begin in the 1970s with prices and unemployment rising together, which the followers of Keynes had said should not happen, and with the oil shock of chapter 74. Explain the end of the fixed exchange rates set up in chapter 69. Explain the case made by Hayek and Friedman, and follow it into government under Thatcher and Reagan: state firms sold, union power broken, top taxes cut. Follow the debt crises of Latin America and Africa and the conditions lenders attached, and the rapid sale of state property in Russia. Trace the freeing of trade and capital and China's entry into the world trading system. Then explain the financial crisis of 2008: how home loans were packaged into securities rated as safe, why the banks were rescued, and what politics followed. Ask what a laid-off steelworker, a migrant worker in a Chinese factory, the finance minister of an indebted country, and a banker each gained and lost.

*Working answer:* The argument of chapter 49 swung back toward markets after the 1970s and partly away again after 2008. Over those decades inequality between countries fell, as hundreds of millions of people in Asia left poverty, while inequality inside many countries rose. Which policies caused which result is disputed.

### 79. Why did war return after the Cold War?
Begin in 1994 with a radio station in Rwanda naming people to be killed. Follow the genocide there, the wars it set off in neighboring Congo, which killed far more people, and the wars that broke up Yugoslavia, and ask why international forces that were present did not stop them. Then trace Russia's seizure of Crimea in 2014 and its full invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Explain what cheap drones and satellite images changed about a battlefield. Tell each from the position of neighbors who became killers or victims, peacekeepers under orders not to act, and soldiers and civilians on both sides of the Ukrainian front.

*Working answer:* The end of the superpower contest removed one restraint and did not remove nationalism, contested borders, or leaders willing to use them. The promise made after the Holocaust that genocide would be prevented was not kept. The causes of the war in Ukraine are disputed, and the chapter sets out the competing accounts.

### 80. What did the war on terror change?
Begin with the attacks of September 11, 2001. Follow the American invasion of Afghanistan and then of Iraq, the second justified by claims about weapons that proved false. Trace the Iraqi insurgency, the war between Sunni and Shia militias, and the rise of the Islamic State. Explain what the campaign added at home and abroad: drone strikes, detention without trial, and mass surveillance. Follow the Arab uprisings of 2011 and the war in Syria, and the return of the Taliban to Kabul in 2021. Then return to the ground of chapter 75 with the Hamas attack of October 2023 and the war in Gaza that followed. Tell each from the positions of those who decided and those who lived with it, including Iraqi families, Afghan villagers, American soldiers, and Palestinian and Israeli civilians.

*Working answer:* Each war left behind an unresolved grievance, an armed movement, or a foreign commitment that shaped the next. The United States spent two decades and did not build the states it set out to build. Religious conviction, national claims, and the interests of outside powers are all present, and the parties themselves disagree about which of these the conflicts are about.

### 81. Why ban a pleasure?
Begin in 1920 with barrels of beer being emptied into a New York gutter. Go back over what the book has already shown: beer in the rations of chapter 4, rum and tobacco in chapter 43, opium in chapter 53. Explain in plain terms what alcohol, opiates, and stimulants do in the body and why some people cannot stop. Follow American Prohibition from the churches and women's groups that campaigned for it to the smugglers who profited and the repeal of 1933. Trace the international ban on opium and cocaine from its first treaties to the "war on drugs" declared in 1971, and follow that war to a coca farm in the Andes, the cartels of Colombia and Mexico, and the prisons of the United States. Then follow the opioid epidemic, which began with a legal painkiller sold by a company. Compare places that chose otherwise, such as Portugal, which stopped prosecuting drug users in 2001. Tell it from the positions of a temperance campaigner, a farmer, a police officer, a doctor, and a person who is addicted.

*Working answer:* People have used intoxicants in every society in this book, and states have taxed them, traded them, and banned them, sometimes all at once. Which substances were legal has depended as much on who used them and who profited as on the harm they do. Bans have reduced use and created violent markets, and no society has found an arrangement without heavy costs.

### 82. Who is on the other end of the wire?
Trace a message across the internet back through its layers: the network, the microchip, the transistor, the wartime computer. Explain what each made possible. Follow the supply chain of an advanced chip to show how few places can make one. Ask who governs a network no state built alone.

*Working answer:* Public research laid the foundations and private companies built what most people use. The system joined billions of people and concentrated key steps in a handful of firms and places.

### 83. Why would a stranger accept a number on a screen?
Begin at a market stall in Nairobi where a customer pays by text message. Return to the coin of chapter 11 and ask the same questions: what is being promised, and who stands behind it? Explain what happens in the second after a card is tapped: where the message goes, who checks it, and who takes a share. Trace the path from the first credit cards of the 1950s to online shops in the 1990s, mobile money in Kenya, phone payments in China, India's public payment system, and Bitcoin, an attempt at money with no issuer at all. Follow one online order backward through the warehouse, the container ship of chapter 77, and the delivery driver. Ask who can now see every purchase, who can freeze an account, who is shut out when cash disappears, and what happened to the shopkeeper.

*Working answer:* Most money was already a record in a bank's books. Digital payment turned nearly every purchase into a record as well, held by a company or a state, which returns to the question of chapter 4: who can read the record? Trust moved from the issuer of the coin to the operators of the network. The convenience is real, and it was paid for in privacy and in the concentration of trade in a few firms.

### 84. Who decides what a billion people see?
Begin in Myanmar in 2017, where hatred of the Rohingya minority spread through a social network that for many people was the whole internet, and where a campaign of killing and expulsion followed. Establish that false news is old: the forged pamphlets of chapter 35, the forgeries and broadcasts of chapter 64, the radio of chapter 79. Then explain what changed. Anyone can publish to everyone. A ranking system chooses what each person sees, and it is tuned to hold attention because attention is sold to advertisers. Explain how such a feed works. Follow the uprisings of 2011 organized online, foreign interference in elections, the rumors of the pandemic in chapter 86, and images and voices that machines can now fake. Set this beside China's choice of an internet the state filters. Tell it from the positions of a moderator paid to look at the worst of it, an engineer, an activist, a censor, and a teenager.

*Working answer:* Lies, rumors, and propaganda did not begin with social media. What is new is the speed, the reach, the tailoring to each person, and the fact that the ranking rules of a few companies decide what spreads. How much this has changed what people believe and how they vote is disputed, and the chapter sets out the evidence on each side. It is the eighth question in its present form: who is allowed to say what is true?

## Part VIII — Decline
*Scale: the species and the planet. The book's questions are now asked of humanity as a whole.*

### 85. What does the empire run on?
Apply the questions about resources and technology to eight billion people. Explain how vaccines, antibiotics, and clean water cut the death rate, how reliable contraception later cut the birth rate, and why the population multiplied in between. Trace the fossil energy, synthetic nitrogen, and fresh water behind an ordinary meal. Explain how burning carbon warms the planet and how that is known. Follow the environmental movement from a book about pesticides to worldwide climate protests. Ask who has emitted the most, who bears the earliest costs, and what climate negotiations have and have not delivered.

*Working answer:* The present population exists because of medicine and depends on energy and fertilizer flows that did not exist two centuries ago. The costs of those flows fall later and on different people than their benefits.

### 86. What does a crisis reveal?
Begin in January 2020, when the genetic code of a new virus was posted online and a vaccine was designed within days, while the virus itself was already travelling by air. Look back at the crises the book has told: the plague of chapter 32, smallpox in chapter 36, cholera in chapter 54, the influenza of 1918 in chapter 59, the famines of chapters 63 and 71, the crashes of chapters 62 and 78. Explain how a virus spreads, and what a quarantine, a test, and a vaccine each do. Follow COVID-19 through closed borders and locked-down cities, hospitals without beds, factories without parts, governments paying people to stay home, work and shopping moving online, rumor outrunning the evidence, and a vaccine made in under a year and shared unequally. Compare how different governments responded and what it cost them. Say what is and is not known about where the virus came from. Tell it from the positions of a nurse, a delivery driver, a child out of school, a health minister, and a family that lost someone.

*Working answer:* A crisis shows what a society depends on and whom it protects. The connections that carried the virus around the world in weeks also produced a vaccine in a year. Who died depended on age, wealth, and government as well as on the virus. Earlier crises left institutions behind them, such as quarantine, public health boards, central banks, and welfare states, and it is too early to say what this one has left.

### 87. Can a civilization hold weapons that could end it?
Reconstruct a night when a nuclear false alarm was not passed up the chain of command. Survey the nuclear arsenals that remain. Explain how genes are read and edited, what that has made possible in medicine and farming, and why the same tools raise the risk of an engineered disease. Then examine cooperation that worked, including arms treaties, the eradication of smallpox, and the repair of the ozone layer. Ask what those successes had in common.

*Working answer:* Survival so far has owed something to institutions and something to luck. The record of cooperation is better than its reputation and has clear limits.

### 88. Who gets to decide what a machine can do?
Follow one AI system backward from a decision affecting a person to its training data, chips, electricity, workers, owners, and public authorities. Place AI in the line of earlier technologies in this book that changed who did what work: writing, printing, the steam engine. Set out what it makes newly possible, including this book, alongside the fears it raises about human work, specialization, and control. Set out the present arguments about where technology leads: that AI could end human control, that it should be built as fast as possible, and that humans should merge with their machines. Compare futures shaped by concentrated ownership, public oversight, or international coordination. Return to the ration record at Uruk. In both settings, who can inspect the record, challenge a decision, and obtain a remedy?

*Working answer:* The story of AI is the story of humans. It depends on concentrated chips, energy, data, and human labor, like every powerful technology before it, and the oldest question about records still applies: who can read them and who can contest them.

### 89. How do empires end, and is this one different?
Return to the endings already told: the Bronze Age palaces, Persia, Qin, the Roman West, the Maya cities, the Mexica and the Inca, the Zunghars, the Mughals, Spanish rule in the Americas, the Qing and Ottoman empires, the European colonial empires, the Soviet Union. Sort what failed in each case, whether revenue, legitimacy, ecology, or outside shock, and what survived. Weigh the running dispute between material causes and ideas on the evidence of the whole book, and give a verdict on the preface's proposal that people fight for what they believe, including what the non-violent movements of chapters 61 and 72 show. Then ask what is different about a system with no outside, and what a downfall of the human empire could look like. Mark clearly where evidence ends and speculation begins.

*Working answer:* Earlier empires ended as governments while their peoples, languages, and techniques carried on, often under a successor. A planetary system has no neighbor to absorb its failure, and no previous empire could see its own position as clearly as this one can.

## How each chapter is built

A chapter is as long as its question needs. Most will run between 5,000 and 7,000 words, and none is padded or trimmed to hit a number. The elements below are a checklist for planning a chapter. They are never section headings, and the finished chapter is a story that draws on them in the order its subject needs and leaves out any that do not apply. A chapter about an idea can follow its changing meaning across places. A technology chapter can pause the chronology to explain how a device worked, what it cost, and where it was useful. Chapters do not all open the same way: some begin with an object, some with an argument between two people, some with a machine, and some with a decision whose outcome was still open.

1. **The world before.** Anchor the opening question in a documented practice, object, place, or decision. Establish what people could do and what stood in their way.
2. **What people knew.** State what could be explained at the time and what could not. Credit no one with knowledge that came later.
3. **Every party's perspective.** Identify who could decide, who could refuse, and who bore the consequences. Look for disagreement within each group.
4. **The catalyst.** Reconstruct why action became possible or urgent at that moment. Distinguish a long-running pressure from a specific decision.
5. **Technology and resources.** Trace one material requirement to its source. Explain the mechanism. Show whose work, knowledge, or permission turned it into usable power.
6. **Why they fought.** Where there is a war, say who served by conviction, for pay, under orders, or under threat, and what the evidence is.
7. **Consequences.** Follow one immediate outcome and one later consequence. Return to the opening question. Show what changed in practice and where the older arrangement persisted.
8. **A plausible alternative.** Choose an option someone actually considered. Explain what it would have required and where prediction becomes speculation.
9. **The handoff.** End on what the chapter leaves unresolved for the next, and on how the scale of dependence has changed.

## Help for the reader

**In every chapter.** A line under the title gives the place and the years covered. Each historical term is explained in the sentence where it first appears. The references consulted are kept in a lookups file beside the chapter and are not printed in it.

**In every part.** Each part opens with a map of the world it covers and a one-page timeline that sets its chapters side by side, so a reader can see what was happening in China while Rome was expanding.

**At the back.** A glossary of terms, a list of the people named in the book with one line on each, and the four indexes of technologies, conflicts, empires, and ideas.

## Conventions

- **Dates.** BCE and CE. An uncertain date is given as "about," and a disputed one says so.
- **Names.** People and places take the form most familiar in English. Where a people's own name differs from the common one, both are given at first mention, as with the Mexica, commonly called Aztecs. Chinese names are spelled in pinyin.
- **Units.** Metric. An old unit is explained when it matters to the story.
- **Money.** Old sums are not converted into modern currency. They are expressed as what they bought at the time: days of wages, measures of grain, or a share of a government's revenue.
- **Counts.** Populations and death tolls are given as ranges where the estimates differ, with a note on who did the counting.

## Research standards

- For each opening example, say where the evidence comes from, when it dates from, and what its limits are. Dialogue is used only where a source records it, and a later recollection is distinguished from an observation made at the time.
- Test a ruler's account against material evidence and records produced by others. If only the ruler's words survive, say whose experience remains inaccessible.
- For contested causes, identify the rival explanations and the evidence each one explains.
- Distinguish voluntary service, economic pressure, conscription, and direct coercion. A soldier's presence tells us little on its own about willingness to die.
- Date maps and estimates. Explain what a border represented and who counted the people on either side.
- Explain science and technology at the level of mechanism.
- For conflicts still under way at the end of 2025, state what is established, what each party claims, and what cannot yet be known.

## Maps, diagrams, and images

Images are common throughout the book. Nearly every chapter has a map, a technology gets a diagram wherever its mechanism can be drawn, and every chapter shows at least one piece of the evidence it rests on. The whole book carries roughly 300 to 350 images.

**Three kinds of image, three rules.**

| Kind | Examples | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| The evidence itself | The Uruk ration tablet, the Lachish reliefs, a Luther pamphlet, a map made at the time | Always sourced from the museum, library, or archive that holds it. Never recreated. |
| Reference maps | The extent of an empire, a trade route, a campaign, a partition line | Drawn for the book from cited data, in one house style. |
| Mechanism diagrams | The Newcomen engine, movable type, the Haber–Bosch process, a chain reaction | Sourced where a good original exists. Otherwise redrawn for the book and checked against a reliable reference. |

**Maps.** Each map is generated by code from open geographic data, with boundaries taken from a named source. Every map carries a date. Where a border was a zone and not a line, the map shows it that way, distinguishing land an empire governed, land it influenced, and land it only claimed. Every part opens with a map of the world it covers. Chapters on empires, conquests, colonial rule, trade routes, and wars each carry at least one map of their own. Four are planned in detail: the wind systems of the Atlantic (34), the causeways and lake of Tenochtitlan (36), and, both in chapter 68, the 1947 partition line through Punjab and the proposed partition of Palestine beside the 1949 armistice lines.

**Diagrams.** The first choice is an original: a period engraving, a patent drawing, or a figure published by a government scientific agency. Where none is clear enough or free to use, the diagram is redrawn as a clean line drawing, labeled in plain English, and checked against a reliable reference before it goes in.

**No generated pictures.** AI image generators are not used for maps, diagrams, or illustrations, because they produce geography and labels that look right and are wrong. Maps and redrawn diagrams are built from data and sources that can be checked.

**Legibility.** Every map and diagram must be readable at the width of a phone and in greyscale. No distinction rests on color alone, and no label is too small to read on a small screen.

**Captions.** Every caption says what the image is, who made it and when, and what it cannot show.

**Rights.** The book is free to read and the author keeps the copyright. An image is used only if its terms allow free redistribution: it is out of copyright, it is released under an open license, or the holder has given written permission. Each image carries a credit line. If no usable version can be found, the image is redrawn or left out.

**The image ledger.** Each chapter keeps an image ledger. It records the source, the creator, the date, the license, a link, and any changes made. No image goes into the book without an entry.

## How the book is made

**AI drafts, and checks what it is not sure of.** AI makes the breadth of this book possible for one author. The level of checking is set by what is feasible for a book of this size:

- What the AI knows with confidence is written directly.
- A reference is consulted for anything it is not confident of, and always for exact figures, exact dates, anything that is contested, and events of 2024 and 2025.
- Quotations are never written from memory. Each is taken from a source.
- Any reference is acceptable if its contents are plausible. There is no fixed list of approved sources.
- A claim that cannot be checked and is not held with confidence is cut, or stated as uncertain.
- Each chapter's lookups file names the references that were actually consulted.

**The loop for each chapter.** Each chapter is written in its own session, starting from this plan, the style sheet, and one-paragraph summaries of the chapters already finished.

1. **Outline.** Set out the chapter's argument and section order from what is already known, and mark every uncertain point: exact figures, dates, quotations, and contested claims.
2. **Targeted lookups.** Research only the marked points, plus the main rival explanations where a cause is disputed.
3. **Draft.** Write the prose.
4. **Independent check.** A different model, which has not seen the drafting, reads the chapter cold and lists every claim it thinks is wrong, doubtful, or overstated, and every passage that is unclear.
5. **Resolve.** Each flag is checked against a reference before the text is changed. A flag is a reason to look something up, not a verdict.
6. **Images.** Maps, diagrams, and sourced images are added in a separate pass once the text is stable.

**Who does what.**

| Task | Tool | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Outline and prose | Claude Fable 5.2 | The prose is the product, and one model keeps one voice across the book. |
| Lookups and image sourcing | Claude Sonnet 5.5 | The work is mostly reading, so volume matters more than depth. |
| Independent check | Codex CLI | A model from a different maker makes different mistakes than the one that wrote the chapter. |
| Disputed points | Claude and Codex separately | Each sets out the rival explanations on its own. Where they disagree, the point gets a reference lookup. |
| Resolving flags | Claude Opus 5.5 | It looks up each flagged claim and fixes or keeps it. |
| Map and diagram code | Claude Opus 5.5, reviewed by Codex | Ordinary coding work with a second reader. |
| Glossary, timelines, list of people, ledger formatting | Claude Haiku 5.5 | Mechanical extraction. |

Codex checks and never writes. It does not edit chapter text, and it does not touch the preface or anything else in the author's voice.

**Working answers are tested.** The finished chapter says whether its working answer held, was revised, or was overturned.

**Drafting order.** Chapter 48, on coal, is drafted first. It has a mechanism to explain, a contested cause, and several parties with opposed interests, so it tests the whole method. It is drafted twice from the same outline, once by Fable 5.2 and once by Opus 5.5, and the author reads both without knowing which is which. If they cannot be told apart, the cheaper model writes the rest. Chapter 68, on the two partitions, is second, because it is the hardest test of telling one event from every side. After those two the author reviews the voice, the length, the images, and the checking, and this plan is revised before anything else is written. The remaining chapters then follow in order from chapter 1.

**Formats.** The chapters are written as plain text files, and all three editions are built from that single source. The website is the main edition: maps can be enlarged, each image links to its ledger entry, and a reader can open one chapter without downloading the book. The PDF is the faithful, printable download. The e-book is offered for reading on small screens and e-readers, where maps show less detail.

**Publication.** The book is written for its author first. If it turns out well, it is released free to read in all three formats, with the image ledgers published alongside it. Errors found after release are corrected on the website, and each correction is listed with its date.
