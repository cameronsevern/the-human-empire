## Factual flags

1. **Major — new economic explanation is false.**

   > “In an honest trade, goods of equal value would have gone back the other way to pay for them.”

   Legitimate trade does not require equal-value merchandise to return along the same route. Payment can involve money, credit, financial claims, or transactions with other trading partners. Morel’s conclusion needs the particular evidence of exploitation in the Congo; this general rule cannot establish it. **Correct fact:** exports and imports of goods need not balance, even in legitimate commerce. [Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, explanation of the balance of payments](https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/page-one-economics/2025/oct/what-is-the-balance-of-payments).

2. **Major — muzzle-loading does not establish weapon parity.**

   > “On a battlefield, therefore, the two sides were roughly matched weapon for weapon.”

   The preceding sentence places this parity before the 1860s. But muzzle-loading weapons differed substantially in ignition, accuracy, and range. Britain adopted the Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle-musket before that cutoff; the National Army Museum gives its effective range as 800–1,000 yards. **Correct fact:** European and African forces could both use muzzle-loaders without having equivalent weapons; the widening technological gap included rifled muskets before breech-loaders and machine guns. [National Army Museum, Pattern 1853 Enfield](https://collection.nam.ac.uk/detail.php?acc=1979-07-60-1).

3. **Major — examples of earning tax money become a false exhaustive choice.**

   > “It could do so in one of two ways: by growing a crop that Europe wanted, such as cocoa, cotton or groundnuts, or by sending its young men to work for wages in a mine or on a settler's farm.”

   Families also earned cash by selling food and livestock into African markets, trading, and undertaking other paid work. **Correct fact:** export cultivation and mine or settler-farm employment were important routes, not the only two. Research on colonial Kigezi documents cash earnings from food crops, livestock, and regional trade. [G. Carswell, “Food Crops as Cash Crops: The Case of Colonial Kigezi, Uganda”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1471-0366.00065).

4. **Minor — the new railway sentence reintroduces the earlier false contrast.**

   > “A railway built for the people who lived there would have joined their towns to one another.”

   In context, this implies that the colonial lines failed to connect towns. They did connect towns: the colonial Kayes–Bamako railway is one example. **Correct fact:** export and imperial priorities shaped the networks, but connecting African towns and extracting resources were compatible functions. The old sentence flagged in `check.md` was corrected; this new addition brings its implication back. [World Bank, railway history, Annex 1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08ca3e5274a27b2001313/C21-TP8.pdf).

5. **Minor — “for good” overstates Adwa’s result.**

   > “It showed that a European army could be beaten for good, and it also showed what that took.”

   Adwa decisively ended that Italian attempt at conquest, but Italy invaded again in 1935 and occupied Ethiopia in 1936–41. **Correct fact:** the victory secured Ethiopian independence against the nineteenth-century invasion; it did not permanently end Italian conquest attempts. [Bahru Zewde, *A History of Modern Ethiopia*, “The Italian Occupation 1936–1941”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/history-of-modern-ethiopia-18551991/italian-occupation-19361941/282596A9423875613429990FBCE4696D).

6. **Minor — a remaining internal contradiction from the earlier check.**

   > “One African state came through, and the reason is instructive.”

   The opening now explicitly identifies **Ethiopia and Liberia** as exceptions to European rule. Naming Liberia fixed the earlier omission, but this sentence still reduces two surviving states to one. **Correct fact:** Ethiopia is the example here of independence preserved through defeating an invading European army. Source: the chapter’s own sentence, “Thirty years later they ruled all of it except Ethiopia and Liberia…”.

## Clarity flags

- **Signposting:**  
  > “The speed of what followed can be put in two figures.”  
  > “The contest had a logic that drove it on.”  
  > “The catch lay in the last four words.”

  These announce an explanation instead of giving it. The last also makes readers count backward through the prose. All conflict with the prohibition on signpost sentences.

- **A restored qualification disappears again:**  
  > “Europeans had been sailing along this coast for four hundred years, so their absence from the interior needs explaining.”

  “Their absence” loses the opening’s carefully restored “with few exceptions.” It also makes exploration and permanent occupation sound interchangeable.

- **Exception-breaking absolutes:**  
  > “The belief did what no chief could have done, and brought separate peoples into a single rising.”  
  > “That rivalry, which had hurried the conquest everywhere else, could be turned to his advantage.”

  The useful claims are that the belief united different peoples and that Menelik exploited European rivalry. “No chief could have done” and “everywhere else” add unnecessary universal claims.

- **Ambiguous physical attendance:**  
  > “These pieces of paper were what Leopold took to the conference.”

  “Took” invites readers to picture Leopold attending and presenting the documents himself. The intended point appears to concern his diplomatic case and representatives.

- **Unclear accounting:**  
  > “The territories that had been grabbed just in case mostly cost more to hold than they brought in, and their parliaments were unwilling to spend money on them.”

  “Brought in” could mean colonial tax revenue, metropolitan revenue, export earnings, or private profits. These are different accounts. The new sentence sounds more precise than “did not pay” while leaving the measure unspecified.

- **An institutional conclusion does not follow from individual mortality:**  
  > “An army or a trading company that lost its men at that rate could not stay long.”

  Readers may reasonably wonder how the coastal trading establishments already described persisted. Staff dying and an institution withdrawing are different things; replacement personnel are the missing distinction.

- **Repetition and padding:**  
  > “The general sent to restore order was not content with defeating them. He defeated them…”  
  > “As a protection, however, it failed. The bullets did not turn to water.”  
  > “The difference was the difference between an ally and a subject.”

  These repeat words or deliver the same point twice. The general’s actions already demonstrate his purpose; the second water sentence already demonstrates the protection’s failure.

- **An added sentence is immediately explained away:**  
  > “It was an old state with a long memory of itself.”

  The next sentence supplies the concrete antiquity, church, script, and imperial lineage. This addition contributes little beyond an abstract preview.

- **Scope of “Europeans”:**  
  > “To begin with, there were never many Europeans.”

  The following Nigerian example concerns administrators, whereas this sentence appears to cover all Europeans throughout colonial Africa, including settler societies. Specify the administrative population or geographical scope.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions, or body cross-references. The ordinary transitions and explanatory breathing room are generally consistent with the style sheet.

## Overall

The revision improves continuity, but it is not fact-neutral: several bridges introduce new claims, and three materially distort the explanation of trade, weapons, or taxation. Most earlier corrections remain intact; the railway implication returns, and the Ethiopia–Liberia contradiction remains partly unresolved. No files were edited.
