## Changed facts

No existing date, figure, personal name or quotation was changed or removed. However, the revision adds factual claims and changes the scope or sequence of several existing ones. These are not all errors.

1. **Parliament’s role becomes an explicit claim.**

   Old: “Parliament was a decoration, and in 1975 the shah merged the tame parties into one and told those who did not care to join that they could have their passports and leave.”

   New: “Parliament was a decoration: it met, but it did not decide.”

   The metaphor now explicitly denies parliament decision-making power.

2. **The economic passage acquires additional causal claims.**

   Old: “Dams, steel mills and universities went up, and when the price of oil quadrupled in 1973 the spending became frantic.”

   New: “Then, in 1973, the price of oil quadrupled. Iran sold oil, so the money flowing to the shah grew almost overnight, and the spending became frantic. The capital swelled to match.”

   The revision explicitly connects Tehran’s growth to the oil windfall, although the following population figures cover 1956–76.

   Old: “The money did as much harm as good.”

   New: “The money did as much harm as good, and it did the harm in several ways at once.”

   Added: “Second, the land reform turned out to be less generous than it looked.”

   The new numbered explanation makes disappointing land reform appear to be another harm caused by the oil money.

3. **A complete absence of lawful complaint is introduced.**

   Old: No corresponding sentence.

   New: “Each of these groups now had a grievance, and none of them had a lawful way to voice it.”

   This is stronger than the opening’s “silenced nearly every voice of complaint except the mosque.”

4. **Independent clerical funding becomes funding the king could not interrupt.**

   Old: “The Shia clergy therefore had their own income, their own colleges and a network that reached every village, and they owed nothing to the state.”

   New: “Because the money came straight from believers, the Shia clergy had their own income, their own colleges and a network that reached every village, and they owed nothing to the state. A king could arrest a cleric. He could not cut off the money that kept the rest of them going.”

   The final two sentences introduce a stronger claim about the limits of royal power.

5. **Expulsion acquires an explicit motive and a comparative outcome.**

   Old: “That autumn the shah persuaded Iraq to expel him.”

   New: “That autumn the shah persuaded Iraq to expel him, hoping to cut him off.”

   Old: No corresponding sentence.

   New: “Far from being silenced, he now had a larger audience than ever.”

6. **Coalition diversity becomes an impossibility of agreement.**

   Old: “Each group pictured a different Iran afterwards.”

   New: “A crowd so mixed could never have agreed on a programme. Each group pictured a different Iran afterwards.”

   Added: “Because he would not bargain, he became the voice of all of them.”

   These introduce an absolute claim about agreement and an explicit explanation of Khomeini’s leadership.

7. **A list of revolutionary institutions becomes a chronological sequence.**

   Old: “Committees based in the mosques took over the neighbourhoods. A new armed force, the Revolutionary Guards, was recruited from the young men of the poor districts, answerable to the clerics and not to the army. Revolutionary courts under a hanging judge tried the shah’s generals and ministers in minutes and had them shot on a school roof.”

   New: “First, committees based in the mosques took over the neighbourhoods. Next, a new armed force, the Revolutionary Guards, was recruited from the young men of the poor districts, answerable to the clerics and not to the army. The clergy, in other words, now had soldiers of their own. Then came revolutionary courts under a hanging judge, a man who handed out death sentences freely. They tried the shah’s generals and ministers in minutes and had them shot on a school roof.”

   “First,” “Next” and “Then” add an order of events absent from the original.

8. **Women are explicitly identified as the first former allies attacked.**

   Old: “The new order turned on its former allies.”

   New: “The new order also turned on its other former allies, and the women had felt it first.”

   Added: “The mixed crowd of 1978 had been narrowed down to the clergy and those who followed them.”

   The second addition describes the surviving political coalition, rather than merely recounting repression.

9. **Saddam’s listed considerations become an exhaustive explanation.**

   Old: “He expected an easy victory over a country in chaos, and he feared the effect of Khomeini’s example on his own Shia majority.”

   New: “He had two reasons. He expected an easy victory over a country in chaos, and he feared the effect of Khomeini’s example on his own Shia majority, since a Shia revolution next door might inspire one at home.”

   “He had two reasons” changes the scope; the final clause explains an existing point.

10. **Iran’s manpower is explicitly presented as compensation for equipment shortages.**

    Old: “Iran, short of tanks and aircraft, sent waves of young volunteers across minefields.”

    New: “Iran, short of tanks and aircraft, made up the difference with men, and sent waves of young volunteers across minefields.”

    Added nearby: “Iraq had the richer friends.”

11. **Qutb’s radicalisation becomes a claim about the Brotherhood generally.**

    Old: “One of its writers, a literary critic who had spent two years in America and been disgusted by what he saw, was tortured in prison, and wrote there that rulers who did not govern by God’s law were not Muslims at all and could rightly be overthrown.”

    New: “Under the ban, the movement’s thinking hardened. One of its writers, a literary critic who had spent two years in America and been disgusted by what he saw, was tortured in prison. There he wrote that rulers who did not govern by God’s law were not Muslims at all, and that they could therefore rightly be overthrown.”

    The substantive addition is the first sentence: an individual’s position now characterises the movement.

12. **The Six-Day War’s participants and outcome are made explicit.**

    Old: “The six days of June 1967 discredited them.”

    New: “The six days of war in June 1967, in which Israel beat their armies, discredited them.”

    This is an accurate explanatory addition.

13. **The Afghan passage adds an explicit explanation of village bombing and civilian suffering.**

    Old: “The Soviets answered by bombing the villages that sheltered them.”

    New: “The fighters lived among the villagers, so the Soviets answered by bombing the villages that sheltered them. Ordinary people paid most of the price.”

    The first addition largely unpacks the original; the second explicitly identifies civilians as bearing the greatest burden.

14. **The aid-distribution arrangement becomes explicitly exclusive.**

    Old: “The money was handed out by the intelligence service of Pakistan, which favoured the most fiercely religious commanders.”

    New: “Neither country handed out the money itself. That job fell to the intelligence service of Pakistan, which favoured the most fiercely religious commanders.”

    This strengthens the claim from Pakistan administering the programme to neither donor distributing its funds directly.

15. **Birth dates become a claim about personal experience.**

    Old: “Two thirds of Iranians have been born since the revolution.”

    New: “Two thirds of Iranians have been born since the revolution, and so have never known any other kind of state.”

    Being born after 1979 establishes that they did not live under Iran’s monarchy; it does not establish that they have never lived under or known another political system.

The added definitions of *shah*, *bazaar*, *jurists*, immunity and martial law explain existing facts. Other additions chiefly restate existing implications—for example, the cassette’s portability, the bitterness of accepting peace and the effect of sealed coffins.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — the new institutional chronology is misleading.**

   > “Next, a new armed force, the Revolutionary Guards, was recruited from the young men of the poor districts, answerable to the clerics and not to the army.”
   >
   > “Then came revolutionary courts under a hanging judge, a man who handed out death sentences freely.”

   Revolutionary courts were already conducting trials and executions in February 1979; the first executions occurred on 15–16 February. The Guards emerged from earlier armed groups but were formally established in May. Presenting the courts as a subsequent institution reverses the documented chronology. The original unnumbered list avoided that assertion. Sources: [Amnesty International’s contemporary report](https://www.iranrights.org/library/document/338), [RAND’s history and chronology of the Guards](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/21/76/07/28/2021760728/2021760728.pdf).

2. **Major — Qutb’s position is attributed to the movement as a whole.**

   > “Under the ban, the movement’s thinking hardened.”

   Followed by Qutb’s argument for overthrowing supposedly un-Islamic rulers, this implies that his position became the Brotherhood’s collective direction. Repression produced competing tendencies. Its leader Hasan al-Hudaybi opposed the radical doctrine of declaring other Muslims unbelievers and developed a more moderate position. Attribute the hardening to Qutb and his followers. Source: [Oxford University Press, “Opening to Reform: Hasan Al-Hudaybi’s Legacy”](https://academic.oup.com/book/6222/chapter-abstract/149866045).

3. **Minor — the new sentence makes Saddam’s motives exhaustive.**

   > “He had two reasons.”

   The two following considerations are defensible, but they were not the complete set. Territorial objectives, especially control of the Shatt al-Arab waterway, and ambitions for regional predominance also mattered. Removing the exhaustive setup would preserve the chosen emphasis without changing the historical explanation into an exclusive claim. Source: [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Iran–Iraq War,” especially “Iraq’s objectives in invading Iran”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/iraq-vii-iran-iraq-war/).

4. **Minor — the revision reintroduces an absolute about political expression.**

   > “Each of these groups now had a grievance, and none of them had a lawful way to voice it.”

   This newly added sentence goes beyond the opening’s already-corrected account of repression. In 1977, limited liberalisation permitted public opposition through open letters, meetings and other activity; the National Front was allowed to circulate critical letters. Effective political representation was severely restricted, but that is different from having no lawful means of expressing a grievance. Sources: [Encyclopaedia Iranica on opposition mobilisation](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/class-system-vi/), [contemporary reporting in *The New Yorker*](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1978/12/18/letter-from-iran).

5. **Minor — Gromov did not finish crossing alone. Unchanged, but not flagged in the earlier check.**

   > “On 15 February 1989 the last Soviet general walked alone across the bridge over the border river, and said that there was not a single soldier behind him.”

   Gromov travelled in the final armoured personnel carrier, met his son Maxim on the bridge, and walked the final stretch with him. “Alone” is incorrect. Source: [contemporary Associated Press report, reproduced in *The Daily Iowan*, 16 February 1989](https://dailyiowan.lib.uiowa.edu/DI/1989/di1989-02-16.pdf).

## Clarity flags

1. **The new oil-boom bridge blurs an otherwise clear timeframe.**

   > “The capital swelled to match.”

   This follows the 1973 windfall but introduces population growth from 1956 to 1976. The dates themselves are clear and unchanged; the new bridge makes a twenty-year transformation sound like a response to the final three years’ oil revenue.

2. **The numbered economic explanation mixes different causes.**

   > “The money did as much harm as good, and it did the harm in several ways at once.”
   >
   > “Second, the land reform turned out to be less generous than it looked.”

   Inflation fits the oil-money explanation. Inadequate land redistribution belongs to the earlier reform programme. The numbered structure falsely makes them parallel effects of the same cause.

3. **Several additions announce the next point instead of stating it.**

   > “That last detail mattered enormously for politics.”
   >
   > “He had two reasons.”
   >
   > “Instead, an event in November destroyed them, and it began far away.”

   These are signposts of the kind the style sheet prohibits. The following sentences already supply the political consequence, considerations or event.

4. **The coalition sentence uses an unnecessary absolute.**

   > “A crowd so mixed could never have agreed on a programme.”

   “Never” turns a description of this coalition’s divisions into a universal impossibility. The following sentence—“Each group pictured a different Iran afterwards”—makes the intended point without that claim.

5. **Financial independence becomes categorical immunity.**

   > “He could not cut off the money that kept the rest of them going.”

   The paragraph establishes decentralised funding, which explains resilience. It does not establish that the state was incapable of interrupting funding. This is an exception-fragile absolute, not merely a confident interpretation.

6. **Some explanatory additions repeat wording without adding a step.**

   > “A tape was small, cheap and easy to copy, so the voice of a man long in exile could be heard in a Tehran taxi.”

   The previous sentence already describes tapes being copied and played in taxis. The portability explanation helps; the second taxi repeats the destination.

   > “Women who had never worn the long black veil put it on as a badge of defiance, turning a garment into a protest against the king.”

   “Turning a garment into a protest” repeats “a badge of defiance.”

   > “The result was the opposite of the obedience it wanted.”

   Between the account of coercive reforms and “Within a year most of the country was in revolt,” this supplies little beyond another announcement of the result.

7. **The added constitutional gloss clashes with the next sentences.**

   > “On paper it looked like a democracy.”

   The same paragraph says that the constitution itself placed an unelected supreme leader above elected institutions. The intended distinction is between its elected offices and its overriding clerical authority; “on paper” suggests that the authoritarian structure existed only in practice.

8. **The final addition overstates what the demographic fact establishes.**

   > “Two thirds of Iranians have been born since the revolution, and so have never known any other kind of state.”

   “Known” can mean experienced, encountered or understood, and Iranians can have lived abroad. The clear point is that this generation has never lived under Iran’s monarchy.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-reference or body cross-references.

## Overall

The revision improves accessibility, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. Its main risks are newly imposed chronology, broader attribution and absolute claims introduced as connective prose. Most earlier corrections remain intact. No files were edited.
