# 99. Why did Iran have an Islamic revolution?

*Iran, Afghanistan and the Muslim world, from 1963 to 1989*

Iran had a revolution because its king spent twenty-five years forcing the country to become modern at great speed, with oil money and a secret police, and silenced nearly every voice of complaint except the mosque. When nearly all Iranians turned against him at once, it was an exiled cleric who spoke for them. Once the king had gone, that cleric pushed aside his allies and built a state of a kind that had not existed before, a modern republic ruled by a religious judge.

On the morning of 1 February 1979 a chartered French airliner circled low over Tehran before landing, so that its passengers could see what was waiting. Between the airport and the cemetery where the dead of the past year were buried, some three million people lined the road. It was among the largest crowds that had ever gathered anywhere.

The man they had come for was seventy-six. He wore a black turban and a brown cloak, and he had a white beard and heavy black eyebrows over eyes that rarely looked at the person he was speaking to. Ruhollah Khomeini had not seen his country for more than fourteen years.

Over the Iranian border, an American television reporter on the aircraft leaned across and asked him what he felt on returning. The translator repeated the question. Khomeini answered with a single word in Persian. Nothing.

## The king of kings

The ruler whom he had overthrown had left the country two weeks earlier, in tears, carrying a small box of Iranian soil.

Mohammad Reza Pahlavi was the shah who had been restored to his throne in 1953 by a coup arranged from Washington and London. He never lost the stain of it. He was a shy, vain man, devoted to his country's greatness and convinced that he alone understood how to achieve it. Iran, he said, would be among the five leading powers of the world by the end of the century.

He set out to drag it there. In 1963 he announced a programme of reform from above. The great estates were broken up and sold to the peasants. Women were given the vote. Young conscripts were sent to teach in the villages. Dams, steel mills and universities went up, and when the price of oil quadrupled in 1973 the spending became frantic. Tehran, a city of one and a half million in 1956, held four and a half million twenty years later. Tens of thousands of foreign advisers and technicians, most of them American, lived there on salaries that Iranians could only dream of.

None of it was open to discussion. Parliament was a decoration, and in 1975 the shah merged the tame parties into one and told those who did not care to join that they could have their passports and leave. His secret police, trained by the Americans and the Israelis, had informers in offices and classrooms across the country, and tortured many of those it arrested. In 1971 he celebrated what he called the two thousand five hundredth anniversary of the Persian monarchy with a party for the world's kings and presidents in the ruins of the ancient capital, in a city of silk tents, with food flown in from Paris. Most villages in the country had no running water.

The money did as much harm as good. Prices soared. Peasants who had been given plots too small to live on drifted into shanty towns around the cities. The old merchant families of the bazaar were squeezed by new supermarkets and by officials who took bribes. On the last night of 1977 the American president, visiting Tehran, raised his glass and called Iran an island of stability in one of the more troubled areas of the world.

## The cleric

One kind of institution the shah could wound but could not close.

Nine Iranians in ten belong to the Shia branch of Islam, which differs from the Sunni majority of the Muslim world over who should have succeeded the Prophet. The Shia of Iran hold that the rightful line of leaders ran to a twelfth, who vanished in the ninth century and will return at the end of time. Until then, the faithful are guided by learned jurists. Each believer chooses one of the most senior of these, who are called ayatollahs, follows his rulings, and pays him a religious tax. The Shia clergy therefore had their own income, their own colleges and a network that reached every village, and they owed nothing to the state.

Khomeini was a teacher of law and philosophy in the seminary city of Qom. In 1963 he denounced the shah's reforms from the pulpit as an attack on Islam, and was arrested, and the riots that followed were put down with several hundred dead. The next year the government granted American military personnel immunity from Iranian courts. Khomeini said that if the shah ran over an American dog, he would be called to account, but if an American cook ran over the shah, nobody could do anything. They have reduced the Iranian people, he said, to a level lower than that of an American dog. He was put on a plane to Turkey.

He settled in a Shia holy city in Iraq, and there, in a series of lectures in 1970, he set out an idea that most of his fellow ayatollahs found startling. The clergy had traditionally kept their distance from government, which they regarded as a worldly and slightly unclean business. Khomeini argued that Islam contained all the laws that a society needed, and that it followed that those who knew the law best should rule. While the hidden leader was absent, the state should be under the guardianship of the most learned jurist.

## Forty days

In January 1978 a government newspaper published an article that called Khomeini a foreign agent and a writer of love poetry. Seminary students in Qom marched in protest, and the police shot several of them.

In Shia custom the dead are mourned again on the fortieth day. Forty days later there were memorial marches in a dozen cities, and in one of them the army fired and killed more. Forty days after that the cycle repeated on a larger scale, and so it went on through the spring, each set of funerals producing the next.

In August somebody locked the doors of a cinema in the oil city of Abadan and set it on fire, and about four hundred people burned to death. It was the work of religious militants, and most of the country believed that the secret police had done it. On 8 September, the morning after martial law was declared, soldiers fired on crowds in a square in Tehran and the streets around it and killed about ninety people. Rumour made it thousands, and after that there was no going back.

Khomeini's voice had been reaching Iran for years by an unexpected route. His sermons were recorded on cassette tapes, carried across the border by pilgrims, copied by the thousand in the bazaars, and played in mosques and taxis. That autumn the shah persuaded Iraq to expel him. It was a mistake. He moved to a village outside Paris, with good telephone lines and the world's journalists at his door. To them he spoke of freedom and independence, and said that the clergy had no wish to govern.

In October the oil workers went on strike, and the government's income stopped. In December, on the holiest days of the Shia year, more than a million people marched through Tehran.

## Everyone's revolution

It was not a religious uprising alone, and that was its strength. The marchers included bazaar merchants and slum dwellers, communists and nationalists who revered the memory of the prime minister overthrown in 1953, engineers, students and schoolgirls. Women who had never worn the long black veil put it on as a badge of defiance. Each group pictured a different Iran afterwards. They agreed on one thing, and Khomeini, who refused every offer of compromise, said it more plainly than anyone: the shah must go.

The shah had lost his nerve. He was dying of cancer, which he had kept secret even from his American allies, and he could not bring himself to order the massacre that might have saved him. The conscripts of his army were deserting. On 16 January 1979 he flew out, and never returned.

## The guardian

Most of the educated Iranians who had marched expected the old man to retire to Qom and leave government to the politicians. He appointed a mild, elderly engineer as prime minister. Ten days after his return the army declared itself neutral, and the shah's last government melted away. For a few months it looked as if they were right.

The real power was being assembled elsewhere. Committees based in the mosques took over the neighbourhoods. A new armed force, the Revolutionary Guards, was recruited from the young men of the poor districts, answerable to the clerics and not to the army. Revolutionary courts under a hanging judge tried the shah's generals and ministers in minutes and had them shot on a school roof. In March a referendum asked a single question, whether Iran should be an Islamic republic, and ninety-eight per cent of the voters said yes.

The constitution, drafted that autumn and approved in December, was Khomeini's lecture turned into law. Iran would have an elected president and parliament. Above them would stand a supreme leader, a jurist, with command of the armed forces and the last word on everything. A council of clerics and jurists could strike down any law that it judged contrary to Islam, and it came in time to decide who was permitted to stand for election.

The liberals might still have resisted, and an event in November destroyed them. The shah had been admitted to a hospital in New York. Iranians remembered 1953 and suspected a plot to bring him back. On 4 November several hundred students climbed the wall of the American embassy in Tehran and took its staff prisoner. Khomeini had not ordered it. He saw its use at once and gave it his blessing. The prime minister resigned in protest, and anyone who argued for moderation could now be called a friend of America. Fifty-two Americans were held for four hundred and forty-four days, paraded blindfold before the cameras. An attempt to rescue them by helicopter ended in a collision in the desert. They were released within minutes of a new American president taking office.

The new order turned on its former allies. In March 1979, within weeks of the victory, women had been told to cover their hair at work, and when thousands marched against it they were attacked by gangs with knives. Within four years the veil was the law, and the left-wing parties that had helped to make the revolution had been crushed and several thousand of their members executed.

## Poison

What bound the country to its new rulers was an invasion.

In September 1980 the ruler of Iraq, Saddam Hussein, sent his army across the border. He expected an easy victory over a country in chaos, and he feared the effect of Khomeini's example on his own Shia majority. Iranians of every opinion rallied to defend their soil. Within two years they had driven the invader out. Khomeini then refused to stop, and sent his armies into Iraq to overthrow its ruler.

The war lasted eight years, longer than either of the world wars, and it resembled the first of them. The armies dug trenches across the marshes at the head of the Gulf. Iran, short of tanks and aircraft, sent waves of young volunteers across minefields. Iraq used poison gas, year after year, against Iranian soldiers and against a Kurdish town of its own, where five thousand people died in a day. The Arab oil states paid for Iraq's weapons, the Soviet Union and France sold most of them, and the Western powers, which feared Iran more, looked away from the gas. In July 1988 an American warship in the Gulf shot down an Iranian airliner by mistake, killing two hundred and ninety people.

A few weeks later Khomeini accepted a ceasefire. The border had not moved. Somewhere near half a million men were dead. He told his people that the decision was more deadly to him than drinking poison. He died the following June, and millions came to his funeral.

## The solution

Iran's revolution was Shia, and most Muslims are Sunni. All the same it sent a shock through the whole Muslim world, because it showed that a movement calling itself Islamic could bring down a powerful modern state backed by America.

The idea that Islam should be the basis of government had been growing for fifty years. In 1928 a schoolteacher in Egypt had founded a society called the Muslim Brotherhood, to resist British rule and Western habits by returning to the faith. It ran schools, clinics and sports clubs, and its slogan came to be that Islam is the solution. Egypt's military rulers banned it. One of its writers, a literary critic who had spent two years in America and been disgusted by what he saw, was tortured in prison, and wrote there that rulers who did not govern by God's law were not Muslims at all and could rightly be overthrown. He was hanged in 1966, and his book has been the handbook of militants ever since.

For a generation such ideas had been drowned out by another creed. The heroes of the Arab world in the 1950s and 1960s were army officers who promised unity, socialism and the defeat of Israel. The six days of June 1967 discredited them. Many young Arabs concluded that they had lost because they had abandoned God. The oil boom then put enormous sums in the hands of Saudi Arabia, whose rulers used them to spread their own austere version of Sunni Islam.

On 20 November 1979 several hundred armed men seized the Grand Mosque in Mecca, the holiest place in Islam, with tens of thousands of pilgrims inside. Their leader was a Saudi preacher who denounced the royal family as corrupt puppets of the West. It took the Saudi army two weeks, and French advisers, to dislodge them, and hundreds died. The royal family was badly frightened. It answered by giving its clerics more power over daily life and more money to preach abroad.

From then on the two governments, one Shia and revolutionary, one Sunni and royal, competed for the leadership of the faithful, and each backed its own militias across the region. What outsiders called political Islam was never one movement. Its branches denounced one another as heretics.

## The bleeding wound

Afghanistan, Iran's eastern neighbour, was a poor, mountainous country of villages and tribes, and in 1978 a small communist party seized power there in a coup. It set out to remake the countryside by decree. It redistributed land, cancelled debts, and ordered that girls be sent to school, and it shot the village preachers and landlords who objected. Within a year most of the country was in revolt.

The Soviet leaders in Moscow feared that the fall of a communist government on their border would encourage their own fifty million Muslim subjects. At Christmas 1979 Soviet commandos stormed the palace in Kabul, killed the Afghan president, whom Moscow no longer trusted, and installed another. A hundred thousand Soviet soldiers followed.

They found themselves fighting a war that their army had not been built for. They held the towns and the main roads. The countryside belonged to bands of fighters who called themselves warriors of the faith and who ambushed convoys in the mountain passes. The Soviets answered by bombing the villages that sheltered them. About a million Afghans were killed, and five million, a third of the population, fled to camps in Pakistan and Iran.

The United States saw a chance to do to the Soviet Union what had been done to America in Vietnam. Over ten years it sent the fighters several billion dollars in weapons, and Saudi Arabia matched the sum dollar for dollar. The money was handed out by the intelligence service of Pakistan, which favoured the most fiercely religious commanders. From 1986 the Americans supplied a shoulder-fired missile that could bring down a helicopter.

The war was also declared a holy cause, and volunteers came from across the Arab world. One of them was the tall, soft-spoken son of the richest builder in Saudi Arabia. Osama bin Laden brought money and bulldozers, and in 1988, in a Pakistani border city, he and a handful of others founded an organisation to carry the fight elsewhere once this one was won. They called it al-Qaeda, the Base.

The Soviet army lost fifteen thousand men. Their bodies came home in sealed zinc coffins that families were forbidden to open. The Soviet leader called the war a bleeding wound. On 15 February 1989 the last Soviet general walked alone across the bridge over the border river, and said that there was not a single soldier behind him. The Afghans who had beaten him took three more years to bring down the government that he left behind, and then turned their guns on one another.

## Hair

Iran is still governed under the constitution of 1979. The supreme leader who succeeded Khomeini held the post for more than thirty-five years. Two thirds of Iranians have been born since the revolution.

In September 2022 a young woman of twenty-two, visiting Tehran from the Kurdish west of the country, was stopped by the morality police because some of her hair was showing under her headscarf. She died in their custody three days later. For months afterwards, in every province, women and schoolgirls took off their scarves in the street, burned them, and cut their hair in public. Their slogan was three words: woman, life, freedom. Several hundred of them and of the men who marched with them were shot.

Many of those girls were the granddaughters of women who had put on the black veil in 1978, by choice, to march against a king.
