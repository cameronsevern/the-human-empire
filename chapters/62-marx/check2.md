## Factual flags

1. **Major — new chronological contradiction.**

   > “Pay was the first thing to change.”

   The chapter dates the wage improvement from about 1850 but itself places the repeal of the union ban in 1824 and workplace protections earlier. Factory legislation in 1833 and the Ten Hours Act in 1847 preceded the stated wage turning point. Pay therefore was not the first of these improvements. [Sources: Parliament’s factory-legislation chronology](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/livinglearning/19thcentury/keydates/), [1824 repeal Act](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo4/5/95/pdfs/ukpga_18240095_en.pdf).

2. **Major — a new sentence reintroduces a previously corrected misrepresentation.**

   > “His argument needed the workers to be held down until they rose.”

   In context, the following “Instead, their lot slowly improved” makes this mean that improving living standards contradicted a necessary condition of Marx’s argument. Marx explicitly allowed rising wages, better consumption and workers’ savings while arguing that exploitation continued. His theory did not require uninterrupted material stagnation until revolution. The chapter’s retained acknowledgment that he welcomed the ten-hour day further undermines this new framing. This concerns the new assertion, not the defensible interpretation that capitalism proved more adaptable than he expected. [Source: *Capital*, volume I, chapter 25, section 1](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch25.htm).

3. **Minor — misleading description of Smith’s equipment.**

   > “The workshop he described was still a room of men with hand tools.”

   The contrast with subsequent machinery implies that Smith’s example involved only hand tools. Smith expressly described the workshop’s machinery and made machinery one of the reasons divided labour increased output. The accurate distinction is between this small pin manufactory and later mechanised textile factories, not between hand tools and machinery. [Source: *The Wealth of Nations*, book I, chapter 1](https://dhspriory.org/kenny/PhilTexts/Smith/WealthNations.htm).

4. **Minor — inaccurate mechanism of the 1815 Corn Law, reinforced by the addition.**

   > “Since 1815 Britain had taxed imported grain heavily, to protect the incomes of the landowners who filled Parliament.”
   >
   > “The tax kept foreign grain out, and so it kept the price of a loaf up.”

   The 1815 law principally prohibited foreign grain from entering domestic consumption below specified British price thresholds; above those thresholds it permitted admission without duty. Later arrangements used tariffs. Calling the whole sequence a heavy tax starting in 1815 conflates those mechanisms. “Restrictions on imported grain” would preserve the argument accurately. [Source: 1815 Importation Act, sections III–IV](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo3/55/26/pdfs/ukpga_18150026_en.pdf).

5. **Minor — the first potato failure was partial.**

   > “In 1845 a disease destroyed the potato crop in Ireland, where three million people ate almost nothing else.”

   This reads as destruction of the entire national crop in 1845. That year’s loss was substantial but partial; the much more extensive failure followed in 1846. A contemporary official assessment presented to Parliament estimated that half the 1845 crop was destroyed or unfit to eat. “Destroyed much of the potato crop” avoids importing the later catastrophe into the first year. [Source: Commons proceedings, 17 February 1846](https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/1846-02-17/debates/d676c88d-4705-4664-b656-6b6120f56b9c/CommonsChamber).

6. **Minor — lawful campaigning continued alongside machine-breaking.**

   > “With the lawful route closed, the hammers came out.”

   Failed petitions helped provoke Luddism, but this presents a clean replacement of petitioning by violence. Framework knitters were still organising parliamentary appeals in February 1812, after machine-breaking had begun in 1811. The correct sequence includes overlapping legal campaigning and destructive action. [Source: contemporary framework-knitters’ resolutions of 17 February 1812, reproduced in *Writings of the Luddites*](https://www.luddite.no/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Writings_of_the_Luddites.pdf).

## Clarity flags

- **Announcement rather than explanation:**  
  > “Dividing up work has a consequence, however.”  
  > “Instead, he proposed something much simpler.”  
  > “Their reasoning was short.”

  Each announces the explanatory sentence that follows. These are the clearest clashes with the prohibition on signpost sentences. The concrete pin-worker example and the actual reasoning already provide the transition.

- **An ambiguous absolute about earnings:**  
  > “None of this reading earned a penny.”

  Does this mean Marx received no payment for reading, that his economic research produced no immediate income, or that none of it informed paid journalism? Those are different claims. The existing account of insufficient newspaper earnings and Engels’s support already establishes the financial predicament.

- **Relief versus one relief programme:**  
  > “The relief did not last, however.”

  The next sentence says responsibility was transferred. Readers may infer that relief stopped altogether when the intended point is that the emergency soup-kitchen scheme ended.

- **An explanation placed before its evidence:**  
  > “He therefore saw the cotton trade from both sides.”

  At this point, readers have learned only that Engels worked in his father’s business. Mary Burns’s tours establish the second side in the following sentences. “Therefore” points backwards to an insufficient explanation.

- **Exception-fragile absolutes:**  
  > “An owner could do without any one worker for as long as he liked. A worker could not do without wages for more than a few days.”

  The bargaining imbalance is clear, but “any one,” “for as long as he liked” and “a few days” are unnecessary universal claims. An indispensable employee or a worker with savings breaks them.

- **Ownership does not eliminate every incentive:**  
  > “Since the customers were also the owners, the shop had no reason to cheat them.”

  Collective ownership explains the intended alignment of interests; it does not establish that managers or individual members could never benefit from dishonest conduct. “No reason” exceeds what the example demonstrates.

- **Repeated explanation:**  
  > “In that day his work adds more value to the cotton or the iron than the wage he is paid.”  
  > “In other words, he makes more in a day than he is given for it.”

  The second sentence repeats the first less precisely: “makes more” could mean total output rather than added value. The first sentence already explains the point plainly.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or prohibited cross-references. Many other added transitions comply with the sheet’s instruction to give readers room.

## Overall

The revision improves continuity, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. The most important repairs are the new chronology of British improvements and the new claim about what Marx’s argument required. Most earlier corrections remain intact. No files were edited.
