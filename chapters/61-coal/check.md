1. **Contradiction with chapter 53: the factory is invented twice.**  
   > “So the factory was invented, and it was invented without steam.”

   Chapter 53 explicitly says: “The sugar islands had factories a hundred years before the first cotton mill was built in England.” Chapter 61 also acknowledges Derby’s earlier silk factory. The defensible claim is that Cromford established a widely copied pattern for **cotton factories**, not that it invented the factory. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [chapter 53](chapters/53-sugar/chapter.md:53), [Cromford Mills’ history](https://www.cromfordmills.org.uk/about/our-history/).

2. **Factual overstatement: the wool comparison changes the denominator.**  
   > “If Lancashire’s mills had had to use wool instead of cotton, Britain would have needed more sheep pasture than it had land.”

   Pomeranz’s calculation is approximately **23 million additional acres for replacing 1830 cotton imports**, under particular assumptions about sheep and yarn yields. It does not exceed Britain’s entire land area. His comparison concerns agricultural acreage, not all land; the draft also drops the date and presents a counterfactual estimate as an unconditional fact. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence*, pp. 276 and 315](https://www.hansrajcollege.ac.in/hCPanel/uploads/elearning/elearning_document/Kenneth_Pomeranz_The_Great_Divergence_China.pdf).

3. **False absolute, contradicted within the chapter.**  
   > “Without the coal, the tinkerers would have had nothing to burn.”

   They had wood and charcoal. The chapter itself already says, “Engines can burn wood, and iron can be smelted with charcoal.” Coal’s advantage in this argument is that it supplied energy without requiring corresponding forest acreage—not that it was the only combustible fuel. This also directly breaks the rule against absolutes that one exception defeats. **Confidence: certain.** Source: [the chapter’s own counterfactual passage](chapters/61-coal/chapter.md:125).

4. **Factual overstatement: the energy transition is made too absolute.**  
   > “For all of history, humans lived on recent sunlight.”

   A few paragraphs later, coal supplies about half of England and Wales’s energy **before Newcomen’s engine**; the chapter also describes earlier Chinese coal use. The sustainable claim is that human societies **mostly** depended on recent sunlight, with substantial local exceptions already developing. The preceding “almost everything” does not fully rescue this separate absolute sentence. **Confidence: high.** Source: [the chapter’s coal baseline](chapters/61-coal/chapter.md:27).

5. **Incorrect interval.**  
   > “A century also passed between 1688 and Cromford.”

   Cromford’s first mill dates to 1771: **83 years**, or roughly eight decades, after 1688. The chapter itself supplies 1771–72 earlier. Since the elapsed time is being used as evidence against an explanation, the exaggeration matters. **Confidence: certain.** Source: [Historic England’s Cromford record](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1488614?section=official-listing).

6. **Continuity problem with chapter 55: cheap borrowing sounds immediate.**  
   > “Property was fairly safe from seizure, and the government could borrow cheaply because lenders trusted it to pay.”

   Following “After a revolution in 1688,” this compresses the financial change into an apparent immediate result. Chapter 55 carefully says it took a generation, with borrowing rates still reaching fourteen per cent in the 1690s and falling to three by the 1750s. Preserve that delay here. This is a misleading compression rather than an outright contradiction. **Confidence: high about the continuity issue.** Source: [chapter 55](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:119).

7. **Reader comprehension: spinning is explained, but weaving is assumed.**  
   > “The power loom, which had been invented in the 1780s but did not work well until the 1820s, finished the job.”

   A reader without textile knowledge has learned how fibres become thread, but not how a loom turns thread into cloth or what powering it changes. That missing step weakens the explanation of the handloom weaver’s displacement. One plain sentence explaining that weaving interlaces threads, and that the powered loom mechanised this work, would bridge the gap.

8. **Reader comprehension: the promised single thread changes dates and industries.**  
   > “Follow a single thread of cotton around 1850, and it runs across half the world.”

   The first person subsequently introduced is Crabtree, working in a **woollen** mill around 1818; the narrative then ranges through several decades and ultimately gives the fourth position to two people. The individual qualifications are honest, but the framing promises one contemporaneous cotton chain that the examples do not actually follow. Present these as connected kinds of experience across the factory age.

9. **Reader confusion and signposting: British workers are introduced after already appearing.**  
   > “So much for the people along the thread.”  
   > “What of Britain’s own workers, the ones the factories were supposed to enrich?”

   The child and handloom weaver just discussed are already British workers. The actual transition is from individual experiences to aggregate wages and health. These sentences both obscure that distinction and announce the next topic instead of making the connection.

10. **Style: the body debates the reliability of a minor source.**  
    > “A story told long after his death had him writing to one of London’s leading scientists, but when historians went looking for the letters they found no trace.”

    Whether scientific knowledge influenced Newcomen matters; the archival history of an unverified correspondence story need not. This conflicts with “The page never discusses whether a minor source can be trusted.” State that no firm evidence establishes what scientific work he knew, then continue. The surrounding uncertainty is **not** a hedging stack; the problem is the source-investigation detour.

11. **Style: explicit signpost sentences.**  
    > “The engine worked like this.”  
    > “The surprise is who built it.”  
    > “There are several big answers, each with something to it.”

    These announce an explanation, surprise or set of answers. The next sentences already supply the substance. “The surprise is who built it” is especially close to the style guide’s explicitly prohibited “Here is the strange part.”

12. **Style: the historical debate becomes an outline-shaped survey.**  
    > “The first is about prices.”  
    > “The second is about people.”  
    > “The third is luck of geography.”  
    > “The fourth is about rules.”  
    > “The fifth is empire and slavery.”

    The issue is not the positions taken or the inclusion of disagreement. It is the repeated proposition–objection format, which makes this section read as a checklist. It conflicts with the guide’s preference for a story rather than “a case built point by point,” and its instruction that planning elements should not become the prose’s form.

13. **Style: an unnecessary absolute in the closing image.**  
    > “The chimneys rise above the roofs in their hundreds, each one marking a mill where engines drive thousands of spindles, and each one breathing out the ancient forests of the coal.”

    “Each one” makes every chimney in view a steam-powered spinning mill. The chapter itself includes other coal-burning industries and domestic heating. Restrict the image to the cotton-mill chimneys or remove the universal claim.
