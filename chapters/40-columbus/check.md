1. **Factual—central argument; high confidence.** “They did not conquer these places, and they did little trading.” Also: “Their purpose was display.”

   Display mattered, but the fleets also conducted substantial commerce and military interventions. Zheng He captured a ruler of Sri Lanka in 1411; the voyages brought large quantities of pepper into China. Distinguish the absence of lasting territorial annexation from the absence of coercion. The relative importance of commerce and imperial domination is debated; “little trading” and display alone are not sound summaries. Sources: [Zheng He’s commemorative inscription](https://www.professorcampbell.org/sources/zhenghe.html), [Tansen Sen’s study of the expeditions](https://www.academia.edu/30950814/The_impact_of_Zheng_Hes_expeditions_on_Indian_Ocean_interactions), [Geoff Wade’s reassessment](https://ari.nus.edu.sg/publications/wps-31-the-zheng-he-voyages-a-reassessment/).

2. **Contradiction with chapter 33, factual overstatement and break of the absolutes rule; high confidence.** “The trading ships of the Indian Ocean were lighter, many of them sewn together with cord, and they carried no artillery, because no power had ever tried to control the open sea by force.”

   Chapter 33 describes Srivijaya’s navy compelling ships to enter its ports and plundering those that bypassed them, followed by the Chola naval attack of 1025. Zheng He supplies another precedent for force directed at maritime commerce. These were not continuous control of the whole ocean, but neither was Portugal’s empire. The defensible distinction is the Portuguese combination of naval artillery, forts and compulsory passes—not the invention of maritime coercion. Sources: [chapter 33](chapters/33-ocean/chapter.md), [Wade](https://ari.nus.edu.sg/publications/wps-31-the-zheng-he-voyages-a-reassessment/). The shipbuilding contrast also needs qualification: Indian builders used iron fastenings before the Portuguese arrived. [Richard Unger’s shipbuilding study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/08438714241272548).

3. **Factual—overcompressed explanation; high confidence.** “They had been the project of one emperor, and they were run by the servants of the palace, whom the regular officials detested.” Also: “Its government was also a single court, so that one decision settled the matter for a continent.”

   Yongle sponsored the first six expeditions, but Xuande commissioned the seventh after an interruption. The history itself demonstrates that a successor could reverse a decision. Moreover, ending these state expeditions did not end Chinese maritime commerce or tribute relations. Centralised patronage remains a defensible explanation for the fleets’ vulnerability; a single decision closing a continent does not. Sources: [voyage chronology](https://www.chinaheritagequarterly.org/articles.php?issue=002&searchterm=002_zhenghe.inc), [study of Ming maritime institutions](https://brill.com/view/journals/cahs/1/1/article-p3_3.pdf).

4. **Factual—misleading chronology and economic geography; high confidence.** “Out in the ocean, on the homeward loop, the Portuguese found the uninhabited islands of Madeira and the Azores, and planted them with sugar cane.”

   Following the 1434 Bojador passage, this makes both discoveries sound subsequent. Portuguese reconnaissance of Madeira dates to 1419 and of the Azores conventionally to 1427. It also conflates their economies: Madeira became the major sugar producer; the Azores supplied wheat, among other products. Sources: [NOVA University’s exploration chronology](https://eve.fcsh.unl.pt/pt/cronologias/exploracao-do-atlantico-1419-1500), [David Birmingham, *Trade and Empire in the Atlantic*](https://www.perlego.com/book/1617403/trade-and-empire-in-the-atlantic-14001600-pdf).

5. **Factual—wrong vessel, affecting the explanation; high confidence.** “A caravel passed Cape Bojador in 1434 and came back.”

   Gil Eanes passed it in a *barca*, not the lateen-rigged exploratory caravel just described. That matters because the sequence presents the caravel as enabling this particular breakthrough. Sources: [NOVA University’s Gil Eanes entry](https://eve.fcsh.unl.pt/pt/pessoas/gil-eanes), [Portuguese Navy museum’s account of the lateen caravel](https://cultura.marinha.pt/en/museumarinha_web/colecoes_web/pecasemdestaque_web/Pages/caravela-latina.aspx).

6. **Factual—doubtful explanation; high confidence that revision is needed.** “The second thing Europeans wanted was gold, since they had to pay for the spices with something and their own mines were nearly exhausted.”

   Europe experienced bullion shortages, but this is not equivalent to its gold mines being nearly exhausted. Hungarian goldfields, including those around Kremnica, were important late-medieval producers. State the demand for bullion without making general geological exhaustion its cause. Sources: [Peter Spufford, “The Victory of Gold”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/money-and-its-use-in-medieval-europe/victory-of-gold/C9F8E4197148083581BE589C2C50153E), [study of late-medieval precious-metal production](https://publ.royalacademy.dk/backend/web/uploads/2020-02-14/AFL%206/H_104_00_00_2009_5924/H_104_15_00_2009_2444.pdf).

7. **Factual—ending rests on an inaccurate formulation; high confidence.** “The line was fixed by its longitude, and so, for the next two and a half centuries, neither kingdom was able to say exactly where it ran.”

   Tordesillas specified **370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands**, not an agreed numerical longitude. The treaty left unresolved which island and which league supplied the measurement. Navigational limitations compounded an ambiguous definition; the problem was not simply waiting for accurate shipboard clocks. The treaty even proposed a joint expedition to locate the boundary. Sources: [treaty text](https://doctrineofdiscovery.org/tordesillas/), [Spain’s National Geographic Institute](https://www.ign.es/resources/docs/IGNCnig/catalogo_expoMadrid2019.pdf).

8. **Chronology and reader confusion; high confidence.** “The Spanish experts gave the same opinion as the Portuguese ones, and for six years the queen of Castile, Isabella, kept him waiting.”

   The section starts in 1488, then narrates the Portuguese rejection and move to Spain, making six years’ waiting before January 1492 impossible. Columbus had already moved to Castile in 1485 and approached its monarchs in 1486; 1488 was a renewed Portuguese approach. Identify the return visit or begin with the earlier rejection. Source: [Columbus biography and chronology](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/history/explorers-travelers-and-conquerors-biographies/christopher-columbus).

9. **Factual and unsupported superlative; high confidence on the superlative, medium-high on the misleading arrival scene.** “The giraffe had come home with the largest fleet the world had ever seen.”

   Earlier fleets were substantially larger by ship count and personnel: the Mongol invasions of Japan are clear counterexamples. Separately, Bengal’s envoys presented the giraffe in September 1414, while Zheng He’s main fourth expedition returned in 1415. Do not merge the animal’s arrival with the whole fleet’s homecoming. Sources: [Institute of Nautical Archaeology on the invasion fleets](https://nauticalarch.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/INAQ1991-18-1.pdf), [Sally Church, “The Giraffe of Bengal”](https://arinbasu.github.io/articles/giraffe_bengal.pdf).

10. **Factual—specific voyage asserted beyond the evidence; high confidence about the uncertainty.** “They sailed as far as South America and came back with the sweet potato.”

    Pre-European contact is well supported, as is the sweet potato’s presence in Polynesia. Those findings do not establish this particular outward-and-return voyage or its cargo. Genetic evidence allows competing reconstructions of who travelled where. Chapter 2’s “They even made contact with South America” is appropriately narrower; this is an unsupported strengthening rather than a direct contradiction. Source: [Ioannidis and colleagues, *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/S41586-020-2487-2).

11. **Factual—misleading technological origin story; high confidence.** “Portuguese shipbuilders borrowed the triangular sail used by Arab sailors, which is hung from a long slanting pole and can be swung along the length of the ship.”

    This presents a fresh fifteenth-century borrowing where a long Mediterranean history is needed. Lateen rigs were established in Mediterranean sailing centuries earlier, including among Christian sailors; their origins cannot safely be reduced to this transfer from Arab sailors. Sources: [Julian Whitewright’s research](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/466594/), [Museo Galileo’s account of medieval sailing rigs](https://brunelleschi.imss.fi.it/michaelofrhodes/ships_sailing.html).

12. **Factual—navigation simplified past accuracy; high confidence.** “In Lisbon it stands about thirty-nine degrees above the horizon, and at the equator it sits on the horizon itself.”

    This accurately approximates the **celestial pole**, but fifteenth-century Polaris circled several degrees away from it—about 3½° around 1500. Its observed height therefore changed through the night, requiring a correction. Without that distinction, the next sentence teaches a materially inaccurate method. Source: [*The Journal of Navigation*, “A Note on the Kamal”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02B9D2A3903EE7D758F9A83922FA3AA2/S0373463300033506a.pdf/note_on_the_kamal.pdf).

13. **Factual—century compressed into an immediate response; high confidence.** “When Portugal's experts said no to Columbus, Castile said yes, and when Castile found land, Portugal, England, France and the Netherlands all rushed to send ships of their own.”

    The Dutch expansion belongs substantially later: their first expedition to the East Indies sailed in 1595, more than a century after Columbus. Portugal’s exploration also preceded his voyage, as this chapter has already explained. The competition argument works, but “all rushed” falsely synchronises different developments. Source: [Rijksmuseum’s documentation of the 1595–1597 expedition](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/Fruitbomen-en-kruiden-op-Java-1596--55b749a78e8b2e8c8d8c7a094175c0f7).

14. **Factual—dubious ship detail; medium-high confidence.** “The largest of these were probably sixty or seventy metres long, with watertight compartments in the hull and as many as nine masts.”

    The nine-mast detail is not securely established alongside that conservative length estimate. Edward Dreyer traces the elaborate mast-based ship classification to a much later novel and rejects its unsupported numbers. Keep the secure construction features and omit the mast count unless a stronger source supports it. This concerns the evidence, not the perfectly acceptable single “probably.” Source: [Dreyer, *Zheng He*, especially pp. 178–179](https://www.scribd.com/document/920875932/Dreyer-2007-Zheng-He-China-and-the-Oceans-in-the-Early-Ming-Dynasty-1405-1433).

15. **Reader confusion with a factual implication; high confidence.** “They also bought people.”

    The immediate example is the Lagos sale of 1444, whose captives were taken in Portuguese raids. Although the following sentence calls them “captured,” the opening encourages readers to understand this expedition as buying people from African sellers. Distinguish raiding from purchasing before moving to plantation labour. Source: [NOVA University’s account of the 1444 expedition](https://eve.fcsh.unl.pt/pt/pessoas/gil-eanes).

16. **Reader comprehension—missing explanation of the instrument.** “With a brass ring for sighting the sun and a copy of the tables, a pilot anywhere on earth could now find his latitude, his distance north or south, to within about a hundred kilometres.”

    “Brass ring” does not let a reader understand how the measurement happens. Explain that the instrument hangs vertically, its movable sight measures the sun’s angle above the horizon, and the seasonal table converts that angle into latitude. This is precisely where *style.md* asks for the device’s operation in sequence.

17. **Reader orientation—unexplained political geography.** “Because the spices and gold that Europeans wanted reached them through a long chain of foreign merchants, and a sailor from Genoa persuaded the queen of Castile that Asia lay a few weeks to the west.”

    The title and later narrative say Spain, while the opening and financial argument say Castile. The requested earlier chapters do not explain their relationship. A short identification of Castile as one of the kingdoms making up Spain would prevent readers from mistaking these for separate prospective sponsors. Ferdinand’s kingdom is also never identified.

18. **Clear break of the numerical-selection rule.** “There were twelve pieces of striped cloth, four red hoods, six hats, four strings of coral, six washbasins, a case of sugar, two barrels of oil and two of honey.”

    Eight inventory quantities overwhelm one point: the presents were embarrassingly modest. *style.md* explicitly says “One number per point” and prefers one vivid example. The washbasins already provide that example and the section’s title.
