I found the following issues, ordered by seriousness. Confidence labels concern the factual correction. No files were edited.

1. **Fact — metal tools are wrongly excluded.**  
   > “Tools and weapons were of stone, above all of obsidian, a black volcanic glass that can be chipped to an edge sharper than a steel razor.”

   Andean peoples also made copper and bronze tools and weapons. Within this chapter’s period, Sicán metalworkers produced hoe blades, digging-stick tips, awls and knives. Stone remained important, but the preceding distinction between metal ornaments and stone tools is false. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [Shimada’s account of Sicán metallurgy](https://www.academia.edu/347180/Who_were_the_Sic%C3%A1n_Their_Development_Characteristics_and_Legacies), [Metropolitan Museum bronze axe](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/315476).

2. **Fact/style — complete isolation is an untenable absolute.**  
   > “People there started from the same beginnings as people everywhere else, with stone tools, fire and a few wild plants, and they were then left alone for fifteen thousand years.”

   Norse people were in Newfoundland by 1021, and genetic evidence supports Polynesian–Native American contact around 1200. These contacts do not invalidate the argument for independent American developments, but they do invalidate fifteen thousand years of uninterrupted isolation. The opening’s “no contact with the rest of the world” needs the same distinction. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [Norse dating study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03972-8), [Polynesian contact study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2487-2).

3. **Fact — the explanation of immunity conflates different processes.**  
   > “The people of the Americas had never lived among herds of cattle, pigs and sheep, and so had never caught the diseases that pass from herds to their keepers and had no resistance to them.”

   The relevant vulnerability was lack of previous exposure to particular introduced infections, not a general absence of resistance caused by not keeping livestock. Diseases such as smallpox circulated between humans; living beside cattle was not how Europeans acquired immunity to smallpox. Disease origins, acquired immunity and the conditions that made epidemics lethal need separating. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [*Origins of Major Human Infectious Diseases*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK114494/), [*Virgin Soils Revisited*](https://www.ifch.unicamp.br/ihb/HS18-11textos/VirginSoilWMQ.pdf).

4. **Fact/style — carrying capacity is turned into an absolute limit on warfare.**  
   > “Armies marched on foot and carried their own food, which set a limit of a few days' walk on any campaign.”

   That limit applies only without replenishment. Armies could obtain supplies from settlements, tribute systems and stores. Hassig describes how Aztec provisioning along military routes supported campaigns far beyond the distance initial rations allowed. The chapter itself later describes Andean stores capable of feeding armies. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Ross Hassig, discussion of logistics and expansion](https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/13884/1/40.pdf.pdf).

5. **Contradiction with chapter 10 — wheels do not require animal power.**  
   > “As the first potters and wagon builders of the Old World had found, a wheel is no use without a strong animal in front of it.”

   [Chapter 10](chapters/10-wheel/chapter.md) explicitly says: “The wheelbarrow needs no animal at all.” It also distinguishes the stationary potter’s wheel from a cart wheel. Heavy early wagons benefited from traction animals; wheels generally did not require them. This also breaks the rule against absolutes defeated by one exception. **Confidence: high.**

6. **Fact — Mexico disappears from North America.**  
   > “No town in North America grew as large again for more than four hundred years.”

   Tenochtitlan reached perhaps 200,000 inhabitants by 1521, vastly exceeding the chapter’s Cahokia estimate. Mexico is part of North America. A comparison restricted to the region north of Mexico would need its own dates and population threshold. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Metropolitan Museum, Tenochtitlan](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/tenochtitlan).

7. **Fact/reader orientation — Inca accounting is silently assigned to earlier states.**  
   > “Knots were tied along each hanging cord, and the knots recorded numbers by position, in tens.”

   The immediately preceding states belong to about 500–1000, but this describes the familiar Inca decimal khipu system. Earlier Wari cord records differ; research has proposed grouping by fives, rather than establishing the same decimal system. Identify whose records are being described and signal any move forward in time. **Confidence: high on the chronological conflation; medium on the proposed Wari base-five interpretation.** Sources: [Urton’s study of Middle Horizon cord keeping](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/from-middle-horizon-cordkeeping-to-the-rise-of-inka-khipus-in-the-central-andes/04964C31803194A5EA0F1565617F00C9), [NIST’s explanation of Inca khipus](https://www.nist.gov/nist-museum/standardizing-empire).

8. **Fact/style — an unresolved question is presented as a universal negative.**  
   > “No Andean people ever wrote down their language.”

   Whether some khipus encoded language remains disputed. Research has identified possible phonetic and lineage-name encoding, although that does not establish a fully deciphered pre-Columbian writing system. The defensible distinction is that no pre-Columbian Andean system recording language has been securely deciphered. **Confidence: high that the absolute exceeds the evidence.** Source: [Sabine Hyland, “Writing with Twisted Cords”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/691682).

9. **Fact — the Venus accuracy figure is not securely supported.**  
   > “One of their tables predicts the appearances of Venus as morning and evening star, and it drifts from the true motion of the planet by about two hours in five hundred years.”

   The basic table uses 584 days against an average cycle of approximately 583.92 days: nearly two hours’ discrepancy **per cycle**, before corrections. Correction schemes existed, but their interpretation does not justify presenting two hours in five centuries as an established observational achievement. **Confidence: high that the stated precision needs replacing; scholarly interpretations of the corrections vary.** Sources: [study of the table and its corrections](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/6cr1s6jd), [assessment of the observational evidence](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002182869402501903).

10. **Fact — the opening event’s day count is wrong.**  
    > “It took place 1,412,846 days after the beginning.”

    The execution’s Long Count is **9.15.6.14.6**, giving  
    **9 × 144,000 + 15 × 7,200 + 6 × 360 + 14 × 20 + 6 = 1,406,446 days**.  
    This arithmetic does not depend on which accepted correlation is used to convert the date into a European calendar. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [epigraphic study recording the date](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-68942024000200055&script=sci_arttext), [Smithsonian calendar explanation](https://maya.nmai.si.edu/calendar/calendar-system).

11. **Fact/explanation — the calendar’s crucial numerical exception is omitted.**  
    > “Counting on fingers and toes, they worked in twenties where we work in tens, and each place in a number was worth twenty times the place below it.”

    In the Long Count just introduced, the places represent 1, 20, **360**, 7,200 and 144,000 days. The third place multiplies by eighteen, not twenty. Without that exception, readers cannot understand the dates the passage claims to explain. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Smithsonian, “The Calendar System”](https://maya.nmai.si.edu/calendar/calendar-system).

12. **Fact — Cahokia’s burial account uses an outdated interpretation.**  
    > “In one small mound, archaeologists found a man laid on a platform of twenty thousand shell beads arranged in the shape of a falcon.”

    Reanalysis identified a central male–female pair, undermining the familiar account centred on a solitary male “Birdman.” The reconstruction of the beads as an unequivocal falcon also requires caution. Moreover, the following account of women killed “at the same moment” should not imply that all Mound 72 burials were one funeral: the mound contains multiple burial episodes. **Confidence: high on the revised identification and multiple episodes; medium on the precise bead arrangement.** Sources: [2016 reanalysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/abs/paradigms-lost-reconfiguring-cahokias-mound-72-beaded-burial/E32140B66F1FC5234DC40D2A0479A43B), [National Park Service site documentation](https://npgallery.nps.gov/NRHP/GetAsset/a916ef19-e5fd-45db-99d5-30e77a99f742/).

13. **Fact — Maya warfare is reduced too far to capturing nobles.**  
    > “The aim of a war was seldom to take land.”

    Capturing rulers mattered, but Maya warfare also involved conquest, destruction and control over populations and resources. Evidence of Witzna’s burning in 697 challenges the older contrast between limited Classic-period warfare and destructive warfare only near the collapse. “Seldom” requires support that the chapter does not provide. **Confidence: medium-high.** Sources: [research on Witzna](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70204599), [researchers’ explanation of its implications](https://news.berkeley.edu/2019/08/05/maya-more-warlike-than-previously-thought/).

14. **Fact — Cahokia’s decline is explained with a disputed environmental story.**  
    > “The reasons were probably the familiar ones, of a crowded city that had used up the wood within reach, a run of floods and dry years, and rulers whose promises wore thin.”

    The wood-overuse hypothesis is contested; excavation found a stable ground surface where that hypothesis predicted erosion and flooding. That does not settle every environmental explanation, but it prevents presenting exhausted woodland as the probable familiar answer. The rulers’ failed promises are also conjectural. **Confidence: high that the explanation is unresolved; medium on the relative contribution of individual causes.** Source: [Rankin and colleagues, testing the ecocide narrative](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/gea.21848).

15. **Fact/contradiction — the transport claim forgets the llama.**  
    > “Every sack of maize, every block of building stone and every load of firewood went on somebody's back, held by a strap across the forehead, or else in a canoe.”

    Both chapter 5 and chapter 10 have already introduced llama pack transport; chapter 36 describes it later too. Across the Americas, dogs also pulled loads on travois. Restricting this sentence to its intended Mesoamerican setting would remove the most obvious contradiction; “every” still overstates the evidence. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [chapter 5](chapters/05-farming/chapter.md), [Alberta’s account of prehistoric animal transport](https://history.alberta.ca/energyheritage/energy/human-and-animal-power/animal-power-in-prehistoric-times/animal-power-in-prehistoric-alberta.aspx).

16. **Fact/style — iron smelting has a documented exception within the period.**  
    > “Iron was never smelted anywhere in the hemisphere.”

    Norse settlers smelted bog iron at L’Anse aux Meadows around the beginning of the second millennium. The intended statement about Indigenous American metallurgy needs to say that explicitly. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Parks Canada’s archaeological designation](https://www.pc.gc.ca/apps/dfhd/page_nhs_eng.aspx?i=58759&id=236).

17. **Fact — a maize staple does not inevitably produce deficiency disease.**  
    > “Maize eaten as the main food, day after day, leads to a wasting disease, because the body cannot get at one of the vitamins locked in the grain.”

    Pellagra results from inadequate available niacin and/or tryptophan. Untreated maize dependence creates a risk when the rest of the diet does not compensate; maize as the main food is not sufficient by itself. Beans, mentioned immediately beforehand, can help provide available niacin. Naming pellagra and the missing vitamin would also make the explanation easier to follow. **Confidence: high.** Source: [WHO’s account of pellagra and its prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/immigrant-refugee-health/media/pdfs/pellagra-who.pdf).

18. **Fact — Cahokia’s mounds are incorrectly given a single form and function.**  
    > “Around it stood more than a hundred smaller mounds, with the wooden houses of chiefs and the temples of their ancestors on their flat tops.”

    Cahokia had platform, conical and ridgetop mounds. Some supported buildings; others served burial purposes. They were not all flat-topped platforms bearing houses or temples. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site’s explanation of mound types](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/mound-types-cahokia-mounds-state-historic-site/5wGDkhkLfODPTA?hl=en).

19. **Reader orientation — the Andean states never acquire usable identities.**  
    > “Between about 500 and 1000, one of them was centred on a city of stone temples beside a lake nearly four thousand metres above the sea.”

    This and “Another built roads…” introduce two anonymous states, then refer back to “Those states” while moving into accounting. A reader cannot readily distinguish their locations or know whose achievements belong to whom. Naming Tiwanaku beside Lake Titicaca and Wari in Peru would serve the style guide’s exception for names needed to follow the story.

20. **Style — an explicit outline replaces narrative progression.**  
    > “What caused it has been argued over for a century, and three kinds of evidence have accumulated.”

    This announces the explanatory structure instead of giving the explanation. The ensuing “The first…”, “The second…” and “The third…” make the passage read like the point-by-point case that `style.md` discourages. The later fourth factor also makes the announced three-part structure awkward.

21. **Style — a surprise is announced before being delivered.**  
    > “There was one more trick, and it was not obvious.”

    This is a direct instance of the prohibited signpost sentence. The maize-processing discovery can begin without announcing its ingenuity first.

I found no clear hedging stacks, explicit mentions of the chapter itself, or prohibited body cross-references. Beyond the transport and wheel points above, I found no clear contradiction with chapters 5, 10 or 30.
