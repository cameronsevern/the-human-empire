1. **Factual error; absolute — high confidence.** “Greek was a harder matter, since nobody in western Europe could read it.”

   Greek knowledge was scarce among Latin scholars, not absent. William of Moerbeke translated numerous Greek works into Latin in the thirteenth century; Robert Grosseteste was another earlier Greek scholar. Replace “nobody” with a statement about its rarity. [Dictionary of Scientific Biography](https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/dictionaries-thesauruses-pictures-and-press-releases/moerbeke-william).

2. **Misleading historical premise; tension with chapter 36 — high confidence.** “For a thousand years, Europeans had lived among Roman bridges, aqueducts and domes that they could not have built themselves, and had taken it for granted that the great age was in the past.”

   This makes a particular difficulty—matching an exceptionally large ancient dome—into a millennium of general engineering incapacity and universal pessimism. Chapter 36’s “Walls of glass” already describes medieval builders inventing ambitious structural solutions and competing to build higher. Keep the comparison specific to the Pantheon’s span; the earlier chapter does not support this sweeping account of medieval ability or attitudes. [Chapter 36](chapters/36-castles/chapter.md).

3. **Factual misstatement; inconsistency with chapter 45 — high confidence.** “To pay for it, the popes sent salesmen north into Germany with certificates of forgiveness, printed by the thousand.”

   Indulgences purported to remit punishment remaining after sins had been forgiven, rather than sell forgiveness itself. Chapter 45 explicitly explains that distinction before describing abuses in their sale. “Certificates promising relief from purgatory” would preserve the ending without confusing the doctrine with the sales pitch. [Catholic Church’s definition](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_two/section_two/chapter_two/article_4/x_indulgences.index.html); [chapter 45](chapters/45-reformation/chapter.md).

4. **Wrong commissioning history — high confidence.** “In 1501 the government of Florence, which had lately thrown the Medici out, commissioned a statue from a sculptor of twenty-six named Michelangelo.”

   The cathedral’s overseers commissioned *David* for a cathedral buttress. Its installation outside the government palace was decided in 1504. The distinction matters because the paragraph presents the statue as a political commission from its inception, merging its original purpose with its subsequent civic use. [Galleria dell’Accademia](https://www.galleriaaccademiafirenze.it/en/artworks/david-michelangelo/).

5. **Doubtful journey — high confidence about the chronology; moderate confidence that the sentence is simply wrong.** “Michelangelo, in old age, designed its dome, and went to Florence to study Brunelleschi’s before he did.”

   Michelangelo left Florence permanently in 1534, before taking responsibility for St Peter’s in 1547. Brunelleschi’s dome influenced his design, but an old-age research visit to Florence does not fit that chronology. Retain the influence; remove the journey unless a specific earlier visit is intended and documented. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2024-02/Michelangelo-the-last-decades.pdf); [Florence cathedral’s account of the influence](https://duomo.firenze.it/en/opera-magazine/post/11998/michelangelo-e-l-opera-di-santa-maria-del-fiore-un-legame-scolpito-nel-tempo).

6. **Unsupported anatomical superlative — high confidence.** “He cut up about thirty corpses and drew the valves of the heart and a child in the womb more accurately than any medical book of the next two centuries.”

   The dissection count is reasonable, but the blanket comparison is not. His celebrated womb drawing includes a cow’s multiple placenta, incorrectly transferred to human anatomy. Some discoveries were extraordinarily advanced; that does not establish superior accuracy across these subjects for two centuries. Replace the comparison with one specific, verified achievement. [Royal Collection Trust](https://www.rct.uk/collection/stories/leonardo-in-the-royal-collection/leonardos-study-of-anatomy).

7. **False generalisation about manuscript survival — high confidence.** “The works of the Roman writers survived, where they survived at all, in single copies made by monks hundreds of years before and forgotten on monastery shelves.”

   Some recoveries depended on exceptionally rare manuscripts; Roman literature as a whole did not survive this way. Vitruvius—the architectural author invoked immediately afterwards—survived in numerous medieval manuscripts and was known before the Renaissance. Make the sentence about particular lost works, not all Roman writers. [ICCROM, *A History of Architectural Conservation*](https://www.iccrom.org/sites/default/files/ICCROM_05_HistoryofConservation01_en.pdf).

8. **Overclaim about what the Renaissance originated; tension with chapter 36 — high confidence.** “It produced paintings that look like windows, a new way of reading old books, and the idea that a person should be measured by talent and not by birth.”

   The Renaissance gave individual achievement renewed prominence; it did not originate the idea of judging ability rather than ancestry. Chapter 36 already contrasts hereditary European power with Chinese appointment by examination. “Produced … the idea” turns a defensible claim about emphasis into an untenable claim of invention. [Chapter 36](chapters/36-castles/chapter.md).

9. **Misleading publication history; absolute — high confidence.** “The notebooks were scattered after his death and were not printed until the 1800s, so the anatomists and engineers who came after him had to find everything out again.”

   Leonardo’s anatomical papers largely remained unpublished until the nineteenth century, but material compiled from his writings appeared as the *Treatise on Painting* in 1651 and circulated in manuscripts earlier. Distinguish the anatomical research from his writings generally. “Everything” also overstates both his discoveries and their complete isolation. [University of Oklahoma’s 1651 edition](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/treatise-painting.html); [Royal Collection Trust on the anatomical papers](https://www.rct.uk/collection/stories/leonardo-in-the-royal-collection/leonardos-study-of-anatomy).

10. **False medieval/Renaissance divide — high confidence.** “A painter of the Middle Ages was not trying to show what the eye sees.”

    The following gold-background example describes an important convention, not every medieval painter’s intention. Giotto, Duccio and Cavallini were already exploring naturalism, bodily volume and convincing space before the chapter’s starting date. Specify a kind of medieval religious painting rather than treating naturalistic observation as absent until Brunelleschi. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/italian-painting-of-the-later-middle-ages).

11. **Incorrect explanation of perspective — high confidence.** “Parallel lines that run away from the viewer, such as the edges of a road, appear to draw together, and they meet at a single point on the horizon directly opposite the eye.”

    That location applies to horizontal lines running straight away from the viewer, perpendicular to the picture plane. Other horizontal directions have other vanishing points along the horizon. Simply specify a straight road viewed directly along its length; otherwise the reader learns a false general rule. [University of Utah, *Mathematics of Perspective Drawing*](https://www.math.utah.edu/~treiberg/Perspect/Perspect.htm).

12. **Wrong title at the stated date — high confidence.** “About 1482 a Florentine painter of thirty wrote to the duke of Milan to ask for a job.”

    Leonardo addressed Ludovico Sforza, then Milan’s effective ruler and guardian of the reigning duke, Gian Galeazzo. Calling him “the ruler of Milan” fixes the error without introducing additional names. [Museo Galileo](https://brunelleschi.imss.fi.it/itineraries/itinerary/EarlyMaturityMilan.html).

13. **Break of the rule against exception-fragile absolutes — high confidence.** “It was the first history of art, and it described what had happened in Italy as a rebirth.”

    Vasari’s *Lives* was foundational, but “the first history of art” ignores earlier writing, including Ghiberti’s *Commentaries*, composed about a century before. Describe the book’s influence or its biographical scope instead of claiming an unqualified first. [Metropolitan Museum of Art symposium discussing Ghiberti’s earlier art history](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Age_of_Spirituality_A_Symposium.pdf).

14. **Reader loses the political chronology.** “In 1501 the government of Florence, which had lately thrown the Medici out, commissioned a statue from a sculptor of twenty-six named Michelangelo.”

    Apart from the commissioning error above, this is the reader’s first notice that Medici rule ended. The previous political account leaves Cosimo securely controlling Florence. One brief bridge explaining the family’s expulsion would make the republican symbolism—and the later Medici return in Machiavelli’s story—much easier to follow.

15. **Reader loses the technical explanation.** “A completed ring holds itself up.”

    This supplies the crucial conclusion without explaining it. Earlier, the chapter says an unfinished dome cannot stand; now successive rings supposedly can. Explain how the bricks in a closed course restrain one another, and distinguish that from how the herringbone pattern stabilises work before closure. As written, the reader can repeat the claim but cannot explain the mechanism to a friend, as `style.md` requires.

16. **Reader loses the chronology at the ending.** “Rome had one more thing to build.”

    This follows the sack of 1527 but introduces a project begun in 1506. The next sentence supplies the date, yet “one more thing” initially makes the building sound like Rome’s next undertaking after the sack. Signal the backward step directly. This is a chronology problem, not an objection to the confident ending.
