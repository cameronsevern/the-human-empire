## Factual flags

1. **Minor — new.** “A coin is worth having only if people trust the gold in it, and the florin was trusted.”

   Coins need not contain gold. Florence itself issued silver money alongside its gold florins: the Fitzwilliam holds a Florentine silver grosso dated 1406. The accurate point concerns confidence in **the florin’s gold content**, not a necessary condition for all coin value. [Fitzwilliam Museum, Florentine grosso](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/306790).

2. **Major — new; also contradicts the chapter’s map caption.** “Each was its own small state, and they spent their strength on fighting one another.”

   In the approaching-1494 context, Italian cities were not each independent states. Verona had belonged to Venice since 1405; the chapter itself says Genoa was under Milan in 1494. The correct distinction is between competing **Italian states**, several of which controlled numerous cities. [UNESCO, City of Verona](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/797).

3. **Major — retained and strengthened.** “People of the Middle Ages had not felt it. They had thought of the Romans as more or less their own contemporaries, and painters showed Julius Caesar dressed as a knight.”

   Medieval people could recognise Roman antiquity as a distinct, distant past. Bede’s history explicitly dates Roman rulers and describes the end of Roman government in Britain centuries before his own time. Anachronistic clothing in pictures does not establish ignorance of historical distance. Petrarch’s distinctive interpretation of antiquity can stand; the denial of earlier historical awareness cannot. [Bede, *Ecclesiastical History*, Book I, especially chapter 11](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/bede-book1.asp).

## Clarity flags

- **Signposting:** “Brunelleschi's answer lay in the pattern.” This announces an answer immediately before supplying it, contrary to the explicit rule against signpost sentences.

- **Signposting and overstatement:** “That last point shaped everything that followed.” It announces the next explanation and makes an unnecessary “everything” claim.

- **Signposting:** “The letters sound like a game, but something serious lay behind them.” This announces significance rather than conveying it.

- **Chapter self-reference:** “His place in the story is therefore a different one.” This discusses Leonardo’s role in the author’s narrative. “Different” also leaves the reader to reconstruct the comparison.

- **Padding:** “Petrarch copied them out by hand, and as he copied he read.” Reading while copying adds no useful step. The next sentence repeats “read”.

- **Repeated explanation:** “A dome of that width had been built once already, and it was still standing.” The paragraph has already established the Pantheon’s matching width, survival and significance.

- **Repeated explanation:** “After that, the ring looked after itself.” The following sentence immediately explains the same point more concretely.

- **Overbroad attribution:** “The man who showed how to paint a view was, once again, Brunelleschi.” “Paint a view” is much broader than demonstrating geometrically constructed linear perspective.

- **Confusing mechanism:** “Perspective told a painter where a figure should stand and how large it should be.” Perspective determines apparent size and position once the viewpoint and scene are chosen; it does not choose the composition.

- **Unclear reversal:** “A century later the positions were reversed.” Artists and patrons did not literally exchange positions. The following sentence already makes the intended increase in bargaining power clear.

- **Exception-fragile absolute:** “It became the model for every later history of art, and it described what had happened in Italy as a rebirth.” “Every later history” is unnecessary and indefensible as a universal claim.
