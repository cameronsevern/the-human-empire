# 43. What was the Renaissance?

*Florence and the cities of Italy, from about 1340 to 1527*

The Renaissance was a movement that began in the trading cities of northern Italy, among people who had decided that the ancient Greeks and Romans had done most things better than anyone since, and who set out to equal them. It lasted about two hundred years. It produced paintings that look like windows, a new way of reading old books, and a cult of individual talent. The word is French for "rebirth", and it was coined long afterwards.

In 1418 the cathedral of Florence had a hole in its roof about forty-four metres across.

The city had been building its great church for a century. It was to be the largest in the world, and in 1367 a committee had approved the final design. Where the arms of the church met, there would be an eight-sided dome, wider than any that had been built since the Roman Empire and starting higher above the ground than any dome anywhere.

Nobody on the committee knew how to build it. They approved it anyway, and every year afterwards the cathedral's builders swore on the Bible to follow the model. The walls duly rose. By 1418 the eight-sided base stood ready, open to the rain, as high as a fifteen-storey building.

The trouble was the usual method. An arch or a dome of stone will not stand until the last piece is in place, and so builders held up the unfinished work on a temporary frame of timber and took the frame away at the end. A frame to fill this hole would have needed a forest of trees larger than any that grew in Tuscany, and no one could work out how to make timber span that distance without collapsing under its own weight.

In 1418 the city announced a contest for a solution, with a prize of two hundred gold coins. The winner was a goldsmith named Filippo Brunelleschi, a maker of clockwork, who had never put up a building. He proposed to build the dome with no frame at all.

## Four million bricks

Brunelleschi had prepared himself in an unusual way. Some years earlier, after failing to win another contest outright, he had left Florence and gone to Rome with a friend who was a sculptor. The story was told by a man who knew him in old age.

Rome at that time was a small town of perhaps thirty thousand people, camped in the wreckage of a city built for a million. Cattle grazed in the Forum. The locals burned ancient marble to make lime for mortar. The two Florentines spent their days climbing over the ruins with measuring rods, digging around the bases of columns and sketching vaults. The Romans who watched them assumed that they were hunting for buried treasure.

One Roman building still had its roof, a temple called the Pantheon, which is covered by a concrete dome almost exactly as wide as the hole in Florence. It had stood for thirteen hundred years. The recipe for Roman concrete had been lost, so Brunelleschi could not copy it. What he took from Rome was the knowledge that the thing could be done.

His own dome was built of brick, and it was really two domes, a thick one inside and a thin one outside, with a staircase between them. This made it far lighter than a solid shell. To stop it from bursting outwards under its own weight, he buried great chains of stone and iron in the brickwork at intervals, running all the way round, like the hoops on a barrel.

The cleverest part was the laying of the bricks. As the walls curved inwards, each new ring of wet brickwork leaned further over empty air, with nothing underneath to stop it from sliding off. Brunelleschi had the masons lay most bricks flat, and at regular intervals set a brick on end, so that it stuck up through several layers like a bookend. Those upright bricks ran in spirals up the dome, and they locked each short stretch of new work in place until the ring was complete. Once a ring is closed it cannot fall, for the same reason that the stones of an arch cannot: every brick is trying to slide inwards, and its neighbours on either side are in the way.

He invented the machinery as well. To lift the materials he designed a hoist driven by a pair of oxen walking in a circle, with a gear that could be shifted to raise or lower the load, so that the animals never had to be unhitched and turned round.

The work took sixteen years and four million bricks. It was finished in 1436, and nobody was killed by a collapse. It is still the largest brick dome ever built.

To the people of Florence the dome meant more than a roof. The builders of the great cathedrals had done things no Roman ever tried, but nobody in a thousand years had roofed a space as wide as the Pantheon's, and educated Italians took it for granted that the greatest age was in the past. Now a living man from their own city had matched the ancients, and in one respect had beaten them.

## A city of bookkeepers

That it happened in Florence was no accident. Northern Italy in those centuries was the richest and most crowded corner of Europe, and it was organised in a peculiar way.

Most of Europe was a countryside of lords and peasants with a few small towns. Italy, north of Rome, was a country of cities. Venice, Genoa and Milan each had more people than London, and Florence before the plague had been larger still. Their merchants handled the trade between Europe and the East, and in the cities' long quarrel with emperors and popes they had come out on top. Most of these cities had no king or duke at first. They were governed by councils drawn from their own guilds, and each ruled the villages around it as a small independent state.

Florence had two trades. The first was woollen cloth, which it bought rough from the north, finished and dyed, and sold at a high price all over the Mediterranean. The second was money. In 1252 the city began to strike a small gold coin, the florin, much of whose gold had crossed the Sahara from West Africa. It was so reliable that merchants from England to Egypt used it for their accounts.

Around the coin grew banks. A Florentine bank had partners in a dozen cities, and a merchant could pay money into its office in London and collect it from its office in Rome. Running such a business required clerks by the hundred, and so Florence taught its children. About 1338 a local chronicler reckoned that between eight and ten thousand boys and girls in the city were learning to read, in a population of ninety thousand, and that more than a thousand boys were at special schools learning arithmetic with the Indian numerals.

Ten years after he wrote, the plague killed half the city. It recovered, and it remained what it had been: a place governed by cloth merchants and bankers, where nearly every man of property could read, write and calculate, and where family fortunes were made in a generation. Such men had no ancestors to boast about. They needed some other way to show what they were worth.

The richest of them were the Medici. Their bank, founded in 1397, became the largest in Europe and handled the money of the popes. From 1434 the head of the family, Cosimo, ran Florence as well. He held no title and took care to dress and live like any other citizen, and nothing was decided without him.

## Letters to a dead man

The fashion for the ancients had started a century before the dome, with a poet.

Francesco Petrarch was the son of a Florentine notary, and from boyhood he loved the Latin of Cicero, the great orator of the Roman republic. The Latin of his own day, the working language of the church and the universities, seemed to him a clumsy, barbarous thing by comparison. In 1345, in the library of the cathedral at Verona, he found a manuscript that nobody had read for centuries. It contained hundreds of Cicero's private letters.

Petrarch copied them out by hand. They upset him, because the philosopher he worshipped turned out to be a vain, changeable politician. He responded by writing Cicero a letter, to tell him so. He went on to write to other ancient authors as well, addressing them as friends who happened to be dead.

He had hit on a new way of seeing the past. People of the Middle Ages had thought of the Romans as more or less their own contemporaries, and painters showed Julius Caesar dressed as a knight. Petrarch saw that the Romans had lived in a different world, that it had ended, and that the centuries between it and himself were a long decline. He called those centuries dark. The idea of a middle age between the ancients and the moderns grew out of that.

His followers became book hunters. Many works of the Roman writers survived in a single copy or two, made by monks hundreds of years before and forgotten on a monastery shelf. In 1417 a papal secretary on a visit to Germany found, in one such library, a long Roman poem arguing that the world is made of atoms. Others found lost speeches and histories, and better copies of the one ancient handbook of architecture.

Greek was a harder matter, since hardly anybody in western Europe could read it. Petrarch owned a manuscript of Homer and could do nothing with it except hold it. In 1397 the city of Florence hired a scholar from Constantinople to come and teach the language, and after that the flow did not stop. As the Turks closed in on Constantinople, Greek scholars moved to Italy and brought their libraries. Cosimo de' Medici gave a young scholar a house, a salary and the manuscripts, and set him to put the whole of Plato into Latin.

These scholars called their subjects the humanities: grammar, public speaking, poetry, history and the question of how to live. They believed that such studies made a better citizen than the logic and theology of the universities. They also developed a sharp new tool. A man who has read enough Latin of every period can tell when a piece was written, in the way that a modern reader can tell a letter of 1800 from an email. In 1440 one of them turned that skill on the document by which the emperor Constantine was supposed to have given the popes the rule of western Europe. He showed that its Latin belonged to a time centuries after Constantine's death. The pope's most famous title deed was a fake.

## A window in the wall

The painters wanted to equal the ancients too, and they had a difficulty that the scholars did not. Hardly any ancient painting had survived to copy. They copied nature instead.

Most painters of the Middle Ages were not trying to show what the eye sees. An altar painter set his holy figures against a flat background of gold, and made each as large as its importance required, so that a saint might stand twice as tall as the king kneeling beside him. The picture was a sign pointing at heaven.

A few years before he won the contest, Brunelleschi made a small painting of the eight-sided church that faces Florence's cathedral, and drilled a hole through the middle of it. He stood a viewer in the cathedral doorway, looking through the hole from the back of the panel, and held up a mirror in front. The viewer saw the painting reflected. When the mirror was taken away, he saw the real building in exactly the same place, and could hardly tell the difference.

Brunelleschi had worked out the geometry of sight. The edges of a straight road running directly away from the viewer appear to draw together, and they meet at a single point on the horizon, straight in front of the eye. Once a painter has fixed that point, he can calculate how small every object should be according to its distance. A scholar friend wrote the method down in 1435, and told painters to treat the picture as an open window through which the scene is viewed.

The first painter to use it fully was a young man called Masaccio. About 1427 he painted on the wall of a Florentine church a crucifixion set inside a chapel with a curved ceiling. The chapel is not there. The wall is flat. Nevertheless the ceiling recedes so convincingly that people who saw it thought that a hole had been knocked through the wall.

The same hunger for the real thing sent artists to the mortuary. To carve or paint a body that looked alive, they needed to know what lay under the skin, and the best of them cut up corpses to find out.

As their skill grew, so did their standing. A painter in 1400 was a tradesman who kept a shop and belonged, in Florence, to the guild of the doctors and druggists, because he bought his colours from them. His contract fixed the size of the picture and the quality of the gold and the blue. A century later, popes and kings were competing for the services of a few famous men and putting up with their tempers. In 1550 one painter published a thick book of the lives of the great artists, as though they were saints or generals. It became the model for every later history of art, and it described what had happened in Italy as a rebirth.

## The banker's conscience

All of this cost a great deal, and the people who paid had reasons of their own.

The first was fear of hell. The church taught that lending money at interest was a sin, and banking was lending at interest, however carefully it was disguised in the paperwork. Cosimo de' Medici asked the pope how he could make amends, and was advised to rebuild a monastery. He did, and kept a cell in it for his own prayers. His grandson later added up what the family had spent on buildings, charity and taxes between 1434 and 1471. It came to more than six hundred thousand florins, at a time when a skilled craftsman earned a few dozen florins a year. He thought the money well spent.

The second reason was rivalry. Every guild, every great family and every city wanted a finer chapel, a larger palace or a more famous artist than the next, and the artists, who knew it, tried to outdo each other. The third was politics. A work of art could say things about its owner that he could not decently say himself.

In 1494 the Florentines lost patience with Cosimo's great-grandson, drove the Medici out and governed themselves again. Seven years later the overseers of the cathedral commissioned a statue from a sculptor of twenty-six named Michelangelo. He carved a naked young man, more than five metres tall, out of a single block of marble: David, the shepherd boy who killed a giant. It had been meant for the cathedral roof. When the city's leaders saw it, they set it up in front of the town hall instead, as the image of a small republic that was not afraid of anyone.

A few years afterwards the pope summoned Michelangelo to Rome and ordered him to paint the ceiling of the popes' own chapel. Michelangelo protested that he was a sculptor and not a painter. He then spent four years on a scaffold with his head tipped back, covering a ceiling forty metres long with more than three hundred figures. He wrote a comic poem about what the work had done to his neck.

## Ten kinds of weapon

About 1482 a Florentine painter of thirty wrote to the ruler of Milan to ask for a job. The letter lists his qualifications in ten numbered paragraphs.

He can build light, strong bridges that can be carried with an army. He can drain the moat of a castle under siege. He knows how to destroy any fortress that is not founded on rock. He can make cannon, mortars and catapults of new designs, covered wagons that guns cannot hurt, tunnels dug in silence, and ships that resist fire. In time of peace, he adds at the end, he is as good an architect as any man, and he can carve in marble, bronze or clay, and paint as well as anyone.

The writer was Leonardo da Vinci. He knew his customer. The rulers of Italy were at war with each other most of the time, the cannon was changing warfare under their feet, and what a prince wanted from a clever man was engineering. Leonardo got the job. In Milan he designed stage machinery and canal locks, and painted on a monastery wall a Last Supper that began to flake within his lifetime, because he had experimented with the paint.

He finished very little. About twenty paintings by him survive. What he left instead was several thousand pages of notebooks, written from right to left in mirror writing, on every subject that crossed his mind: the flight of birds, the flow of water round an obstacle, the muscles of the shoulder, a machine for grinding lenses, the reason the sky is blue. By his own count he cut up more than thirty corpses, and he worked out how the valves of the heart close, by the swirling of the blood behind them, which doctors did not confirm until the twentieth century.

He published none of it. The notebooks were scattered after his death, and the anatomy was not printed until the 1800s, so the anatomists who came after him had to find it all out again. What he shows is what the age admired. Its hero was the man who could do everything, and it saw no boundary between a painting and a siege engine. Both were problems to be solved by looking hard at the world.

## Things as they are

Italy's cities were rich, brilliant and unable to combine. In 1494 that bill came due. The king of France crossed the Alps with an army and a train of bronze cannon mounted on wheeled carriages. Fortresses that had been expected to hold out for months fell to him in hours. For the next sixty years, France and Spain fought their wars up and down Italy, and the Italian states were reduced to choosing sides.

One Florentine watched it from close up. Niccolò Machiavelli spent fourteen years as a senior official of the city's republic, riding on missions to kings and warlords. In 1512 the Medici returned with a Spanish army. Machiavelli was dismissed, and a few months later he was arrested on suspicion of plotting and hung by his wrists from a rope. When he was released, he went to his farm and wrote a short book of advice for rulers.

For centuries, books of that kind had told princes to be just, merciful and pious. Machiavelli wrote about what he had seen to work. A ruler who tries to be good all the time, he said, will come to ruin among so many who are not good. It is best to be both loved and feared, but if a ruler cannot have both, it is much safer to be feared. The book shocked Europe, and it has been read ever since, because it applied to politics the rule that the painters had applied to the body. It described the thing as it is.

In 1527 an army belonging to the king of Spain, who was also emperor in Germany, unpaid and out of control, broke into Rome and looted it for months. The great age of the Italian cities was over. By then, however, the movement had crossed the Alps in the luggage of scholars, artists and invading soldiers, and the rest of Europe was learning Greek and drawing in perspective.

Rome was left with a building site. Back in 1506 the pope had decided to pull down the church of Saint Peter, which had stood since the time of Constantine, and to replace it with the largest church in the world. Michelangelo, in old age, designed its dome, with Brunelleschi's in mind. The building took a hundred and twenty years. To pay for it, the popes sent salesmen north into Germany with printed certificates that promised the buyer a shorter stay in purgatory.
