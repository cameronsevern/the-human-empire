# 45. Why did Japan close itself off?

*Japan and Korea, from 1543 to about 1700*

It never closed completely. The family that ended a century of civil war kept a few guarded doors open and shut the rest, because foreign ships brought two things that threatened its hold on the country: a religion whose followers obeyed a distant pope, and trading profits that could make a rival lord strong.

In the autumn of 1543, a storm drove a Chinese trading ship onto the shore of Tanegashima, a small island off the southern tip of Japan. Among the people on board were two or three Portuguese merchants, the first Europeans to set foot in the country. The islanders noticed their long noses and the odd way they ate with their fingers. They noticed above all the objects that two of them carried.

Each was an iron tube about a metre long, fixed to a wooden stock. The owner poured in a black powder and a small lead ball, touched a smouldering cord to a hole near the back, and with a flash and a bang knocked down a duck at a distance that astonished the onlookers. The lord of the island was sixteen years old. He watched the demonstration and bought both guns on the spot, for a very large sum.

He then handed one of them to his swordsmith and told him to make more. The smith managed everything except the screw that closed the back end of the barrel, a thing no Japanese craftsman had seen. According to the island's tradition he solved that problem the following year, when another Portuguese ship came by with a metalworker aboard. Within a few months the island had dozens of guns, and merchants from the mainland were arriving to buy them and learn the method.

Japan took up the gun faster than any country had before. Thirty years after that storm, Japanese armies were putting thousands of gunners into a single battle, and the country probably owned more firearms than any kingdom in Europe. A hundred years after it, Japan's rulers had expelled the Portuguese and had no further use for armies of gunners.

## The country at war

The Portuguese had landed in a country that had been fighting itself for three generations. Japan had an emperor, who lived in the old capital of Kyoto and was honoured by everyone and obeyed by no one. It had a supreme general, the shogun, who was supposed to rule in the emperor's name and had lost control of everything outside the capital. Real power lay with about two hundred regional lords. Each held a castle and a stretch of rice land, kept an army of sword-carrying retainers called samurai, and fought his neighbours whenever he saw a chance.

In this sort of war, anything that gave an edge was copied at once. Japanese swordsmiths were among the finest metalworkers in the world, and they were soon making guns as good as the Portuguese originals. They also improved them. They added a lacquered cover to keep the rain off the burning cord, which mattered in a wet country.

The gun changed who could fight. A mounted samurai spent his whole life learning to use the bow and the sword. A peasant could be taught to load and fire in a few weeks, and a line of peasants with guns could kill the samurai before he got close.

The lord who saw this most clearly was Oda Nobunaga, a ruthless commander from the centre of the country. In 1575, at a place called Nagashino, he faced the most feared cavalry in Japan. He put his gunners, a thousand or more of them, behind fences of wooden stakes, with a river in front, and waited. The horsemen charged the fences again and again and were shot down from behind them. It was the same method that the Ottomans had used against the horsemen of Iran sixty years earlier, discovered independently at the other end of Asia.

## Pound, knead and eat

Three men, one after another, used the new kind of army to put Japan back together. The Japanese have a saying about them. Nobunaga pounded the rice, his successor kneaded the dough, and the third man sat down and ate the cake.

Nobunaga conquered the centre of the country and was then betrayed by one of his own generals, who trapped him in a temple in 1582. His place was taken by the ablest of his commanders, Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Hideyoshi was a small, ugly, brilliant man who had been born a peasant and had started his career carrying Nobunaga's sandals. By 1590, through war and bargaining, he had brought every lord in Japan under his authority.

Having climbed from the bottom, Hideyoshi made sure that nobody could follow him. In 1588 he ordered all farmers in the country to hand over their swords, spears, bows and guns. He announced that the metal would be melted down to make nails and bolts for a great statue of the Buddha, so that the farmers would be blessed in this life and the next. The real purpose was plain enough. The order was not carried out everywhere, and many villagers kept a gun for hunting. From then on, however, bearing arms in war was the business of the samurai, and a villager could no longer become a soldier, as Hideyoshi himself had done.

He also sent surveyors through every province to measure the fields and work out how much rice each could grow. Every village was given a figure, and every lord's rank was fixed by the total for his lands. For the first time the ruler of Japan knew what the country was worth, down to the last valley.

He now commanded several hundred thousand experienced soldiers and had no more enemies in Japan for them to fight. He had also been saying for years that he would one day conquer China. In 1591 he informed his lords that the time had come, and that he would get there through Korea.

## Learned in a morning

Korea was a different kind of country from Japan, and in 1592 it had been largely at peace for two hundred years.

It was a kingdom governed on the principles of Confucius, by officials who earned their posts in written examinations, as in China. The men who ran it were scholars, and they regarded soldiering as a low trade. The king sent tribute to the emperor in Beijing, whom he acknowledged as his superior, and in return China left Korea to manage its own affairs.

The kingdom had recently produced one of the cleverest inventions in the history of writing. Koreans had always written with Chinese characters, which fit the Korean language badly and take years to learn, so that only the sons of gentlemen could read. In the 1440s a king named Sejong decided that this was wrong. He complained that ordinary people who had something to say could not put it into writing, and that innocent men were punished because they could not read the charges against them.

Sejong and his scholars therefore designed a new alphabet from nothing. It had twenty-eight letters. The shapes of the consonants were based on the positions of the tongue and lips when making the sound, and the letters were stacked into neat square blocks, one block to a syllable. The king's announcement claimed that a clever man could learn the system before the morning was over, and that even a slow one could do it in ten days. Many of the scholars at his court hated it. They thought that a script anyone could learn was vulgar, and for centuries the alphabet was used mainly by women and by people of no rank. It is now the writing of all Koreans.

This orderly kingdom of scholars was no match for what landed on its south coast in May 1592. Hideyoshi sent a hundred and fifty thousand men, veterans of twenty years of civil war, a quarter of them carrying guns. The Korean army had almost no firearms of that kind. The invaders took the capital, Seoul, in three weeks. The king fled north to the Chinese border, and by midsummer the Japanese held most of the peninsula.

## Thirteen ships

An army of that size, far from home, has to be supplied by sea, and the Korean navy was commanded by a man of unusual ability. Yi Sun-sin was an officer who had failed his first military examination by falling off a horse, and had been stuck for years in minor posts because he would not flatter his superiors.

Korean warships were heavier than Japanese ones and carried cannon, while the Japanese preferred to pull alongside an enemy and board. Yi had also built a few vessels of a special design. The whole top deck was covered by a curved roof studded with iron spikes, so that the ship could not be boarded, and guns fired through ports on every side. His sailors called them turtle ships. With these and his ordinary warships, Yi sank Japanese transport fleets one after another through the summer of 1592. At one battle he pretended to retreat, drew the pursuing fleet into open water, and wrapped his own ships around it in a half-circle. Of seventy-three Japanese ships, fourteen got away.

The supplies and reinforcements meant for the march on China never arrived. At the same time, the Chinese emperor sent an army across the border to support his loyal neighbour, and Korean monks and farmers were ambushing Japanese columns in the hills. The invaders fell back to a ring of forts on the south coast.

Hideyoshi tried again in 1597. By then Yi's rivals at court had arranged for him to be arrested, tortured and reduced to the rank of a common soldier. His replacement lost almost the entire fleet in one night. Yi was hurriedly given back his command and found that it consisted of thirteen ships. He took them to a narrow strait where the tide runs like a river, waited until more than a hundred Japanese vessels had been swept into it, and attacked when the current turned against them. He did not lose a ship.

Hideyoshi died the following year, and his generals were glad to go home. Yi was killed by a bullet in the last battle of the war, attacking the retreating fleet. He told his nephew to hide his death until the fighting was over.

The war had achieved nothing for Japan and had wrecked Korea. The country lost a large part of its people and most of its farmland went out of use for years. Japanese soldiers had been paid by the number of enemies they killed. Since heads were too bulky to ship, they cut off noses and ears, packed them in barrels of salt, and sent them home to be counted. There is a grassy mound in Kyoto where tens of thousands of them are buried. The armies also carried off some fifty thousand Korean captives, among them whole villages of potters, whose descendants founded the porcelain industry for which Japan later became famous.

## The pilot's boast

The Portuguese who followed the first merchants to Japan had brought priests with them. Jesuit missionaries arrived in 1549, and they did remarkably well. Some of the southern lords were baptised and ordered their subjects to follow, partly from conviction and partly because the Portuguese ships, with their Chinese silk and their guns, preferred to call at Christian harbours. By 1600 there were about three hundred thousand Japanese Christians, a larger share of the population than there is today.

Hideyoshi had been friendly to the priests at first. In 1587, after a tour of the Christian south, he abruptly ordered them out of the country, complaining that their converts were wrecking temples and that a lord had handed them the port of Nagasaki. He did not enforce the order, since he wanted the trade. What troubled him was the thought of where their loyalty lay. A samurai was supposed to obey his lord without limit, and here was a faith that told its members to obey God, and God's representative in Rome, before any earthly master. He had also heard what was happening in the Philippines, where the Spanish had arrived with friars and stayed as rulers.

In 1596 a Spanish galleon on its way from Manila to Mexico was wrecked on the Japanese coast, and Hideyoshi's officials seized its cargo. The story that reached Hideyoshi was that the ship's pilot, hoping to frighten them, had shown them a map of the Spanish empire and explained how it had grown so large. First, he said, the king sent priests to convert the people, and then he sent soldiers, who joined up with the converts. A few months later Hideyoshi had twenty-six Christians, foreign priests and Japanese believers, marched to Nagasaki and crucified on a hill above the harbour.

The Tokugawa family, who took power after him, finished the job. Christianity was banned outright in 1614. Priests were hunted down, and believers were told to renounce their faith or die, often slowly. The last resistance came in 1637, when some thirty-seven thousand people in the south rose in revolt and shut themselves in an abandoned castle. They were farmers with their wives and children, led by a few masterless samurai, and most of them were Christians driven to despair by taxes and famine. They held out for four months. When the castle fell, almost all of them were killed.

After that, every household in Japan had to register with a Buddhist temple. In the districts where Christians had been numerous, officials came round once a year with a small bronze plaque showing Christ or the Virgin Mary, laid it on the floor, and watched while each person in the village stepped on it. A few thousand families went on practising their faith in secret for seven generations, baptising their children and murmuring half-remembered Latin prayers, and their descendants revealed themselves to a French priest in 1865.

## Hostages in the capital

The man who ate the cake was Tokugawa Ieyasu, a patient lord from the east who had waited through the reigns of both his predecessors. After Hideyoshi's death he defeated his rivals in a great battle in 1600, and three years later he had the emperor name him shogun. His family held the office for the next two hundred and sixty-five years.

The Tokugawa had watched two unifiers fail to pass their power on, and they designed a system to make sure that nothing would ever change again. They governed about a quarter of the country directly from their castle town of Edo, the city now called Tokyo. The rest they left to some two hundred and sixty lords. The problem was to keep those lords from combining against them.

Every lord was required to spend every other year living in Edo, in attendance on the shogun. When he went home to his own lands, his wife and his heir stayed behind in the capital. The shogun therefore held the family of every lord in Japan as permanent hostages. Guards on the main roads were told to look out for two things in particular, guns going towards Edo and women coming away from it.

The arrangement had a second effect, which was just as useful. A great lord could not travel like a private person. He marched to Edo and back with hundreds or thousands of retainers in procession, and he had to keep up two grand households. This ate up a large share of his income, year after year, and left him little to spend on soldiers.

Foreign trade did not fit into such a system. A lord in the far south-west with a good harbour could grow rich on Portuguese silk and could buy foreign cannon, a thousand kilometres from Edo. The foreign religion, meanwhile, gave the shogun's subjects a loyalty that he did not control. Between 1633 and 1639 the shogun's government issued a series of orders that dealt with both dangers. No Japanese was to leave the country, on pain of death. Any Japanese living abroad who came back was to be executed. Ships large enough to cross the ocean were not to be built. The Portuguese were expelled for good, and when they sent an embassy the next year to ask for a second chance, the shogun had sixty-one of its members beheaded and sent the rest home to report it.

The doors that stayed open were chosen with care. The Dutch were Protestants who were willing to leave their religion at the harbour mouth, and they had proved it by lending a ship to bombard the Christian rebels. They were allowed to stay, on an artificial island in Nagasaki harbour. It was shaped like an open fan, about two hundred paces long, and joined to the town by a single guarded bridge. A dozen or two Dutchmen lived there, forbidden to cross the bridge without permission, and a few ships came each year. Chinese merchants traded at Nagasaki as well, in far larger numbers. There were three other doors. The lord of Tsushima, an island in the strait, handled trade with Korea, with which peace had been patched up. The lord of Satsuma, in the far south, dealt with the Ryukyu islands and through them with China. A lord in the far north traded with the Ainu people of the island now called Hokkaido.

All four doors were watched by the shogun's men or by lords he trusted. The country was not sealed. Its foreign trade had been placed under the government's eye.

## The quiet centuries

In return for all this, Japan received something that almost no other country had in those centuries, which was peace. From 1638 until the 1860s there was no war among the lords of Japan, and none with a foreign power.

The samurai, who made up about one family in fifteen, kept their two swords and had nobody to use them on. They became clerks, tax officials and schoolteachers. Guns went on being made, under licence, and farmers kept them for shooting boar and deer. For two hundred years, however, no Japanese army fired one in battle. Farmers cleared new land, and the population doubled in a century. The lords' processions, and the need to supply their households in Edo, covered the country with good roads, inns and shipping routes. By 1700 Edo had about a million people and was the largest city in the world.

The townspeople could read. Small private schools, many of them held in temples, taught boys, and many girls, to read, write and use the abacus, and by the 1800s nearly half of Japanese men were literate, a level matched in few other places on earth. Publishers in the big cities printed novels, travel guides, farming manuals and colour pictures of actors by the thousand.

The phrase "closed country" was coined in 1801, by a Japanese scholar who was translating a Dutch book. The people who made the policy had no such name for it, and they did not think of themselves as hiding.

Each summer, when the Dutch ship came into Nagasaki, its captain was required to hand the governor a written report of everything that had happened in the outside world since the last one. Interpreters translated it, and a copy went to Edo by fast courier. The shogun's ministers read about the wars of Europe, the fall of dynasties in China and, in time, about ships that moved without sails. They knew a good deal about the world that they were keeping out, and for two hundred years the reports gave them little reason to change course.
