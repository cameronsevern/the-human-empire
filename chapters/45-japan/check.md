I found no clear contradiction with chapters 22, 31, 32 or 39–44, and no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or prohibited cross-references. The findings below are ranked by seriousness. No files were edited.

1. **Factual error — Japan did not nearly abandon firearms.**
   
   > “A hundred years after it, Japan's rulers had expelled the Portuguese and nearly given up the gun itself.”
   
   > “Guns were made only under licence, in small numbers, and the art of using them was almost forgotten.”
   
   These repeat the discredited “giving up the gun” account. Firearms remained in military inventories and widespread civilian use, including hunting and crop protection. Reduced battlefield use during peace is not lost knowledge. **Confidence: high.** [Tamara Enomoto, “Giving Up the Gun? Overcoming Myths about Japanese Sword-Hunting and Firearms Control”](https://www.academia.edu/79623059/Giving_Up_the_Gun_Overcoming_Myths_about_Japanese_Sword_Hunting_and_Firearms_Control).

2. **Factual error and break of the rule against fragile absolutes — the sword hunt did not disarm everyone outside the samurai class.**
   
   > “From then on only samurai carried weapons, and a villager could no longer become a soldier, as Hideyoshi himself had done.”
   
   The decree’s sweeping wording did not describe its implementation. Villagers retained firearms, bows and other weapons; some were permitted to carry swords. The separation of warriors and farmers developed through several measures, rather than taking complete effect with the 1588 order. **Confidence: high.** [Enomoto, especially pp. 50–52](https://www.academia.edu/79623059/Giving_Up_the_Gun_Overcoming_Myths_about_Japanese_Sword_Hunting_and_Firearms_Control).

3. **Misleading factual conclusion — controlled trade was not a single government trading monopoly.**
   
   > “Its foreign trade had been turned into a government monopoly.”
   
   The shogunate controlled access and delegated particular foreign relationships to domains, while merchants continued conducting trade. Distinguish a monopoly over permission and diplomatic channels from the government monopolising the buying and selling. This distinction matters to the chapter’s central explanation. **Confidence: high.** [Arano Yasunori, “Foreign Relations in Early Modern Japan”](https://www.nippon.com/en/features/c00104/foreign-relations-in-early-modern-japan-exploding-the-myth-of-national-seclusion.html).

4. **Doubtful causal account and missing chronology — Hideyoshi’s turn against missionaries predates the pilot’s boast.**
   
   > “What changed his mind was the thought of where their loyalty lay.”
   
   The narrative moves from this explanation to the shipwreck of 1596 without mentioning his **1587** missionary-expulsion edict. That omission makes the shipwreck appear to explain the original reversal. The earlier edicts explicitly objected to forced conversions, destruction of shrines and temples, and lords giving territory to the church. Divided loyalty is a defensible interpretation, but the documented actions need to anchor it. **Confidence: high.** [Hideyoshi’s 1587 edicts, translated at Columbia University](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/main_pop/ps/ps_japan-tokugawa-edicts-christianity.htm).

5. **Doubtful causal claim — invading Korea was not demonstrably an improvised solution to unemployed soldiers.**
   
   > “He commanded several hundred thousand experienced soldiers and had no more enemies in Japan for them to fight, and unemployed armies are dangerous to the man at the top.”
   
   > “Hideyoshi's solution was to send them abroad.”
   
   Disposing of potentially troublesome armies is one interpretation, not an established explanation. Hideyoshi expressed ambitions to conquer China before completing Japan’s unification. His imperial ambitions can be stated confidently; the proposed domestic motive needs distinguishing from fact. **Confidence: high on the chronology; medium on the relative importance of motives.** [Kenki Adachi, “Hideyoshi’s invasions of Korea reconsidered”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/review-of-international-studies/article/orderbuilding-from-the-margins-hideyoshis-invasions-of-korea-reconsidered-through-liminality-and-competing-systems/7DBD2B864DB2BC9E6F45A28AEB27567D).

6. **Misleading ending — foreign reports did prompt changes before Perry.**
   
   > “They knew a good deal about the world that they were keeping out, and for two hundred years they saw nothing in the reports to make them change their minds.”
   
   Dutch reports about the Opium War helped alarm the shogunate. In 1842 it relaxed the order to repel foreign ships and showed increasing interest in Western artillery. The sentence leaves readers with an unchanging policy and an incurious response that the evidence does not support. **Confidence: high.** [National Diet Library, “Opening of Japan and Japan-Netherlands Relations”](https://www.ndl.go.jp/nichiran/e/s1/s1_4.html).

7. **Chronological problem — the rebellion cannot explain measures already underway.**
   
   > “The foreign religion, meanwhile, had already shown that it could inspire a rebellion.”
   
   This immediately introduces the orders of **1633–1639**, but the rebellion just described began in **1637**. It could reinforce the later restrictions, especially the Portuguese expulsion, but cannot explain the beginning of that sequence. The dates supplied by the chapter itself expose the problem. **Confidence: high.**

8. **Factual overstatement and fragile absolute — Korea had not experienced two centuries without fighting.**
   
   > “Korea was a different kind of country from Japan, and in 1592 it had been at peace for two hundred years.”
   
   Korea had avoided anything comparable to Japan’s prolonged civil wars, but suffered substantial coastal fighting, including the disturbances of 1510 and Japanese raids in 1555. “Relative peace” would preserve the comparison without erasing those events. **Confidence: high.** [National Institute of Korean History, account of the 1555 raids](https://contents.history.go.kr/mobile/kc/view.do?code=kc_age_30&levelId=kc_i304800).

9. **Factual overstatement — the peace claim erases frontier warfare.**
   
   > “From 1638 until the 1860s there was no war on Japanese soil, at home or with foreigners.”
   
   Shakushain’s war against the Matsumae domain occurred in 1669. Ezo’s relationship to Japan was different from that of the main islands, so this needs geographical precision rather than a modern territorial assumption in either direction. The defensible point is the prolonged absence of nationwide civil war. **Confidence: high on the counterexample; the territorial wording needs care.** [Foundation for Ainu Culture, history of Shakushain’s war](https://www.ff-ainu.or.jp/web/learn/culture/together/files/rekishitobunka_EN.pdf).

10. **Factual overstatement — Dutch commercial pragmatism was specific to Japan.**
    
    > “The Dutch were Protestants who cared about profit and had no interest in converting anyone, and they had proved it by lending a ship to bombard the Christian rebels.”
    
    The Dutch East India Company supported Protestant missionary activity elsewhere, notably Taiwan. What distinguished its conduct in Japan was its willingness to accept the prohibition on missionary work. Bombarding Catholic rebels also does not establish religious indifference. **Confidence: high.** [Huygens Institute, VOC records from Taiwan](https://www.huygens.knaw.nl/en/resources/de-dagregisters-van-het-kasteel-zeelandia-taiwan-1629-1662-2/).

11. **Factual error — the Dutch shipping limit belongs to a later period.**
    
    > “A dozen or two Dutchmen lived there, forbidden to cross the bridge without permission, and one or two ships came each year.”
    
    The two-ship limit began in **1715**, beyond the chapter’s stated endpoint. Several Dutch ships annually visited during much of the seventeenth century. This understates the scale of the surviving connection. **Confidence: high.** [Dejima’s official history](https://nagasakidejima.jp/english/history/).

12. **Doubtful detail — the Nagashino gun count is disputed.**
    
    > “He put three thousand gunners behind fences of wooden stakes, with a river in front, and waited.”
    
    Three thousand is the familiar traditional figure, but readings of the chronicles differ; one thousand is another major estimate. Massed firearms behind prepared defences are a sound basis for the scene. The exact count should not be presented as settled. **Confidence: high that the number is disputed; medium on any replacement figure.** [“Preserving Fields of Conflict,” discussion of Nagashino and its sources](https://www.academia.edu/94854409/Preserving_Fields_of_Conflict_Papers_from_the_2014_Fields_of_Conflict_Conference_and_Preservation_Workshop).

13. **Doubtful factual claim — the gun’s superiority in range is overstated.**
    
    > “The owner poured in a black powder and a small lead ball, touched a smouldering cord to a hole near the back, and with a flash and a bang knocked down a duck at a distance no bow could match.”
    
    “No bow could match” requires evidence about the actual distance and a meaningful comparison of effective ranges. Early firearms offered much less dramatic advantages than this suggests; military historian Thomas Conlan describes their initial improvements in range and penetration as incremental. The demonstration can establish novelty and destructive power without this unsupported superlative. **Confidence: medium-high.** [Conlan, “Instruments of Change,” especially p. 147](https://tconlan.scholar.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf3991/files/tconlan/files/war_and_state_building_in_medieval_japan_-_7._instruments_of_change_organizational_technology_and_the_consolidati._copy.pdf).

14. **Misleading description — the population inside Hara Castle was not simply an army of farmers.**
    
    > “The last resistance came in 1637, when some thirty-seven thousand farmers in the south, most of them Christians driven to despair by taxes and famine, rose in revolt and shut themselves in an abandoned castle.”
    
    The commonly quoted total includes women, children and other dependants, as well as fighters and masterless samurai. Calling all of them rebellious farmers obscures the civilian character of many victims of the subsequent massacre. **Confidence: high.** [Shimabara Castle’s official history](https://shimabarajou.com/en/history).

15. **Factual overstatement and internal confusion — Sejong’s scholars were not unanimously hostile.**
    
    > “The scholars of his own court hated it.”
    
    Some court scholars opposed the alphabet; others helped explain and promote it. Indeed, the preceding sentences say that Sejong and his scholars designed it. Chŏng Inji, a scholar associated with the project, wrote the approving postscript containing the morning-and-ten-days claim. Distinguish the opponents from the collaborators. **Confidence: high.** [Columbia’s translation of Chŏng Inji’s postscript](https://community.gsb.columbia.edu/main_pop/ps/ps_korea-alphabet.htm), [Ch’oe Malli’s opposition](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/main_pop/ps/ps_korea-alphabet-dissent.htm).

16. **Factual overstatement and unclear timeframe — popular schools were not generally temple-run.**
    
    > “Village schools run by temples taught boys, and many girls, to read, write and use the abacus, and by the end of the period nearly half of Japanese men were literate, a level matched in few other places on earth.”
    
    These schools had historical connections to temples, but nationally most teachers were commoners; clergy and samurai also taught. Moreover, “the end of the period” needs a date: readers have been promised a chapter ending around 1700, whereas the familiar literacy estimates concern the late Tokugawa period. **Confidence: high on school organisation; the literacy estimate needs a dated source and definition.** [Japan’s education ministry, “Education for Commoners”](https://www.mext.go.jp/b_menu/hakusho/html/others/detail/1317227.htm).

17. **Reader orientation — the rulers responsible for the persecution have not yet been introduced.**
    
    > “The rulers who came after him finished the job.”
    
    The reader follows persecution through 1637 and secret Christianity through 1865, then returns to Ieyasu’s accession in 1600–1603. Naming the Tokugawa here and briefly locating the change of regime would prevent readers from attributing this whole policy to an unidentified succession—or to Ieyasu alone.

18. **Reader orientation — the four doors cannot readily be counted or located.**
    
    > “Korea, with which peace had been patched up, traded through an island in the strait, and two other lords were licensed to deal with the islands to the south and the peoples of the north.”
    
    The next paragraph says “All four doors,” but the reader has encountered Dutch and Chinese trade, an unnamed island, and two unnamed regions. Explain that Dutch and Chinese commerce shared Nagasaki, then identify the other three channels: Tsushima–Korea, Satsuma–Ryukyu, and Matsumae–Ainu. These names serve the explanation rather than cluttering it. [Arano’s account of the four portals](https://www.nippon.com/en/features/c00104/foreign-relations-in-early-modern-japan-exploding-the-myth-of-national-seclusion.html).

19. **Style — two sentences announce explanations instead of giving them.**
    
    > “Both things were done for reasons that made sense at the time.”
    
    > “Their solution was simple and extremely effective.”
    
    Both are signposts of the kind expressly prohibited by `style.md`: the first promises reasons, and the second announces and evaluates a solution before stating it. The surrounding story can supply those explanations directly. This is a structural issue, not an objection to the narrator taking a confident view.
