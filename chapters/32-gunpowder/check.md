1. **Factual error; high confidence.** “They had to make do with infantry.”

   The Song maintained cavalry and obtained horses through breeding and substantial frontier trade. Shortages constrained their mounted forces; they did not eliminate them. The preceding sentence about enemies refusing to sell horses does not explain the other available suppliers. [Paul J. Smith’s account of the tea-and-horse administration](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/ordering-the-world-approaches-to-state-and-society-in-sung-dynasty-china.html) describes annual procurement quotas of 15,000–18,000 horses. This matters because the chapter’s explanation of military defeat rests heavily on this claim.

2. **Doubtful chronology; high confidence.** “For about two centuries it remained a curiosity.”

   Military applications were established before 1050: a gunpowder arrow was demonstrated to the emperor in 970, and further gunpowder weapons in 1000. Possible military use dates to 904. The safe correction is that weapon development was underway during the tenth century. [Tonio Andrade, *The Gunpowder Age*, chapter 1](https://dokumen.pub/the-gunpowder-age-china-military-innovation-and-the-rise-of-the-west-in-world-history-9781400874446.html).

3. **Doubtful quantitative foundation; high confidence that the estimate is contested.** “From the tax records, historians have estimated the country's output of iron in the year 1078 at well over a hundred thousand tonnes.”

   This presents Hartwell’s influential estimate without its substantial evidential problem. Donald Wagner finds that the assumptions used to convert government receipts into national output lack justification. He considers roughly 114,000 tonnes plausible, but unsupported by direct quantitative evidence. The following comparison with Europe inherits that uncertainty. Retain the scale of the industry through a firmer example, or identify this explicitly as one estimate. [Wagner, “The administration of the iron industry in eleventh-century China,” conclusion](https://www.donwagner.dk/SongAdmin/SongAdmin.pdf).

4. **Factual error; high confidence.** “The first two Song emperors tried to take the passes by force and were badly beaten.”

   This conflates campaigns against Northern Han with attempts to recover the Sixteen Prefectures. The two major failed recovery campaigns, in 979 and 986, belonged to the **second** emperor, Taizong. Taizu’s northern campaigns were directed against Northern Han. [Peter Lorge, *The Reunification of China*, introductory discussion](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811070/84759/excerpt/9781107084759_excerpt.pdf).

5. **Misleading chronology; high confidence.** “They moved their capital to the Chinese plain, took a Chinese dynastic name that means ‘gold’, employed Chinese officials, and held examinations.”

   Its placement makes these developments consequences of capturing Kaifeng. The Jurchen adopted the dynastic name **Jin** in 1115, before that conquest. Their principal capital moved to modern Beijing in 1153 and to Kaifeng in 1214. Separate the founding of the dynasty from these later changes; otherwise readers may assume Kaifeng became their capital immediately. [Valerie Hansen, “The Kitan-Liao and Jurchen-Jin”](https://history.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/V%20Hansen,%20Kitan-Liao%20and%20Jurchen-Jin%20%202019.pdf); [Ari Daniel Levine’s study of Jin Kaifeng](https://www.academia.edu/5455571/_Welcome_to_the_Occupation_Collective_Memory_Displaced_Nostalgia_and_Dislocated_Knowledge_in_Southern_Song_Ambassadors_Travel_Records_of_Jin_dynasty_Kaifeng_).

6. **Factual error; high confidence.** “In 1012 the emperor had thirty thousand bushels of a new kind of rice seed brought up from Vietnam and handed out to farmers in the south.”

   The imperial shipment was procured **from Fujian**, where Champa rice was already cultivated, and distributed to drought-affected regions around the lower Yangzi and Huai. Vietnam describes the variety’s earlier provenance, not the origin of this particular shipment. [Randolph Barker, “The Origin and Spread of Early-Ripening Champa Rice”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12284-011-9079-6).

7. **Doubtful technical specificity; medium-high confidence.** “Under the Song, ironmasters in the north learned to fire their tall furnaces with coal that had been baked into coke, and to drive the bellows with waterwheels.”

   Mineral fuel is supported; the specific assertion that it was necessarily coke is less secure. Wagner distinguishes evidence for coal use from the unresolved question of how it was prepared and used. “Used coal in ironmaking” is firmer than this precise technological sequence. [Wagner, “Blast furnaces in Song–Yuan China”](https://donwagner.dk/SongBF/SongBF.pdf). Also explain *bellows*—the device blowing air into the furnace—on first appearance.

8. **Factual overextension; high confidence.** “The dynasty lasted there for another century and a half, with the north lost, paying a yearly tribute to the Jurchen and addressing their ruler as a superior.”

   The sentence makes the tribute relationship sound continuous throughout that century and a half. Jin fell in **1234**, decades before Song ended in 1279; diplomatic terms also changed during the intervening period. Attach the payments and subordinate relationship to the relevant peace settlement, rather than the dynasty’s entire remaining lifetime. [Hansen, “The Kitan-Liao and Jurchen-Jin”](https://history.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/V%20Hansen,%20Kitan-Liao%20and%20Jurchen-Jin%20%202019.pdf).

9. **Technical inaccuracy; high confidence.** “Set the mixture alight, and the whole of it burns in an instant, with no need of air, and turns into a volume of hot gas hundreds of times larger than the powder was.”

   Black powder produces substantial **solid residue as well as gas**; it does not all turn into gas. “Releases a large volume of hot gas” preserves the explanation without the error. [Experimental analysis of gaseous and solid combustion products, *RSC Advances*](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/ra/d4ra05979d).

10. **Reader loses the succession; high confidence.** “Huizong, his son, and some fourteen thousand members of the court and household, including the palace women, were marched to Manchuria as prisoners.”

    The son was the reigning emperor, **Qinzong**: Huizong had abdicated in 1126. Without that sentence of context, readers assume Huizong remained in command throughout both sieges. The subsequent “One of his younger sons” also needs an unmistakable reference to Huizong. [Hansen’s account of the abdication and conquest](https://history.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/V%20Hansen,%20Kitan-Liao%20and%20Jurchen-Jin%20%202019.pdf).

11. **Misleading connection with chapter 22; high confidence.** “Like the Han before them, the Song could not breed good horses on rice fields, and their enemies would not sell them any.”

    Chapter 22 explicitly describes “Han armies of cavalry” and an expedition taking 140,000 horses. The agricultural constraint itself is compatible with that account, but this analogy followed by “make do with infantry” implies a shared inability to field cavalry. Explain the distinction between difficulty obtaining horses and having none. There is no comparable clear contradiction with chapter 31.

12. **Missing explanatory bridge.** “Mix powdered charcoal intimately with powdered saltpetre, however, and each grain of fuel has its oxygen lying right beside it.”

    The discovery story used **honey**, sulphur, saltpetre and an arsenic mineral. The explanation silently switches to charcoal. A reader needs one sentence explaining that honey supplied combustible material in the early mixture, while the familiar later recipe used charcoal.

13. **Missing mechanism.** “Another was wrapped round the shaft of an arrow, just behind the head.”

    Explain that the incendiary charge was lit and the arrow **shot from a bow**. Otherwise a general reader may picture a rocket propelled by the powder, especially before the subsequent discussion of powder propelling objects.

14. **Signpost sentence.** “What happened to it afterwards depended on the kind of country in which it had been found.”

    This announces an explanation instead of giving it, contrary to “No signpost sentences.” State the connection between Song resources and weapons production directly, or let the following scene establish it.

15. **Exception-breaking absolute.** “For centuries, followers of the Daoist teaching had believed that the right combination of substances, properly heated, would yield a drug that made the body immortal, and they had worked their way through everything that could be dug up, ground down and cooked.”

    “Everything” is an unnecessary universal claim. “A vast range of minerals and other substances” would retain the breadth without asserting exhaustive experimentation.
