Read the requested files; no files changed. Findings below are ordered by seriousness. Line numbers refer to [chapter 10](chapters/10-wheel/chapter.md).

1. **Unsupported central explanation — metal tools were not a demonstrated prerequisite.**  
   > “It needs an axle that fits it exactly, a metal tool to cut that fit, and a strong animal to pull the load.” (L5)

   Neolithic carpenters made sophisticated joints with stone tools long before wheeled vehicles appeared. Wells dated to 5206–5098 BCE preserve split boards and complex timber joints. This does not establish how the first wheels were made, but it undermines the claim that carpentry had to await metal. The same problem recurs in L27 and L33. **Confidence: high.** [Tegel et al., 2012](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0051374).

2. **Wrong comparison of ancestry figures.**  
   > “In Germany, about three quarters of the ancestry of the people buried after that date came from the steppe herders.”  
   > “In Britain, a few centuries later, the change was closer to nine tenths.” (L109)

   These percentages measure different things. The German estimate concerns sampled **Corded Ware people**, not everyone buried subsequently. Britain’s approximately 90% describes replacement of the previous population’s ancestry by continental newcomers who themselves had mixed ancestry; it does not mean 90% steppe ancestry. **Confidence: high.** [Haak et al., 2015](https://reich.hms.harvard.edu/sites/reich.hms.harvard.edu/files/inline-files/nature14317.pdf); [Olalde et al., 2018](https://reich.hms.harvard.edu/sites/reich.hms.harvard.edu/files/inline-files/nature25738_Olalde_2.pdf).

3. **The proposed convergence is contradicted by earlier ploughing evidence.**  
   > “At about the same time as the wheel, they began to harness oxen in pairs to drag a plough.” (L31)

   Plough marks in Switzerland dated to 5100–4700 BCE are interpreted as evidence of animal traction, substantially before wheeled vehicles around 3500 BCE. They do not establish the exact harness arrangement, but the chapter cannot confidently date the beginning of animal ploughing to the wheel’s appearance. This also weakens “All three came together” in L33. **Confidence: high on earlier animal traction; lower on its precise beginnings.** [Van Willigen et al., 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-02837-5).

4. **Single invention is presented as something the dates prove.**  
   > “What they show clearly is something else: the wheel was not invented again and again by different peoples.”  
   > “It was invented once, or very nearly, and then it spread from farm to farm faster than anything had spread before.” (L45)

   Near-contemporary discoveries establish widespread early use, not a unique invention. Different early wheel-and-axle designs leave independent invention a live possibility. “Faster than anything” additionally claims a comparison the evidence supplied cannot establish. Keep rapid appearance across a large area; distinguish that from the hypothesis explaining it. **Confidence: high that the certainty is unwarranted; the actual number of inventions remains unresolved.** [Swiss National Museum](https://blog.nationalmuseum.ch/en/2020/06/who-invented-the-wheel/).

5. **Animal power is confused with usefulness, creating an internal contradiction.**  
   > “It becomes useful only when there is a large, strong, patient animal to put in front of it, and that animal was the ox.” (L31)

   Animal traction makes heavy wagons much more useful; it is not necessary for useful wheeled transport. The chapter supplies its own counterexample at L63: “The wheelbarrow needs no animal at all.” This matters because the categorical claim also supplies the explanation for Mexico’s lack of carts. **Confidence: high; source: the chapter’s own wheelbarrow example.**

6. **The language argument overstates what shared vocabulary establishes.**  
   > “A people cannot have a word for something they have never seen.”  
   > “So the speakers of that parent language had wheels, axles and yokes, and they were still one people, speaking one tongue, after the wagon was invented.” (L107)

   People can name things learned about through others. The relevant evidence is systematic correspondence between inherited words, with borrowing and changes of meaning considered—not direct visual acquaintance. Shared vocabulary also does not establish a single undifferentiated people. Specify that the wheel argument concerns a later common linguistic stage; the deeper ancestor including Anatolian is a separate chronological question. **Confidence: high on the faulty inference; moderate on exact linguistic dates.** [Lazaridis et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08531-5).

7. **The Americas’ large animals did not all disappear.**  
   > “The large animals of the Americas had died out soon after people arrived, and the horses and the wild cattle were among them.” (L59)

   Many large mammals disappeared, including native horses, but bison and other large mammals survived. “Wild cattle” is particularly misleading: aurochs were not native American animals, while American bison survived through descendant populations. The relevant absence is suitable **domesticated draft animals**, not large animals generally. **Confidence: high.** [National Park Service: ancient bison](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/ancient-bison.htm).

8. **Mexican transport excludes its waterways.**  
   > “Everything that moved in their world moved on human backs.” (L57)

   Canoes carried people and goods on lakes, rivers and coastal routes. This is a substantial omission in an argument about transport alternatives, not merely a stylistic exaggeration. Restrict the claim to the predominance of human porters in overland freight. **Confidence: high.** [UNAM: Mesoamerican naval technology](https://www.noticonquista.unam.mx/amoxtli/1409/1407).

9. **The hemisphere-wide claim overlooks working dogs.**  
   > “The one beast of burden left in the whole hemisphere was the llama, far to the south in the Andes.” (L59)

   Indigenous North Americans used dogs to haul loads on travois. Llamas were the principal domesticated large pack animals, but not the hemisphere’s only load-bearing animals. **Confidence: high.** [National Park Service transportation account, written by Mandan Hidatsa Arikara Nation members](https://www.nps.gov/articles/transportation.htm).

10. **Botai horse keeping is a genuine disagreement, presented as settled.**  
    > “Their villages are full of horse bones, and their cooking pots carry traces of mare’s milk.”  
    > “Nobody can milk a wild horse, so these people were keeping horses in some fashion.” (L89)

    The pottery residues have been interpreted as mare’s milk, supporting horse management. That interpretation and other domestication evidence have been challenged; another interpretation treats Botai primarily as specialised horse hunters. The chapter can favour management, but should briefly acknowledge the consequential disagreement, as `style.md` permits. **Confidence: high that the evidence is contested; moderate on which interpretation is correct.** [Outram et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19265018/); [Taylor and Barrón-Ortiz, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8018961/).

11. **“Every modern horse” contradicts the preceding paragraph.**  
    > “Every modern horse descends from a population that lived on the steppe near the lower reaches of the Volga and the Don, north of the Caucasus mountains.” (L93)

    This needs “modern domestic horses.” Przewalski’s horses belong to a distinct lineage—the very distinction L91 has just introduced. **Confidence: high.** [Librado et al., 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04018-9).

12. **Composite construction was not compulsory.**  
    > “So the wheel has to be built from planks cut along the length of the tree, shaped into parts of a circle, and pinned together so firmly that they behave as one piece.” (L25)

    Early wheels could also be made from a **single longitudinal plank**. The valid distinction is between wood cut across and along the grain; it does not imply that every wheel required several boards joined together. **Confidence: high.** [Belfiore et al., engineering investigation of prehistoric wheels](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X17301931).

13. **Continuity problem with the earlier chapters’ transport story.**  
    > “Everything they moved went on somebody’s back or on a sledge: the harvest from the field, firewood from the forest, dung from the cattle pen out to the land.” (L47)

    As an unrestricted account of farming before carts, this excludes boats and pack animals. Readers have already encountered grain carried by donkeys in [chapter 6, L38](chapters/06-taxes/chapter.md:38) and grain floated along the Nile in [chapter 7, L109](chapters/07-kings/chapter.md:109). Those passages are not themselves proof of dates before the wheel, so this is principally a **scope and continuity conflict**, rather than a clean chronological contradiction. Specify local overland hauling by the particular farming household.

14. **The steppe’s water problem is introduced but never solved.**  
    > “That means carrying a shelter, bedding, tools, food, and above all water, across country where there may be none for days.” (L73)

    The following wagon explanation accounts for the family’s belongings and water, but leaves the reader wondering what the herd—and the oxen pulling the wagon—drink. Explain access to watering places and how movement was organised around them. Otherwise the machine seems to solve a logistical problem that remains unresolved.

15. **Two migrations into Europe need distinguishing for the existing reader.**  
    > “About 4,800 years ago, the ancestry of northern and central Europe changed sharply.” (L109)

    Chapter 5 already explained a major ancestry change caused by farmers arriving from Turkey. A general reader could mistake this for the same event or wonder whether the account has changed. One sentence identifying this as a **later movement into an already farming Europe** would connect the stories without a chapter cross-reference.

16. **The language section withholds useful identifying terms.**  
    > “The ancient language of India shared hundreds of words and whole patterns of grammar with Greek and Latin, and also with the old languages of the Germans, the Celts and the Slavs.” (L103)

    Identify Sanskrit and explain “Indo-European” once. “The ancient language of India” misleadingly suggests there was only one, while the unnamed language family is the subject of the entire section. These names help the reader follow the argument rather than adding decorative detail.

17. **A crucial mechanical part goes unexplained.**  
    > “A hub sits at the centre.” (L123)

    A reader unfamiliar with wheels needs to know that the hub is the central wooden block through which the axle passes and into which the spokes fit. The surrounding explanation names rim, spokes and sockets but never connects this new construction back to the axle problem established earlier. This falls short of `style.md`’s requirement that readers could explain the device afterwards.

18. **Absence of surviving evidence becomes knowledge of everyone’s thoughts.**  
    > “For all that time, people who saw wheels every day did not think to put one under a hand-barrow.” (L63)

    The archaeological appearance of a device cannot establish that nobody previously imagined or tried it. State the delay in its known appearance or adoption. This is precisely an exception-breaking absolute, not an objection to the narrator taking a view.

19. **The military conclusion uses avoidable universals.**  
    > “Men on foot cannot catch a chariot and cannot get away from one.” (L129)  
    > “Every one of those kings drew the same conclusion, which was that he had to have chariots of his own, whatever they cost.” (L133)

    The first needs the conditions that make the claim meaningful: open ground and a functioning team, with no refuge or obstacle. The second asserts every ruler’s decision without establishing it. Describe the speed advantage and the resulting pressure on kingdoms to acquire chariots; neither point requires universality.

20. **Two sentences announce coming material instead of delivering it.**  
    > “On one kind of land, it changed everything.” (L67)  
    > “The steppe had one more thing to offer, and the herders were the people best placed to take it.” (L87)

    These are signposts of the kind `style.md` prohibits: the reader is promised a consequential landscape or another advantage before being told what it is. Name the grassland and the horse directly.
