# Chapter 11 fact-check lookups

Verdict key: correct / wrong / imprecise / could not verify. Chapter file not edited. Sections are appended as they are checked.

## Opening (Uluburun discovery and cargo)

1. "In the summer of 1982, a young man diving for sponges off the south coast of Turkey came up and told his captain that he had seen something odd on the seabed... they looked like biscuits with ears."
   - Verdict: correct. The finder was sponge diver Mehmet Cakir, summer 1982, off Uluburun near Kas; his description was "metal biscuits with ears."
   - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck ; https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/

2. "In the age of bronze, copper was shipped as flat slabs, each about as heavy as a full suitcase, with four corners drawn out into handles" (oxhide ingots).
   - Verdict: correct. Oxhide ingots typically ~25 kg with four handle-like projections.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot

3. "On a steep slope, some fifty metres under the surface"
   - Verdict: correct. Pulak (Met catalogue essay) gives the site depth as 42 to 61 m; Wikipedia 44-52 m.
   - Sources: https://www.blackwoodconservation.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Uluburun-Shipwreck-and-Late-Bronze-Age-Trade.pdf (Pulak) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck

4. "a ship that had sunk about 3,300 years ago."
   - Verdict: correct. Late 14th century BC; dendro/radiocarbon ~1320 +/- 15 BC (INA), Kuniholm ~1327 BC. About 3,330 years.
   - Source: https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/

5. "It took eleven summers and more than twenty thousand dives to bring it up."
   - Verdict: correct. Eleven campaigns 1984-1994 (INA also says 11 seasons), 22,413 dives (INA: "more than 22,000").
   - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck ; https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/

6. "The ship had been about fifteen metres long, built of cedar."
   - Verdict: correct. Pulak: reconstructed length about 15 m, beam about 5 m; hull planks Lebanese cedar (tenons and pegs oak).
   - Source: Pulak PDF above (blackwoodconservation.org); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck

7. "She was carrying ten tonnes of copper, in more than three hundred and fifty of those slabs."
   - Verdict: imprecise (minor). 354 copper ingots, about 10 tons, but only 317 are oxhide-shaped; the rest are other forms (bun/plano-convex etc.). "Slabs" for all 354 is slightly loose.
   - Replacement: "She was carrying ten tonnes of copper, in about three hundred and fifty ingots, most of them those slabs with handles."
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck

8. "Beside the copper lay one tonne of tin."
   - Verdict: correct. About 1 ton of tin ingots (Pulak; Wikipedia).
   - Sources: Pulak PDF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck

9. "There were lumps of deep blue glass, cast in discs, for melting down and working."
   - Verdict: imprecise. About 175 raw glass ingots, ~350 kg, cobalt blue and turquoise, with a few purple and one amber. Pulak describes them as roughly truncated cones (round cake/disc-like), not flat discs; and not all blue.
   - Replacement: "There were about a hundred and seventy-five round cakes of raw blue glass, for melting down and working."
   - Source: Pulak PDF (Met catalogue essay)

10. "logs of black hardwood from tropical Africa"
    - Verdict: correct. 18 logs of African blackwood (Dalbergia melanoxylon), ~1 m each (the "ebony" of the sources).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck

11. "a length of elephant tusk, and more than a dozen hippopotamus teeth."
    - Verdict: correct. One elephant tusk and 14 hippopotamus teeth (Pulak).
    - Source: Pulak PDF

12. "ostrich eggshells, tortoise shells"
    - Verdict: correct. Three ostrich eggshells (Pulak); tortoise carapaces listed in the cargo and used as lyre sound boxes.
    - Sources: Pulak PDF; https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/

13. "a hundred and fifty tall jars, most of them full of a tree resin that was burned as incense."
    - Verdict: correct. More than 150 Canaanite jars; terebinth resin in about 100 of them, over half a ton. Use as incense is the standard interpretation (some also propose perfume/medicinal).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck

14. "beads of amber from the Baltic Sea"
    - Verdict: correct (as personal items, not bulk cargo). Amber beads found; Pulak lists "Baltic" among the cultures represented (Beck's analysis identified Baltic succinite).
    - Source: Pulak PDF

15. "swords of two different countries"
    - Verdict: correct. Canaanite and Mycenaean swords (Pulak: one Canaanite sword, two Mycenaean swords among others).
    - Source: Pulak PDF

16. "a gold ring bearing the name of an Egyptian queen."
    - Verdict: wrong. It is a small gold scarab (an amulet/seal, not a ring) inscribed with the name of Nefertiti, wife of Akhenaten.
    - Replacement: "...and a small gold scarab bearing the name of the Egyptian queen Nefertiti."
    - Sources: Pulak PDF (Met catalogue, cat. no. 223); https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/

17. "they turned out to come from at least ten different lands, which today lie between Sweden and Afghanistan."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Pulak lists cultures: Canaanite, Mycenaean, Cypriot, Egyptian, Nubian, Baltic, northern Balkan, Old Babylonian, Kassite, Assyrian, Central Asian, possibly south Italian/Sicilian (11-12). Cline is quoted as "at least seven." "Sweden" is not documented (Baltic amber, not a Swedish find); Afghanistan is a preliminary guess (lapis/tin).
    - Replacement: "When the finds were sorted, they turned out to come from more than ten different peoples and lands, from the Baltic coast in the north to Nubia in the south, and from Italy in the west to Central Asia in the east."
    - Sources: Pulak PDF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck

18. "All of it was in one modest ship, on what seems to have been an ordinary run."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Pulak calls it "an elite dispatch of an enormously wealthy and diverse cargo," possibly royal in character, not ordinary. Ship size is fair ("modest": 15 m).
    - Replacement: "All of it was in one modest ship, on what seems to have been a single trading voyage up the coast from the Levant."
    - Source: Pulak PDF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck

## Nine parts copper, one part tin

19. "Copper was the first metal that people learned to melt and cast."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Copper was the first metal smelted from ore (Balkans, c. 5400-5000 BC, Belovode/Plocnik), but native copper and gold were worked earlier, and gold was cast by the Chalcolithic. "First metal smelted from ore" is safe.
    - Replacement: "Copper was the first metal that people learned to smelt out of rock."
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age ; https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/holocene/chalcolithic.htm (low quality; no better source found)

20. "A copper knife loses its edge quickly, and a copper sword bends."
    - Verdict: correct (soft metal; tin bronze is harder, "harder than copper alone").
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze

21. "Melt nine parts of copper with one part of tin, and the result is bronze."
    - Verdict: correct. Classic Bronze Age casting bronze was about 10% tin (about 6% for hammered sheet; modern ~88/12).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze

22. "[Bronze] also melts at a lower heat and flows more freely into a mould... an axe head with a socket... in one pour."
    - Verdict: correct. Bronze melts at about 950 C (copper 1083 C); tin bronze casts better than arsenic copper; socketed axes are the typical cast tool.
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze ; https://vanderkrogt.net/elements/element.php?sym=Cu (copper 1083 C)

23. "For about two thousand years, bronze was the best material that people had for anything that needed to be hard"
    - Verdict: correct as a round figure (tin bronze from the mid/late 4th-3rd millennium BC to iron c. 1200-1000 BC; roughly 2,000 years).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze

24. "The ship... was carrying its two ingredients in exactly the right proportion, ten of copper to one of tin. Melted together, her cargo would have made eleven tonnes of bronze, which is enough to arm several thousand soldiers."
    - Verdict: correct. 10:1 ratio, ~11 tons of bronze; published estimates of about 5,000 swords/soldiers armed.
    - Sources: https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/ ; https://www.inverse.com/science/uluburun-shipwreck-tin-trade-route/amp

25. "Cyprus had so much of [copper ore] that the metal's name, in most European languages, comes from the island's."
    - Verdict: correct for English, Romance and Germanic languages (Latin aes Cyprium, later cuprum, "metal of Cyprus"). Not Slavic/Finnic etc. ("most European languages" is acceptable; "English and many other European languages" is tighter).
    - Sources: https://www.etymonline.com/word/cupreous ; https://www.wiktionary.com/wiki/cuprum

26. "[Tin] is one of the scarcer metals in the earth's crust, and there was almost none of it in the lands where the first cities grew. There was none in Egypt, almost none in Iraq, and none in Greece."
    - Verdict: correct in substance. Tin ore is far rarer than copper; Egypt and Mesopotamia have no usable tin deposits. Greece has no significant tin. (Mesopotamia has none; "almost none in Iraq" is fine.)
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze ; https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/early-tin-in-the-near-east/

27. "The nearest large sources lay far to the east, in the mountains of Central Asia, or far to the west, at the Atlantic end of Europe."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Tin was also mined in the Taurus Mountains of Anatolia (Kestel/Goltepe) and Pulak/Powell find the Uluburun tin came partly from the Taurus (Powell attributes about two thirds to the Taurus/Kestel region, a source scale debated). Western Afghanistan tin is also proposed. So not only far east or far west.
    - Replacement: "The large sources lay far to the east, in the mountains of Central Asia, or far to the west, at the Atlantic end of Europe, and a smaller one lay in the Taurus mountains of Turkey."
    - Sources: Pulak PDF (blackwoodconservation.org); https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/11/221130151458.htm ; https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/early-tin-in-the-near-east/

28. "Chemists have recently tested the tin from the wreck... About a third of it came from a valley in what is now Uzbekistan, more than three thousand kilometres from the place where the ship went down."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Powell et al. (Science Advances 2022, 105 ingots, ~91% of the tin) found one third of the ingots made from Central Asian tin, "Uzbekistan and Tajikistan"; the named deposit, Mushiston, lies in Tajikistan (Zeravshan mountains). Distance: roughly 3,500 km straight-line to the Turkish coast (ScienceDaily: more than 2,000 miles from Haifa). Also, the Frontiers in Earth Science rebuttal (Berger et al. 2023) rejects Mushiston as a source, so the finding is contested.
    - Replacement: "Chemists have recently tested the tin from the wreck to find where it was mined. In 2022 a team reported that about a third of it came from the mountains of Central Asia, in what is now Tajikistan and Uzbekistan, more than three thousand kilometres from the place where the ship went down."
    - Sources: https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/tin-from-uluburun-shipwreck-shows-small-scale-commodity-exchange-/ ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/11/221130151458.htm ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1211478/full

29. "Every bronze blade in Egypt or Greece contained metal that had crossed mountains and deserts, and had passed through the hands of many strangers on the way."
    - Verdict: correct as a general statement (Egypt and Greece had no tin; "every" is slightly strong but safe for tin bronze).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze

## The donkeys of Ashur

30. "About 3,900 years ago, there was a small city called Ashur on the river Tigris, in the north of Iraq."
    - Verdict: correct. Old Assyrian trade period c. 1950-1750 BC (Kanesh level II c. 1945-1835, level Ib c. 1832-1700). Ashur (Assur) lies on the west bank of the Tigris in northern Iraq.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kültepe

31. "Tin came to it from the east, carried by other traders. Fine woollen cloth came up from Babylonia in the south."
    - Verdict: correct. Tin reached Assur from the east (Elam/Iran, bought via intermediaries); textiles from Babylonia were re-exported (plus Assyrian-woven cloth). Penn Museum: tin came from the east, probably western Afghanistan.
    - Sources: https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/early-tin-in-the-near-east/ ; https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2023/features/assyrian-women-of-letters/

32. "A thousand kilometres to the north-west, across the mountains, lay the highlands of Turkey, which were rich in silver and gold, were busy making bronze, and had no tin of their own."
    - Verdict: correct. Distance Assur to Kanesh about 1,000-1,200 km (Archaeology magazine: 750 miles). Anatolia traded silver and gold; "had no tin of their own" is the standard Old Assyrian view (Taurus tin was minor/not used by Assyrians).
    - Source: https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2023/features/assyrian-women-of-letters/

33. "They loaded tin and cloth onto donkeys, about seventy kilos to an animal"
    - Verdict: correct (range). Secondary sources give 60-80 kg per donkey; one citing Veenhof and Larsen gives about 67.5 kg of tin (with 30 textiles). No primary figure checked.
    - Source: https://www.cappadociahistory.com/post/k%C3%BCltepe-kanesh-karum (via search results); https://jamiehmiller.substack.com/p/the-bronze-age-merchants-who-built

34. "The trip took six weeks."
    - Verdict: correct as the common figure ("six weeks across 750 miles" per Archaeology magazine; the Met Museum gives 6-8 weeks).
    - Sources: https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2023/features/assyrian-women-of-letters/ ; https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/30004518

35. "At the far end, in a town in the middle of Turkey, they kept a permanent settlement of their own, with houses, warehouses and an office that settled disputes."
    - Verdict: correct. The karum of Kanesh (Kultepe, near Kayseri): houses, archives, and the karum office/assembly that judged disputes.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karum_(trade_post)

36. "archaeologists have found more than twenty thousand clay tablets" in the ruins of those houses.
    - Verdict: correct. Over 22,000 (some counts 23,000) tablets, nearly all from the lower town karum.
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kültepe ; https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2023/features/assyrian-women-of-letters/

37. "It was run by family firms. The father stayed in Ashur and raised the money from investors, who put in gold for a term of years and took a share of the profit. The sons lived at the Turkish end and did the selling."
    - Verdict: correct in outline. Family firms confirmed (Kultepe article); the investment contract (naruqqum) was a pooled fund of gold for a long term (often ten years) with profit split between investors and the agent. Typical pattern: a senior partner in Assur, sons and brothers in Anatolia. (One-third profit share not confirmed.)
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kültepe ; https://scholarship.kentlaw.iit.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3007&=&context=cklawreview ; https://nuttersworld.com/civilisations-that-collapsed/first-global-network/

38. "Tin bought in Ashur sold in Turkey for about twice what it had cost."
    - Verdict: correct (gross). Scholarly estimates give about 100% gross markup on tin and textiles, with net profits around 50% after taxes, transport and losses.
    - Sources: https://www.aabri.com/manuscripts/162508.pdf ; https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:3721454/download

39. "Out of that came the taxes owed to every local ruler along the road, the donkey drivers' wages, and losses to weather and thieves."
    - Verdict: correct. Tablets record road taxes to local rulers, wages of donkey drivers, and smuggling to avoid the palace's pre-emption and taxes.
    - Source: https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2023/features/assyrian-women-of-letters/

40. "The wives ran the households in Ashur and wove much of the cloth that went on the donkeys, and they wrote to their husbands in a tone that needs no translation."
    - Verdict: correct. Women in Assur wove textiles for export and wrote to husbands in Kanesh; over 300 letters by or to women (Michel).
    - Sources: https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2023/features/assyrian-women-of-letters/ ; https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-02995550

41. "One complains that he has criticised the last batch of cloth as too thin, having criticised the batch before as too thick."
    - Verdict: could not verify; no letter found matching this. Closest real letter: Lamassi (wife of Pushu-ken) wrote: "Who is this man who lives in your house and who is criticizing the textiles when they get to him?" and "I try my best to make and send textiles to you!" A different letter, from the trader Puzur-Assur to Waqqurtum, asks for more of the thin textile she sent. Neither says "too thin then too thick."
    - Replacement: "One writes back, with some heat, to ask who it is that lives in his house and criticises the cloth whenever it reaches him, when she has done her best to make it and send it."
    - Sources: https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2023/features/assyrian-women-of-letters/ ; Cécile Michel, Women of Assur and Kanesh (SBL 2020) https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/9780884144557.pdf

42. "Another asks why the neighbours have built a new house while she is still waiting for the silver he promised."
    - Verdict: could not verify; no letter found matching this. Closest: Taram-Kubi (wife of Innaya) wrote that he had "cleared out the house and emptied everything," left her "not a single shekel" of silver, and that she wanted to enlarge the house by buying the neighbour's. She asked for silver from the sale of her cloths to buy barley during a famine. Nothing about neighbours building a new house.
    - Replacement: "Another, Taram-Kubi, tells her husband he has emptied the house and left her not a single shekel of silver, and begs him to come home."
    - Sources: https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/publications/mesopotamia/2020-10-31.html ; https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2023/features/assyrian-women-of-letters/

43. "Behind that one sword stand the miners of two countries, three or four sets of merchants, a hundred donkeys, a dozen rulers..."
    - Verdict: illustrative figure, not a checkable fact (hundred donkeys and dozen rulers are invented for the example). Caravans of 200-250 donkeys are attested, so the scale is plausible.
    - Source: https://www.cappadociahistory.com/post/k%C3%BCltepe-kanesh-karum

## Brothers

44. "By about 3,400 years ago, the trade had grown..."; Amarna age.
    - Verdict: correct. Amarna letters date to c. 1360-1330 BC, about 3,350 years ago.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters

45. "Egypt held the Nile and the coast of what is now Israel and Lebanon. A kingdom called Hatti ruled most of Turkey from a fortress city in the central highlands. Babylon held the south of Iraq, and Assyria... the north."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Egypt held Canaan (Israel, much of Lebanon, southern Syria) as vassal states; Hatti (Hattusa) did not rule "most" of Turkey in the Amarna age (western Anatolia was Arzawa, Assuwa, etc., and Mitanni held northern Syria and upper Mesopotamia then). Assyria only became a significant power late in the Amarna period. The chapter omits Mitanni.
    - Replacement: "A kingdom called Hatti ruled the central highlands of Turkey from a fortress city. Mitanni held the north of Syria and Iraq. Babylon held the south of Iraq, and Assyria, grown from that small trading city of Ashur, was just becoming a power in the north."
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters

46. "In 1887, a woman digging for fertiliser in the ruins of an Egyptian city found a hoard of clay tablets."
    - Verdict: imprecise. The standard story is a local woman digging sebakh (decayed mud brick used as fertiliser) at el-Amarna in 1887; scholars note the account is disputed and possibly a cover story by local dealers. Wikipedia only says local Egyptians dug up most of the tablets around 1887.
    - Replacement: "In 1887, villagers digging for fertiliser in the ruins of an Egyptian city found a hoard of clay tablets."
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters ; https://hist1039-16.omeka.fas.harvard.edu/exhibits/show/under-the-guise-of-greatness/the-amarna-letters ; https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/comment/2792

47. "There are about three hundred and fifty of them, and they are the foreign letters of the Egyptian court. Kings wrote them to other kings."
    - Verdict: imprecise. 382 tablets and fragments are known, 350 of them letters (Knudtzon edited 358). But most letters are from vassal rulers of Canaan and Syria, not great kings; only about 50 are from the "great kings" and Alashiya (EA 1-44 roughly), the rest are from vassals and Egypt's own copies.
    - Replacement: "There are about three hundred and fifty of them, and they are the foreign letters of the Egyptian court. About fifty were written by kings of other great powers; most of the rest came from petty rulers who were Egypt's vassals."
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters

48. "The great kings call each other 'brother'. They ask after each other's health, wives, horses and chariots."
    - Verdict: correct. Great kings address each other as brother; standard greeting asks after the king, his house, wives, sons, horses and chariots.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters

49. "One king sends horses and blue stone, and asks for gold. He adds that in his brother's country, as everybody knows, gold is as plentiful as dust."
    - Verdict: correct. Tushratta of Mitanni, EA 19 (also repeated in other Tushratta letters, e.g. EA 16, 20): "In my brother's country gold is as plentiful as dust (dirt)," translation varies. EA 19 lists gifts incl. lapis lazuli and horse teams with chariots.
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letter_EA_19 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters (search-result translation, "as plentiful as dust")

50. "The king of Babylon complains that the last gold sent to him lost three quarters of its weight when it was melted."
    - Verdict: correct. EA 10 (Burna-Buriash II to the pharaoh): of 20 minas of gold, "not 5 minas" appeared when put in the kiln (more than three quarters lost). EA 7 (also Burna-Buriash): 40 minas, "not even 10" (restored/guess in Moran's edition).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letter_EA_10 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letter_EA_7

51. "The king of Cyprus sends a load of copper with an apology for sending so little. A plague, he explains, has killed his copper workers."
    - Verdict: correct. EA 35 (king of Alashiya): "the hand of Nergal has slain all the men of my country, so there is not a (single) copper-worker"; he sends 500 (talents) of copper and says not to be concerned that it is little. (Papadopoulou questions the 500-talent reading; Moran: epidemic.) "Plague" is the standard rendering of "hand of Nergal."
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letter_EA_35 ; https://ancientegyptonline.co.uk/ea35/

52. "Kings exchanged the things that only kings could command, and each expected a gift of equal value in return."
    - Verdict: correct. Reciprocity of gifts is the central theme; complaints (EA 10, EA 7, EA 19) are about unequal returns.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters

53. "They sealed the arrangements by marrying each other's daughters."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Egypt took foreign princesses (Babylon, Mitanni, Hatti, Arzawa) but refused to send its own: in EA 4 Amenhotep III answers "from time immemorial no daughter of the king of Egypt is given to anyone."
    - Replacement: "They sealed the arrangements with marriages, and the pharaoh took the daughters of his brother kings into his household, though he never sent one of his own."
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letter_EA_4

54. "A princess would travel for months to a country whose language she did not speak, with hundreds of servants and a dowry listed on a tablet as long as a man's arm."
    - Verdict: could not verify the specifics. EA 22 (Tushratta's daughter Tadu-Heba) is a very large multi-column tablet listing the wedding gifts; I found no source giving its length, the servant count, or a travel time in months.
    - Replacement: "A princess would travel to a country whose language she did not speak, with her servants and a dowry listed on a long clay tablet, as Tushratta's daughter did when she was sent to the pharaoh."
    - Source: https://repository.bilkent.edu.tr/bitstream/11693/51237/1/Tushratta_s_requests_to_the_pharaohs.pdf (general)

55. "The king of Egypt and the king of Hatti wrote to each other in the language of Babylon, in wedge-shaped signs on clay."
    - Verdict: correct. Akkadian (Babylonian dialect) was the diplomatic language. Most Amarna letters are in Akkadian; EA 24 is in Hurrian, and a few others in Hittite. "Hardly any is written in Egyptian" is right.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters

56. "It was the first time that anything like an international system had existed"
    - Verdict: imprecise. The great-powers club is best documented at Amarna, but similar brotherhood diplomacy appears earlier, in the Mari letters (18th century BC) and the Ebla archives (c. 2400 BC).
    - Replacement: "It was the best-documented international system of the ancient world: a club of states that recognised each other..."
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters (general context; no earlier-system source fetched)

## What a chariot costs

57. "Each chariot needed a pair [of horses], and a spare pair behind them." and later "A king with a thousand chariots was therefore paying for two or three thousand war horses"
    - Verdict: imprecise. A two-horse team per chariot is standard (Egyptian and Hittite chariots were pair-drawn). A spare pair for every chariot is not documented. It also contradicts the later sentence: a pair plus a spare pair is four horses per chariot, so a thousand chariots would need four thousand, not "two or three thousand."
    - Replacement: "Each chariot needed a pair, and spares behind them." Then "A king with a thousand chariots was therefore paying for two thousand war horses and more."
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kadesh (two horses per chariot in the Kadesh accounts; no source found for a spare pair)

58. "Horses will not stay fit for war on grass alone. They have to be fed grain... trained for months to run in harness, turn together and stand firm."
    - Verdict: correct. The Kikkuli text prescribes oats, barley and hay several times a day over months of conditioning.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kikkuli

59. "A training manual survives from the court of Hatti, written by a master horseman hired from abroad. It lays out a programme of feeding, watering and running that lasts for seven months, set down day by day."
    - Verdict: correct. Kikkuli, "master horse trainer of the land of Mitanni" (a Hurrian), c. 1400 BC; text from Hattusa (found 1906-07); the programme is 214 days (about seven months); Nyland's 1991-92 replication followed the 7-month schedule. "Hired from abroad" is a fair gloss of his Mitanni origin. (The text is in Hittite with Indo-Aryan technical terms.)
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kikkuli

60. "The spoked wheels were the work of specialists, made of several kinds of wood, each chosen for its part."
    - Verdict: correct. Egyptian chariots (e.g. Tutankhamun's) combined elm, ash, birch and other woods for different parts. (Not re-fetched; widely documented.)
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariot (general)

61. "The crew needed bows built up in layers of wood, horn and sinew, which took a year or more to make and cure."
    - Verdict: correct (composite bows; active work a week or two, but drying and curing can take months to a year or more).
    - Source: https://www.bow-international.com/features/a-multi-layered-affair-laminated-and-backed-bows/

62. "They wore coats made of hundreds of small bronze scales, sewn on in overlapping rows."
    - Verdict: correct in kind (bronze scale armour on linen/leather backing, attested at Nuzi, Ugarit and in Egypt, with hundreds to over a thousand scales per coat). Specific count not checked.
    - Source: no fetched source; general archaeological knowledge

63. "A chariot warrior had to spend his life at it, and so he had to be kept by the palace, with land or rations, along with his driver, his groom and the craftsmen"
    - Verdict: correct. The maryannu class and Mycenaean/Hittite chariot-fighters were maintained by palace land grants or rations.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariot (general)

64. "In Greece, the records of the palaces survive on clay tablets, baked hard when the palaces burned. Tablet after tablet lists chariot wheels by the pair, with a note on each as to whether it is fit for use."
    - Verdict: correct. Pylos Sa tablets list wheels, e.g. PY Sa 794: "one pair of wheels, bound with bronze, unfit for service." Tablets baked by the destruction fires is standard.
    - Source: https://sites.utexas.edu/scripts/files/2020/05/1991-TGP-MaritimeMattersInTheLinearBTablets-02.pdf (general Linear B context); search summary quoting Ventris translation of PY Sa 794

65. "Others record the weight of bronze handed out to each smith, smith by smith."
    - Verdict: correct. Pylos Jn series records bronze allotments to individual smiths (the ta-ra-si-ja system).
    - Source: https://www.thearchaeologist.org/blog/the-mycenaean-linear-b-tablets-records-of-a-bronze-age-economy

66. "The largest chariot battle that we know of was fought about 3,300 years ago in Syria, between Egypt and Hatti."
    - Verdict: correct. Kadesh, Year 5 of Ramesses II, c. May 1274 BC (about 3,300 years ago); widely called the largest chariot battle (5,000-6,000 chariots combined).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kadesh

67. "According to the Egyptian account, the king of Hatti brought more than three thousand chariots to the field."
    - Verdict: wrong. The Egyptian account (Kadesh Poem) states 2,500 Hittite chariotry ("he found 2,500 chariotry surrounding him"), elsewhere "like the sand." Modern estimates for the Hittite side are about 2,500-3,500 (Wikipedia gives 2,500) and total about 5,000-6,000.
    - Replacement: "According to the Egyptian account, the king of Hatti brought two and a half thousand chariots to the field."
    - Sources: https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/ancient/1294BC-egypt-kadesh.asp ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kadesh

68. "Neither side won."
    - Verdict: correct. Outcome inconclusive: Hittites kept Kadesh; Ramesses claimed victory; next day ended in stalemate.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kadesh

69. "Some years later, the two kings signed a treaty of peace and friendship, the oldest between great powers whose text survives."
    - Verdict: imprecise. The treaty was signed in Ramesses II's year 21 (c. 1259/1258 BC), about 15 years later. It is the earliest peace treaty known with both parties' versions surviving, but older treaty texts exist (e.g. Ebla-Abarsal, c. 2300 BC; Naram-Sin of Akkad with Elam, c. 2250 BC), so "oldest" is wrong.
    - Replacement: "About fifteen years later, the two kings signed a treaty of peace and friendship. Copies survive from both sides, and no other treaty of the age is so well known."
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Treaty_of_Kadesh ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kadesh

70. "They promised not to attack each other, to send help if the other was attacked, and to hand back each other's runaways."
    - Verdict: correct. The treaty has non-aggression, mutual defence and extradition of fugitives clauses.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Treaty_of_Kadesh

71. "So the system fed itself..." (palaces drawing grain, wool, oil and labour into storerooms and redistributing by tablet).
    - Verdict: correct as a description of palace economies (Linear B, Ugarit, Hatti), an interpretation rather than a single checkable fact.
    - Source: https://www.thearchaeologist.org/blog/the-mycenaean-linear-b-tablets-records-of-a-bronze-age-economy

## The enemy's ships have come

72. "It came apart within about fifty years, a little over 3,200 years ago."
    - Verdict: correct as a round figure. The Late Bronze Age collapse is conventionally c. 1200-1150 BC (Ugarit destroyed c. 1190-1178 BC, Hattusa c. 1200-1180, Pylos c. 1180, Medinet Habu campaign 1177).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

73. "Ugarit was a merchant city, subject to the king of Hatti, with a harbour full of ships and a population that wrote in at least four languages."
    - Verdict: correct. Ugarit was a Hittite vassal; its archives hold Akkadian, Ugaritic, Hurrian, Sumerian, Hittite, Egyptian, Cypro-Minoan, Luwian and more (at least eight or nine).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugarit

74. "Its last letters were still in the houses when the city fell."
    - Verdict: correct (last-years tablets, including letters, were found in the ovens and houses of the destruction level, e.g. the House of Urtenu).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugarit

75. "The king of Hatti writes to demand a shipload of grain, and says that it is a matter of life and death. There is famine in his country."
    - Verdict: correct in substance. In a letter from the Hittite king to the king of Ugarit (usually cited as RS 20.212), he reprimands Ugarit for failing to provide a ship and crew to carry grain from Mukish (north Syria) to Ura (Cilicia) and says it is a matter of life or death. Related letters RS 94.2530 and 94.2523 also concern Hittite demands to Ugarit. Not RS 34.152, which was not confirmed as the grain letter. The sender is not firmly identified in all sources (Tudhaliya IV or Suppiluliuma II).
    - Sources: https://fada.birzeit.edu/bitstream/20.500.11889/4717/1/Halayqa-%20UF-42%20%20the%20Demise%20of%20Ugarit.pdf ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291016979_Crisis_in_Context_The_End_of_the_Late_Bronze_Age_in_the_Eastern_Mediterranean

76. "The king of Ugarit writes to the king of Cyprus that enemy ships have come and burned his towns. He cannot defend himself, he says, because his soldiers and chariots are away in Hatti, and his ships are away on the coast of Turkey, where his overlord had ordered them."
    - Verdict: imprecise. The letter is RS 20.238 from Ammurapi to the king of Alashiya: "My father, behold, the enemy's ships came; my cities were burned... Does my father not know that all my troops are in the Land of Hatti, and all my ships are in the Land of Lukka?" (Lukka = southwest Turkey.) Correct that troops and ships are away. The text does not say the overlord "ordered" the ships to Lukka; the sources treat it as a reasonable but unstated inference (the companion letters RS 94.2530/2523 concern Hittite shipments to Lukka). Other catalogue numbers appear (RS 18.147, RS 20.18 for related letters); RS 20.238 is the usual number for the Ammurapi-to-Alashiya letter.
    - Replacement: "The king of Ugarit writes to the king of Cyprus that enemy ships have come and burned his towns. He cannot defend himself, he says, because his soldiers and chariots are away in Hatti, and his ships are away on the coast of Turkey."
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugarit ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammurapi ; https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789088905544.pdf

77. "Soon afterwards, Ugarit was burned. Arrowheads lie in its streets. People had buried their valuables under the floors of their houses, and nobody came back to dig them up. The city was never lived in again."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Ugarit was sacked and burned c. 1190-1185 BC (some give 1178); arrowheads were found in the destruction layer; buried hoards, such as 74 bronzes under a threshold in the House of the High Priest, were left. But Ugarit was not entirely abandoned: Stratum 2 (1200-1100 BC) shows some later occupation, and nearby Ras Ibn Hani and Minet el-Beida had small later settlements. It was never rebuilt as a city.
    - Replacement: "The city was never rebuilt."
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugarit

78. "About the same time, the capital of Hatti was emptied and burned."
    - Verdict: correct (with nuance). Hattusa was abandoned c. 1200 BC or soon after; recent excavation (Seeher) shows most inhabitants and records had already left, and the royal buildings, temples and gates were burned afterwards.
    - Source: https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/ancient-cultures/ancient-near-eastern-world/the-last-days-of-hattusa/

79. "The kingdom that had fought Egypt to a draw simply stops. Its line of kings ends, its archives end, and its language was never written again."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Written records of the Hittite kingdom end with Suppiluliuma II and Hittite cuneiform ended. But a branch of the royal family survived at Carchemish, the Neo-Hittite states of north Syria continued for centuries, and the closely related Luwian language was still written in hieroglyphs.
    - Replacement: "The kingdom that had fought Egypt to a draw simply stops. Its last king vanishes, its archives end, and its language was never written in cuneiform again."
    - Source: https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/ancient-cultures/ancient-near-eastern-world/the-last-days-of-hattusa/

80. "In Greece, the palaces went one after another, each of them in flames."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Pylos burned c. 1180 BC; Mycenae had an earthquake c. 1250 and fires c. 1190; Tiryns had an earthquake c. 1200; Thebes was sacked repeatedly and burned. Not every palace is shown burned, and some were damaged by earthquakes.
    - Replacement: "In Greece, the palaces went one after another, most of them in flames."
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

81. "The last tablets from one of them, on the south-west coast, record men being sent to watch the shore."
    - Verdict: correct. Pylos (south-west Peloponnese): the o-ka series (e.g. PY An 657, "Thus the watchers are guarding the coast") lists men assigned to coastal watch. (Cline doubts it proves an attack from the sea; the tablets themselves say what the chapter says.)
    - Source: https://sites.utexas.edu/scripts/files/2020/05/1991-TGP-MaritimeMattersInTheLinearBTablets-02.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

82. "With the palaces went the only people in Greece who knew how to write, since writing had been used for nothing but palace accounts."
    - Verdict: correct as the standard view (Linear B was used by palace scribes for administrative records; no literary use known; script went out of use on the mainland).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Dark_Ages

83. "For the next four hundred years, the Greeks were without it."
    - Verdict: correct. Linear B ended c. 1200 BC; the Greek alphabet, adapted from the Phoenician, appears by c. 800-750 BC. (Cyprus kept its own syllabary, but the chapter is speaking of Greece.)
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Dark_Ages

84. "Their population fell by more than half."
    - Verdict: imprecise. There is no solid overall figure. Settlement evidence shows up to 90% of small sites in the Peloponnese were abandoned (Wikipedia, citing survey data), but this is for sites, not a measured population number, and varies by region.
    - Replacement: "Most of the villages of the Peloponnese were abandoned."
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

85. "Egypt came through, and left the one account by a ruler who lived to tell of it. On the wall of his temple, the king describes a league of peoples from the islands and coasts to the north, who came against Egypt by land and by sea."
    - Verdict: correct. Ramesses III, Medinet Habu, year 8 (c. 1177 BC); confederation of Peleset, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Denyen, Weshesh. Other Egyptian accounts exist (Merneptah's Libyan war, the Harris Papyrus), so "the one account" is slightly strong; it is the one account of the invasion by a ruler who fought it, with the reliefs.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Peoples

86. "No country had been able to stand before them, he says. He names Hatti, Cyprus and several others, and says that they were all cut off at once."
    - Verdict: correct. The inscription names Hatti, Qode, Carchemish, Arzawa and Alashiya (Cyprus) as lands that were "cut off" at one time.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Peoples

87. "The carvings beside the words show a battle among ships in the mouth of the Nile. They also show the invaders on land, and among the fighting men there are ox carts full of women and children."
    - Verdict: correct. Naval battle in the Nile mouths and a land battle with the invaders' families in ox-carts.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Peoples

88. "Within two generations it had given up everything that it held outside the Nile valley."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Egypt withdrew from southern Canaan over about a century (many garrisons abandoned without destruction; Jaffa and Aphek destroyed in the 12th century), and lost Nubia around the end of the New Kingdom (c. 1070 BC). "Two generations" is too short for everything.
    - Replacement: "Within a century it had given up Canaan, and soon after it lost Nubia too."
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

89. "The king's own tomb builders stopped work and sat down in protest, because their grain rations had not been paid. It is the first strike in recorded history."
    - Verdict: correct. Deir el-Medina, year 29 of Ramesses III (c. 1157 BC), Turin Strike Papyrus: workers on the royal tomb walked out over unpaid grain rations. Widely described as the first recorded labour strike.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deir_el-Medina_strikes

## One at a time, or all at once

90. "Historians named them the Sea Peoples and imagined a wave of pirates that swept the coast and brought civilisation down."
    - Verdict: correct. "Sea Peoples" is a 19th-century label (Maspero, 1881) from Egyptian "peoples of the sea"; the invasion-of-pirates narrative dominated 20th-century writing.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Peoples

91. "Grains of ancient pollen, taken from the mud of lake beds in Syria and Cyprus, show the countryside growing drier around this time, and it stayed dry for some three centuries."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Kaniewski et al. (PLoS ONE 2013) used a sediment core from Larnaca Salt Lake near Hala Sultan Tekke, Cyprus, plus Syrian coastal sites (Tell Tweini area), and report a drought of about 300 years starting c. 1200 BC. Larnaca is a salt lake. Reasonably accurate; "lake beds" is loose.
    - Replacement: "Grains of ancient pollen, taken from the mud of a salt lake in Cyprus and a coastal marsh in Syria..."
    - Sources: https://typeset.io/pdf/environmental-roots-of-the-late-bronze-age-crisis-37l5ue1tll.pdf ; https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/environmental-changes-contributed-mediterranean-cultural-crisis/

92. "The growth rings of old trees in Turkey record one run of three years, shortly before the capital of Hatti was abandoned, that were among the driest in a thousand years."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Manning et al. (Nature 2023) analysed juniper beams from the Midas Mound at Gordion, central Turkey, and found a severe three-year drought, 1198-1196 BC (plus or minus a few years), coinciding with the Hittite collapse. The "driest in a thousand years" ranking is not in the abstract or press coverage I found.
    - Replacement: "The growth rings of old juniper trees in central Turkey record a run of three years, around 1198 to 1196 BC, of severe drought, at the time the capital of Hatti was abandoned."
    - Sources: https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/02/230208124227.htm ; https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-05693-y (DOI 10.1038/s41586-022-05693-y; page not fetched, login redirect)

93. "The demand for grain as a matter of life and death is one of several. The king of Egypt, a few years earlier, had boasted of sending shiploads of grain to keep Hatti alive."
    - Verdict: correct (as commonly reported). Merneptah's inscription (Karnak) says he sent grain in ships "to keep alive the land of Hatti"; he reigned c. 1213-1203 BC, roughly a decade or two before the fall of Hattusa. Direct Karnak wording not re-read; secondary sources confirm.
    - Source: https://cavac.at/cavacopedia/Merneptah%20Stele ; https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/jaei/article/viewFile/47/49

94. "The peoples whom the Egyptians fought were probably not a single nation of pirates. They were a mixture of the displaced: farmers whose crops had failed, soldiers whose palaces could no longer pay them, sailors who had turned to plunder."
    - Verdict: correct as a scholarly interpretation; the Egyptian reliefs list five or more named groups with families in ox-carts. Whether they were farmers whose crops failed is inference.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Peoples

95. "Some show walls thrown down by earthquakes, and that region lies on a restless seam in the earth's crust."
    - Verdict: correct. Earthquake damage at Mycenae (c. 1250), Tiryns (c. 1200), Beth Shean. (Millek 2023 argues earthquakes were less influential than once thought.)
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

96. "Some palaces seem to have been burned by their own people. In several places it was the palace alone that burned, while the houses of the town were left standing."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Revolt is one proposed cause (systems-collapse analysis lists peasant revolts), but no palace is shown to have been burned by its own people. At Hattusa the fires hit the palace, temples and gates while the town was largely empty. At Ugarit and Pylos, the town also burned or the palace burned in a wider attack.
    - Replacement: "At Hattusa the fires were in the palace, the temples and the gates, and the town around them was empty."
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse ; https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/ancient-cultures/ancient-near-eastern-world/the-last-days-of-hattusa/

97. "A kingdom that needs tin from a thousand kilometres away..."
    - Verdict: correct as an illustration (Assur to Kanesh about a thousand km; Central Asian tin more than three thousand).
    - Source: https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2023/features/assyrian-women-of-letters/

98. "Each of these things had happened before. There had been droughts, earthquakes, raids and revolts in every century, and the kingdoms had recovered."
    - Verdict: could not verify as stated; a general interpretive claim (Cline's "systems collapse" argument). Not a checkable single fact.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

## Who came through

99. "Egypt and Assyria survived as shrunken kingdoms, each pulled back inside its old heartland."
    - Verdict: correct. Egypt lost Canaan and later Nubia; Assyria lost territory in the 11th century and shrank to its heartland before reviving.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

100. "On the coast of Lebanon stood a row of port cities that had spent centuries paying tribute to one great king or another... their ships... sailed further than anyone had before, the whole length of the Mediterranean."
    - Verdict: correct. Phoenicia gained autonomy as Egyptian and Assyrian power waned and Phoenician trade reached the western Mediterranean (Cadiz/Gadir and Carthage, traditional foundations 1100/814 BC; archaeology gives 9th-8th century).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

101. "Cyprus recovered quickly, too."
    - Verdict: correct. Kition shows no violent destruction and Kition and Paphos prospered after 1200 BC; some other sites (Maa-Palaeokastro, Enkomi) were destroyed or abandoned first.
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

102. "a writing system of several hundred signs that took years of schooling to learn. Along that coast, people were already using a far simpler script, of fewer than thirty letters, one for each sound."
    - Verdict: imprecise. Cuneiform used several hundred signs (about 600), correct. The Phoenician alphabet had 22 letters, but all consonants only; the Ugaritic cuneiform alphabet had 30 signs. So "fewer than thirty, one for each sound" is wrong for the Phoenician script (22, one per consonant). The proto-Canaanite alphabet is attested centuries earlier (c. 1900-1500 BC).
    - Replacement: "Along that coast, people were already using a far simpler script, of twenty-two letters, one for each consonant."
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Dark_Ages (Greek alphabet adapted from Phoenician); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugarit (Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform)

103. "With the tin routes broken, smiths in Cyprus and along the same coast turned to a metal that they had known about for centuries... It could not be melted and poured in the furnaces of the time, and had to be hammered into shape while red hot."
    - Verdict: correct in the main. Iron was known earlier (rare meteoritic and early smelted iron); bronze-age furnaces could not liquefy iron (1538 C), so iron was a bloomery product, forged by hammering. Ironworking spread from the Balkans/Anatolia/Cyprus in the 13th-12th centuries. That tin disruption drove the shift is a standard but contested explanation (Wikipedia notes disruption of tin shipments "helping drive" iron adoption; Fitzsimmons and others stress other factors).
    - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

104. "Its one great advantage... Its ore lies in the ground almost everywhere, and it needs nothing added to it."
    - Verdict: correct (iron ore is abundant; no alloying metal is needed for wrought iron).
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse

Wrong: 16 (scarab not a ring), 67 (Hittite chariots 2,500 in the Egyptian account).
Imprecise: 7, 9, 17, 18, 19, 27, 28, 45, 46, 47, 53, 56, 57, 69, 76, 77, 79, 80, 84, 88, 91, 92, 96, 102.
Could not verify: 41, 42 (no letters match the two paraphrases), 54, 98.
