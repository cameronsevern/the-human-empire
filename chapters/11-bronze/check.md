1. **Doubtful causal claim — high confidence.** “With the tin routes broken, smiths in Cyprus and along the same coast turned to a metal that they had known about for centuries and had never thought much of.”

   The proposed tin-shortage explanation for iron’s adoption lacks strong archaeological support; bronze production and metal exchange continued. Iron spread unevenly over centuries. Also, earlier iron was rare and highly valued, rather than disregarded. These are substantive problems with the chapter’s explanation of why the Bronze Age ended. [Erb-Satullo, *The Innovation and Adoption of Iron in the Ancient Near East*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10814-019-09129-6).

2. **Overstated economic premise — high confidence.** “Moreover, the palaces had gathered so much into their own hands that there was little left outside them.”

   This gives the collapse argument an unreliable foundation. Substantial farming, production and exchange existed outside palace control, including in Mycenaean Greece. Palace destruction ended particular administrative systems; it did not necessarily eliminate the district’s economic resources. [Voutsaki, *Mycenaean Glocalism*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/trade-and-civilisation/mycenaean-glocalism-greek-political-economies-and-international-trade/707867BD61CAD048D99A3CEAB3E5D19C).

3. **Unsupported universal claim — high confidence.** “In other words, the most important material of the age could not be made by any kingdom out of what lay within its own borders.”

   Tin deposits and prehistoric exploitation are documented in Anatolia. Their contribution to later supplies is debated, but “any kingdom” cannot be sustained. Indeed, the same 2022 study apparently used for the following provenance story attributes two-thirds of Uluburun’s tin to the Taurus Mountains in Turkey. That attribution is disputed too: the defensible point is widespread dependence on imported tin, not universal geological impossibility. [Powell and colleagues, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9710885/).

4. **Disputed provenance and wrong country — high confidence.** “About a third of it came from a valley in what is now Uzbekistan, more than three thousand kilometres from the place where the ship went down.”

   The proposed source, Mushiston, is in **Tajikistan**. More importantly, its identification as the source of this proportion of the cargo is contested by a detailed published rebuttal. The cargo’s precise origins should not be presented as settled. [Berger and colleagues, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1211478/full).

5. **Misleading contrast between survivors and palaces — high confidence.** “These were places that lived by trade and had no vast palace to feed.” “Their merchants worked for themselves.”

   Phoenician cities retained kings and royal households, and rulers participated substantially in commerce. Independent merchants existed, but the contrast between palace economies that failed and independent traders who survived is too clean. The *Wenamun* narrative, for example, places the king of Byblos directly in the timber trade. [Peter van Alfen, *Phoenician Trade: An Overview*](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/AUTHORS/PhoenicianTrade-vAlfen2015.pdf).

6. **Factual overclaim and contradiction with chapter 9 — high confidence.** “It was the first time that anything like an international system had existed: a club of states that recognised each other, with shared rules, a shared language for business, and a constant flow of goods between them.”

   Earlier diplomatic systems existed, including those documented at Ebla and Mari. Chapter 9 has already described kingdoms that “made alliances, broke them, and fought short wars,” supported by an actual diplomatic letter. Chapter 8 also established long-distance international commerce. The new feature needs to be the scale or particular organization of this system, not the existence of anything resembling one. [Amanda Podany, *Brotherhood of Kings*](https://institutes.abu.edu.ng/idr/public/assets/docs/Brotherhood%20of%20Kings_%20How%20International%20Relations%20Shaped%20the%20Ancient%20Near%20East%20%28%20PDFDrive%20%29.pdf).

7. **Overstatement of Hittite disappearance — high confidence.** “Its line of kings ends, its archives end, and its language was never written again.”

   The imperial administration at Hattusa ended, but branches of the Hittite royal dynasty survived, notably at Carchemish. The disappearance of written Hittite should be distinguished from the continuation of dynasties and traditions, including writing in Luwian. Otherwise the sentence makes the whole political and cultural inheritance disappear. [Metropolitan Museum, *The Hittites*](https://www.metmuseum.org/ja/essays/the-hittites).

8. **Invented necessity in the illustrative supply chain — high confidence.** “A smith there melts it with copper, which arrived by another road from another country.”

   Anatolia had copper resources and an internal copper trade. An Anatolian smith did not necessarily need foreign copper as well as imported tin. The example builds an extra international dependency into the story without evidence. [Metropolitan Museum, Old Assyrian caravan account](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/325851).

9. **Wrong continuity of rulers — high confidence.** “Some years later, the two kings signed a treaty of peace and friendship, the oldest between great powers whose text survives.”

   “The two kings” makes them the combatants just described. Ramesses II fought **Muwatalli II** at Kadesh; he concluded the later treaty with **Hattusili III**. The change of Hittite ruler matters here. [Australian Museum, *New Kingdom Egypt*](https://media.australian.museum/media/dd/documents/New_Kingdom_Egypt_Amenhotep_III_to_the_Death_of_Ramesses_II.7a75f83.pdf).

10. **Speculation presented as a known population history — medium-high confidence.** “They were a mixture of the displaced: farmers whose crops had failed, soldiers whose palaces could no longer pay them, sailors who had turned to plunder.” “They came with their families because they had nowhere to go back to.”

    These are plausible reconstructions, but the evidence does not establish this occupational breakdown or the migrants’ individual motives. Depictions of accompanying families support migration; they do not prove that everyone had lost their home. Keep the distinction between evidence and reconstruction. [Knapp and Manning, *Crisis in Context*](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.3764/aja.120.1.0099).

11. **Chronological compression that misleads — high confidence.** “Their ships went on sailing, and with their overlords gone they sailed further than anyone had before, the whole length of the Mediterranean.”

    This makes the major western Phoenician expansion sound like an immediate consequence of the collapse. The Mediterranean-wide network developed over subsequent centuries; the Met places trading posts around the entire Mediterranean by the late eighth century BCE. Signal that elapsed time. “Further than anyone had before” also requires evidence beyond the existence of Phoenician voyages. [Metropolitan Museum, *The Phoenicians*](https://www.metmuseum.org/ko/essays/the-phoenicians-1500-300-b-c).

12. **Wrong object — high confidence.** “Among the smaller finds were beads of amber from the Baltic Sea, swords of two different countries, and a gold ring bearing the name of an Egyptian queen.”

    The object bearing Nefertiti’s name is a **gold scarab**, not a ring. [Institute of Nautical Archaeology, Uluburun excavation](https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/).

13. **False absolute — high confidence.** “The city was never lived in again.”

    Ugarit did not recover as its former Bronze Age city, but the site had smaller later occupations, including in the Iron Age, Hellenistic, Roman and Ottoman periods. “Never lived in again” is exactly the kind of exception-breaking absolute prohibited by `style.md`. [French Ministry of Culture, Ugarit–Ras Shamra](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/proche-orient/en/ugarit-ras-shamra).

14. **Doubtful characterization of the wreck — medium-high confidence.** “All of it was in one modest ship, on what seems to have been an ordinary run.”

    We do not know that this was an ordinary commercial shipment. The excavators characterize it as an **elite shipment**, carrying luxury goods for a particularly wealthy audience, potentially involving royal gift exchange. Its representative value does not establish an ordinary voyage. [Institute of Nautical Archaeology](https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/).

15. **Uncertain discovery story treated as fact — high confidence about the uncertainty.** “In 1887, a woman digging for fertiliser in the ruins of an Egyptian city found a hoard of clay tablets.”

    Contemporary discovery accounts are contradictory: they variously identify a woman, farmers or undocumented excavations. The secure fact is that the tablets came to light in 1887. Under the style guide’s instruction to settle minor uncertainties before writing, simplify the anecdote rather than narrating one version as established. [Metropolitan Museum, *The Amarna Letters*](https://www.metmuseum.org/ko/essays/the-amarna-letters).

16. **Internal arithmetic contradiction — certain.** “Each chariot needed a pair, and a spare pair behind them.” “A king with a thousand chariots was therefore paying for two or three thousand war horses, thousands of specialists, a mountain of grain and tonnes of bronze.”

    The first sentence requires **four thousand horses**, not two or three thousand. Establish whether the example includes a complete replacement team and make the calculation consistent.

17. **Confusing explanation of bronze proportions — certain.** “Melt nine parts of copper with one part of tin, and the result is bronze.” “The ship off the Turkish coast was carrying its two ingredients in exactly the right proportion, ten of copper to one of tin.”

    Both mixtures can produce bronze, but they are different ratios. After the heading and recipe establish nine-to-one, “exactly” makes ten-to-one look like an unnoticed error. Explain that bronze recipes varied and the cargo supplied roughly the appropriate proportions. The excavators report ten-to-one. [Institute of Nautical Archaeology](https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/).

18. **Reader loses track of the king.** “On the wall of his temple, the king describes a league of peoples from the islands and coasts to the north, who came against Egypt by land and by sea.”

    The preceding Egyptian king was the participant in the chariot battle and treaty. Nothing explicitly tells a general reader that this is now **Ramesses III**, roughly a century after Kadesh, rather than Ramesses II. This is a place where naming the ruler, or clearly identifying him as a later king, earns its space.

19. **Reader lacks the link to earlier terminology.** “The king of Egypt and the king of Hatti wrote to each other in the language of Babylon, in wedge-shaped signs on clay.”

    Chapter 8 introduced **Akkadian** by name. Here “the language of Babylon” leaves the reader unsure whether this is the same language or a new one. Identifying it as Akkadian would connect existing knowledge without making a prohibited chapter cross-reference.

20. **Clear signpost sentences prohibited by `style.md`.** “They show what the end looked like from the inside.” “The broad answer does not depend on it.”

    The first announces what the immediately following letters will demonstrate; the second discusses the answer instead of stating it. Both can disappear without losing historical content.
