# Chapter 12 — Why were the Assyrians so cruel?

**Line under the title:** Northern Iraq and the lands around it, from about 2,900 years ago to about 2,600 years ago.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,500 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Iron ore lies in the ground of most countries, and a clay furnace, charcoal and a pair of bellows will turn it into metal without any trade in tin. That made metal cheap, and cheap metal armed and equipped far larger armies than bronze had. The kingdom that used this best was Assyria, which for three centuries sent an army out almost every year. A siege was still slow and costly, as the ramp at Lachish shows. So the Assyrian kings set out to win without one. They punished rebels in public, carved the punishment on their palace walls and wrote it into their annals, so that the next city would decide that paying tribute was cheaper than fighting. The cruelty was a calculation, and it was aimed. Those who gave in kept their thrones, the ordinary people of a city that fought were marched away to be useful somewhere else, and the worst was kept for the leaders of revolts. Behind the terror stood a working state: provinces, a relay post, a library. It lasted while the army kept winning. When it stopped, nobody had a reason to save it.

## The story

1. **A room in the palace.** Answer first. The Lachish room at Nineveh and what is carved on its walls.
2. **A furnace that cannot melt its metal.** How a bloomery works, step by step, and what the smith does with the bloom. Why iron is no better than bronze and far cheaper.
3. **The yearly campaign.** Ashur's kingdom recovers and goes to war every summer. Iron by the tonne, armies by the hundred thousand, and the king's own boasts.
4. **The mound at Lachish.** Judah stops paying. The ramp of three million stones, the arrowheads, the counter-ramp. The carving and the ground agree.
5. **Who the pictures were for.** The three treatments on one campaign. Visitors to the palace. Were the Assyrians crueller than others, or louder?
6. **A land like your own.** The officer at the wall. Deportation as policy: who was moved, how, and why. What it did to the languages of the empire.
7. **The king's road.** Provinces, governors, and letters that changed riders at each station.
8. **The king who could read.** The library at Nineveh, what was in it and what it was for. The garden with the head in the tree.
9. **Who will grieve for her.** Civil war, Babylon and the Medes, the gate full of bodies, the chiselled face.
