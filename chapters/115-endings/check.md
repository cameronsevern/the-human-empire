1. **Factual error — slavery and machinery.** “It took both the machines that made slavery less necessary and the men and women who decided that it was an abomination, and even then it took a war.”

   In the American case implied by “a war,” machinery helped expand slavery: the cotton gin increased enslavers’ demand for labour, while textile mills enlarged the market for slave-grown cotton. Abolition cannot safely be explained as machinery making slavery unnecessary. **Confidence: high.** [US National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/cotton-gin-patent).

2. **Factual overstatement — disease becomes the entire explanation.** “The Mexica and the Inca lost their governments to conquest and nine tenths of their populations to disease.”

   A roughly 90% decline is an estimate for the Indigenous population of the Americas over approximately a century, not an established identical disease-only toll for these two populations. Epidemics interacted with warfare, famine and colonial disruption. The earlier sentence assigning nine deaths in ten to diseases has the same problem. **Confidence: high on the correction; moderate on any precise population estimate.** [UCL’s account of the demographic research](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2019/feb/great-dying-americas-disturbed-earths-climate).

3. **Factual error and broken absolute — 1989.** “What they had lost was anyone willing to shoot.”

   Romanian army and police units fired on demonstrators in December 1989. Refusal to repress was crucial in some countries, but this sentence applies it to all the eastern European governments just mentioned. **Confidence: high.** [US Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1989-1992/fall-of-communism).

4. **Factual error and contradiction with chapter 27 — Britain’s chronology.** “Houses were of wood and thatch, coins had gone out of use, and nobody was building in stone.”

   “Two hundred years later,” following 476, places this around 676. By then Anglo-Saxons were minting coins, and stone churches were being built: Escomb dates to about 670–675. Chapter 27 places the severe contraction around **500** and says stone construction “had all but ceased”; this chapter shifts the date and removes the qualification. **Confidence: high.** [Ashmolean Museum](https://anglosaxondiscovery.ashmus.ox.ac.uk/Life/dailylife/coin_info.html), [Escomb Church](https://escombchurch.co.uk/about/), [chapter 27](chapters/27-fall/chapter.md).

5. **Factual error and contradiction with chapter 11 — invention of the alphabet.** “When the palaces of the Bronze Age burned, Egypt staggered on, and in the wreckage small peoples on the fringes, who had never mattered, invented the alphabet and learned to work iron.”

   Alphabetic writing predates the collapse by centuries. Chapter 11 correctly says people were **already using** a simpler alphabetic script. Expansion of its use after the collapse is different from invention then. **Confidence: high.** [Archaeological evidence in *Antiquity*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/early-alphabetic-writing-in-the-ancient-near-east-the-missing-link-from-tel-lachish/C73F769B7CF3A7E4E2607958A096B7D8), [chapter 11](chapters/11-bronze/chapter.md).

6. **Factual distortion and contradiction with chapter 50 — Zunghar deaths.** “In the 1750s the Chinese emperor, after decades of war with a nation of nomads on his western frontier called the Zunghars, ordered that they be wiped out, and about four fifths of them were killed, starved or scattered.”

   This drops smallpox, the largest component of the estimate used in chapter 50, and introduces starvation instead. That earlier account gives roughly 30% killed by soldiers and 40% dead from disease, with survivors fleeing or being enslaved. The estimates are uncertain, but this is not a faithful summary of them. **Confidence: high on the mismatch; moderate on exact proportions.** [Peter Perdue, *China Marches West*](https://www.battle-of-qurman.com.cn/literature/Perdue-2005.pdf), [chapter 50](chapters/50-russia/chapter.md).

7. **Factual overstatement — Maya knowledge.** “The Maya kings did not know that they lived at the mercy of a cycle of rainfall, and went on raising temples as the reservoirs dried.”

   The Maya built sophisticated reservoirs to manage seasonal scarcity and climatic instability. Lack of modern climate forecasting does not establish ignorance of dependence on rainfall. The claim about what their kings knew needs evidence; the waterworks point against its broad formulation. **Confidence: high that the sentence overclaims.** [Research on Maya reservoirs in *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2306870120).

8. **Factual overstatement and contradiction with chapter 27 — Roman understanding of prices.** “Roman emperors watched their coins lose value for two centuries without a word for inflation or a theory of what caused it.”

   They lacked modern monetary economics, but they recognised rising prices and offered explanations. Diocletian blamed avarice and imposed maximum prices. Chapter 27 already describes both that intervention and Constantine’s durable gold currency. “Without … a theory” incorrectly turns an inadequate explanation into no explanation. **Confidence: high.** [T. D. Barnes on Diocletian](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~wstevens/history331texts/barnes.html), [chapter 27](chapters/27-fall/chapter.md).

9. **Factual errors and statistical compression — modern progress.** “In the space of a single lifetime it has doubled the length of the average human life, cut the share of children who die from one in five to one in twenty-five, taught most of its members to read, and brought its own numbers, which once seemed bound to grow until they starved, to the point of levelling off.”

   Global life expectancy rose from about 46 years in 1950 to 73 in 2023, not twice its former level within that lifetime. More than doubling requires a baseline around 1900. Population has not yet levelled off: the UN projects approximately two billion additional people before a peak in the mid-2080s. The child-mortality figures also need an age threshold and dates. This breaks “one number per point” by bundling distinct trends with incompatible timescales. **Confidence: high.** [WHO](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/gho-documents/world-health-statistic-reports/2023/world-health-statistics-2023_20230519_.pdf), [UN population projections](https://www.un.org/sustainabledevelopment/blog/2024/07/press-release-wpp2024/).

10. **Factual error — nonviolent movements had material power.** “Gandhi's marchers, the Black Americans who sat down at lunch counters, the crowds with candles in Leipzig had no material power whatever.”

    Nonviolence does not mean absence of material power. The Greensboro campaign, for example, combined sit-ins with an economic boycott. Organisation, purchasing power and economic disruption mattered alongside persuasion. **Confidence: high.** [Smithsonian’s Greensboro account](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/Greensboro-Lunch-Counter).

11. **Factual overstatement — Indian acceptance of British rule.** “It worked for as long as both sides thought it natural, and it was finished once an old man in a loincloth had shown that it was not.”

    Resistance long preceded Gandhi, and independence followed interacting pressures: organised nationalism, wartime costs, challenges to military loyalty and political decisions in Britain. This makes colonial rule depend on a shared belief that demonstrably did not encompass both populations. **Confidence: high.** [National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/teaching-resources/indian-independence-part-one/), [UK Parliament](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/parliament-and-empire/parliament-and-the-american-colonies-before-1765/the-raj-and-independence-1914-47/).

12. **Factual error — nobody defended Qin.** “The first emperor of China unified the country by terror, and his dynasty outlived him by four years, because nobody would lift a finger for it.”

    Qin armies fought substantial campaigns against the rebellions. Zhang Han defeated rebel forces before eventually surrendering. The dynasty’s rapid collapse is real; universal unwillingness to defend it is not. **Confidence: high.** [*China at War*, entries on the Qin campaigns](https://www.battle-of-qurman.com.cn/literature/Li-2012.pdf).

13. **Factual overstatement and contradiction with chapter 11 — universal Bronze Age collapse.** “The palaces of the Bronze Age, three thousand years ago, depended on one another for tin and copper and grain, and when drought, earthquakes and raiders struck several of them in the same decades, they all went down together within a lifetime.”

    Chapter 11 explicitly distinguishes destroyed kingdoms from Egypt and Assyria, which survived, and Cyprus, which recovered quickly. “All” erases the uneven outcome that the earlier chapter explains. **Confidence: high.** [Chapter 11, “Who came through”](chapters/11-bronze/chapter.md).

14. **Factual compression — Maya collapse.** “The cities of the Maya emptied when the rains failed for a century.”

    This suggests one continuous century without rain and a simultaneous abandonment of Maya cities. Evidence instead shows repeated drought episodes and regionally staggered political and demographic collapses. Drought was a major contributor, not a sufficient description of the whole process. **Confidence: high.** [*Ancient Mesoamerica*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ancient-mesoamerica/article/drought-and-the-maya-collapse/B6B6AE2657A4635D65D520142D402DC1), [research review](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-earth-060115-012512).

15. **Factual error — Alexander’s timetable.** “Persia was the largest empire the world had seen, and a king of twenty-two from a small northern country took it apart in eleven years.”

    Alexander invaded in 334 BCE; the Achaemenid monarchy ended in 330. Eleven years approximately spans the invasion to Alexander’s death, including later campaigning beyond the conquest of Persia. **Confidence: high.** [Getty’s chronology](https://www.getty.edu/art/exhibitions/persia/explore.html).

16. **Misleading identification — Indigenous allies.** “The Mexica ruled millions and were overthrown by a few hundred Spaniards, together with tens of thousands of their own resentful subjects.”

    Some allies were former subjects, but the crucial Tlaxcalans were independent enemies of the Mexica. The sentence makes the Indigenous coalition sound entirely like a revolt by imperial subjects. **Confidence: high.** [Research on independent Tlaxcala](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/latin-american-antiquity/article/aztec-and-tlaxcalan-economic-interaction-blockade-or-interregional-exchange/CBE14EE68216FC6CA5BC5C8F2A1BD3C3), [Smithsonian](https://www.si.edu/spotlight/mexican-america/history).

17. **Broken absolute — external dangers.** “Whatever ends this empire will have been done by its own people.”

    No external human society does not mean no external danger. An asteroid impact is an obvious counterexample; natural catastrophes also need not originate in human choices. The preceding “there is no blow coming from outside” makes the same unwarranted move. **Confidence: high.** [NASA’s planetary-defence overview](https://science.nasa.gov/planetary-defense-overview/).

18. **Chronological distortion — settlement and writing.** “Almost the first thing it did, on settling down, was to start keeping records: a clay tablet, a count of barley, a clerk's name.”

    Written tablets and personal names arrived thousands of years after settled farming began. Earlier accounting tokens offer a defensible connection, but they are not the written tablet described here. **Confidence: high.** [Denise Schmandt-Besserat’s chronology of tokens and writing](https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/the-evolution-of-writing/).

19. **Internal contradiction and broken absolute — survival after conquest.** “Where they wanted the land empty, there was no afterwards.”

    This equates genocidal intention with complete success. It also conflicts with the chapter’s own emphasis on surviving Indigenous languages and peoples. Intent to eliminate a population does not establish that nobody survived or rebuilt. The sentence needs to distinguish destruction from literal extinction.

20. **Reader confusion — an unexplained change in the meaning of “empire.”** “The human empire of the present is the whole species, joined into a single system.”

    The opening defines imperial endings as failures of governments. Here “empire” becomes an interconnected species without a common government. Readers need a bridge explaining which features of political empires apply to this metaphor and which do not; otherwise the historical comparison appears to prove more than it does.

21. **Reader confusion — gunboats are presented as an ending.** “The Chinese empire met British gunboats on its rivers, and the Ottoman empire chose the losing side in a world war.”

    The Ottoman example names a war immediately preceding imperial dissolution. The Chinese example supplies an encounter, but no explanation connecting it to the later fall of the Qing. A general reader can easily mistake the Opium Wars for the event that ended imperial government. Explain the intervening connection or select a more direct example.

22. **Reader confusion — the unnamed capital.** “Its commander killed the boy's father and marched on the capital.”

    The next paragraph mentioning a city says the Senate met in Rome, so readers may assume Rome was the capital being approached. Chapter 27 established Ravenna, but that was many chapters ago. Naming Ravenna here would orient the scene without adding an unnecessary person.

23. **Unsupported inference — historical frequency becomes future probability.** “History suggests that the slow way is more probable, because that is how it has usually happened.”

    The chapter has just argued that a globally interconnected civilisation is unprecedented. It cannot then derive comparative probabilities for its collapse from a loosely defined collection of earlier imperial endings without explaining the basis. The passage is already labelled guesswork; the problem is the additional claim that history establishes which scenario is likelier.

24. **Style violation — announcing a verdict.** “If a verdict is wanted, it is this.”

    This directly breaks the prohibition on signpost sentences. The following sentence states the verdict and can stand without this introduction.

25. **Style violation — the chapter discusses its own construction.** “Everything up to this point has rested on evidence.” “What follows is guesswork, and should be read as such.”

    Marking speculation is required by the outline, but these sentences do it through prohibited self-reference and signposting. The first also overclaims: much of the preceding material is interpretation. Mark the uncertainty in the predictions themselves.

26. **Style violation — additional announcements instead of substance.** “Looked at together, the endings in this story fall into a small number of kinds.” “One argument has run under this whole story.” “The second new thing runs the other way.”

    These announce a classification, an argument and a reversal before supplying them. The last is especially opaque: readers must retain the earlier numbering and infer what “the other way” means. State the actual relationship.
