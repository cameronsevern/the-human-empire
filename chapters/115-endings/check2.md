## Factual flags

1. **Major — earlier governments did measure threats.**
   
   > “Its rulers could feel trouble coming, but they could not measure it, and so they often misread it.”
   
   The categorical inability to measure is false. Earlier governments measured conditions affecting food supplies and revenue. Egypt’s nilometers measured flood levels to guide preparation of canals and dykes and determine taxation; the surviving Rawda instrument was commissioned by an Abbasid caliph in 861. Modern measurement is vastly more extensive, but measurement itself is not the distinction. [Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities](https://egymonuments.gov.eg/en/monuments/rawda-island-nilometer/).

2. **Major — catastrophic population loss did not leave successors little to inherit.**
   
   > “In these cases there was little left for any successor to inherit.”
   
   Applied to the immediately following Mexica and Inca examples, this is misleading. Spanish colonial government retained and adapted Indigenous communities, local authorities, tribute arrangements, and labour institutions. Severe demographic destruction coexisted with substantial institutional inheritance. The chapter itself also describes surviving Nahuatl speakers and Andean agriculture. [*Mesoamerican Voices*, Cambridge University Press](https://assets.cambridge.org/052181/2798/excerpt/0521812798_excerpt.htm), [research on colonial Andean labour and tribute](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/free-and-unfree-labour-in-the-colonial-andes-in-the-sixteenth-and-seventeenth-centuries/B00B9D39F97F968E9F6655462D8FB723).

3. **Major — Egyptian farming did not remain unchanged apart from its rulers.**
   
   > “The farm, the river, the crops and most of the customs were the same throughout. Only the master who took the share kept changing.”
   
   Across the stated span—from pharaohs to British consuls—crops, irrigation, agricultural organisation, language, and religion changed substantially. One concrete counterexample is the nineteenth-century expansion of long-staple cotton and irrigation works under Muhammad Ali, which transformed agriculture and the wider economy. Continuity of farming along the Nile is defensible; “only” the recipient of taxes changing is not. [Study in *The Developing Economies*](https://www.ide.go.jp/library/English/Publish/Periodicals/De/pdf/72_02_04.pdf).

4. **Major — a small annual risk need not become large within a century.**
   
   > “A small risk run every year becomes a large one over a century.”
   
   The cumulative probability depends on the annual probability. For independent years with constant risk \(p\), it is \(1-(1-p)^{100}\). An annual risk of one in a million becomes approximately one in ten thousand over a century: larger, but still small. The defensible claim is that repeated exposure increases cumulative risk, not that it necessarily makes it large. [NIST’s binomial probability formula](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda366i.htm).

5. **Minor — “then” misplaces the oil-price collapse.**
   
   > “The Soviet Union lived for its last decade on the sale of oil, and then the price fell.”
   
   The decisive price collapse occurred in 1986, during the Soviet Union’s final decade, not after it. The new transition obscures the timing of the pressure it is meant to explain. [US Energy Information Administration, 1986 outlook](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/archives/3Q86.pdf).

6. **Major — global disease spread is neither universal nor that uniformly fast.**
   
   > “A new disease is in every country within weeks.”
   
   This unchanged sentence is a factual absolute, not merely a confident interpretation. Many new diseases never spread worldwide. Even COVID-19 had reported cases in 114 countries by 11 March 2020, more than two months after the initial outbreak reports, rather than every country within weeks. Rapid international spread is possible; universal spread on this timetable is not a general fact. [WHO situation report, 11 March 2020](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/coronaviruse/situation-reports/20200311-sitrep-51-covid-19.pdf).

## Clarity flags

- **An unclear contrast:** “British rule in India shows the same thing from the other side.” The reader cannot tell what “the other side” means: consent rather than fear, subjects rather than rulers, or successful administration rather than collapse.

- **An unnamed referent:** “The older collapse had an outside too.” The preceding paragraph concerns Rome; the Bronze Age collapse was discussed much earlier. Naming it immediately would avoid a momentary backward search.

- **A signpost without substance:** “Their strength lay elsewhere.” This announces the explanation supplied by the next sentence rather than helping the reader understand it.

- **Repeated explanatory tags:** “The losses, in other words”; “The Spaniards, in other words”; “Materials, in other words”; “Material interest, in other words.” Four occurrences make the new connective voice conspicuous. Several repeat an already explicit point.

- **Padding rather than useful breathing room:** “Weapons, after all, are useless without hands willing to use them” repeats the immediately preceding sentence about willingness to shoot. “All of them, in the end, went home” likewise repeats the withdrawals just described.

- **Repeated opening wording:** “How the present order might end is a matter of guesswork. There are fast ways for the present order to end…” Repeating “the present order … end” in consecutive sentences slows the opening without clarifying it.

- **A broken geographical absolute:** “A system that covers the whole planet has no neighbour, and so whatever happens to it happens everywhere.” Interconnection does not make every disturbance universal. The sentence also blurs worldwide consequences with identical local experiences.

- **Ambiguous potato attribution:** “Yet the potato that its farmers had bred in the Andes…” can imply that Inca imperial farmers originated the crop. Potato domestication began thousands of years before the empire. If later breeding and cultivation are intended, the sentence should distinguish them from domestication. [FAO’s history of the potato](https://www.fao.org/newsroom/story/the-potatos-travel-through-ages-and-continents/).

- **Potentially misleading duration:** “The Soviet army went into Afghanistan with tanks and helicopters and came out nine years later, and the American army did the same thing a generation after that.” “The same thing” can accidentally carry over the nine-year duration, although the American intervention lasted about twenty years.

I found no clear hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-reference, or chapter-number cross-references in the revised text. The earlier review’s repaired examples are not repeated here.
