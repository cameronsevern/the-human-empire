1. **Factual error; absolute — high confidence.** “Every island had its conspiracies and risings, and every one was put down with torture and public executions.”  
   The uprising in Saint-Domingue began in 1791 and culminated in independent Haiti in 1804. Leaving that story for later does not justify saying every rising failed. Limit the claim to particular suppressed rebellions. [Library of Congress](https://guides.loc.gov/francophone-caribbean-collections-library-of-congress/haiti).

2. **Factual error; absolute — high confidence.** “On the sugar islands, deaths outnumbered births in every year for which records exist.”  
   Barbados recorded natural population increase before emancipation: births exceeded deaths across the registration periods from 1817 to 1832. Sustained population decline was widespread, but neither universal nor uninterrupted. This also directly breaks the rule against absolutes defeated by one exception. [Richard Sheridan, *Doctors and Slaves*, demographic tables](https://www.academia.edu/165803140/Doctors_and_Slaves_A_Medical_and_Demographic_History_of_Slavery_in_the_British_West_Indies_1680_1834).

3. **Doubtful central generalisation — high confidence about the geographical problem.** “They grew it on tropical islands with the labour of enslaved Africans, and they drove the price down until the poorest people in Europe could afford it every day.”  
   The evidence presented concerns England, whose consumption was exceptionally high. Continental Europe lagged substantially behind. The defensible claim is that plantation production helped turn sugar into an everyday purchase for many British working households; “the poorest people in Europe” extends that conclusion beyond the evidence. [Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence*, discussion of sugar consumption](https://dokumen.pub/the-great-divergence-china-europe-and-the-making-of-the-modern-world-economy-9781400813025-9780691090108-9780691005430.html).

4. **Chronology mismatch with earlier chapters — medium confidence.** “We eat so much sugar because, about four hundred years ago, Europeans found a way to make a rare spice in enormous quantities.”  
   This dates the discovery to roughly 1600. Chapter 44 already describes Madeira’s sugar cultivation and enslaved African labour in the 1400s; chapter 52 explicitly places that system before Columbus. Chapter 53 itself subsequently locates the method’s development on Portuguese islands. Distinguish the earlier development of the system from its seventeenth-century Caribbean expansion. Sources: [chapter 44](chapters/44-columbus/chapter.md), [chapter 52](chapters/52-slave-trade/chapter.md). This is a continuity problem rather than an unavoidable contradiction if “enormous quantities” means a later increase in scale.

5. **Factual overstatement; absolute — high confidence.** “Europe had seen nothing like this.”  
   The following explanation identifies concentrated labour, specialised tasks and coordinated production as the novelty. Europe already had substantial industrial workplaces, notably Venice’s Arsenal, with organised production stages and standardised components before the Caribbean sugar boom. The plantation–factory comparison works; the assertion of no European precedent does not. [European Route of Industrial Heritage: Arsenal](https://www.erih.net/i-want-to-go-there/site/arsenal).

6. **Production error and explanatory gap — high confidence.** “The syrup was then poured into barrels to cool, and the dark liquid that drained out of them, molasses, was distilled into rum.”  
   This skips the stage that actually produces the sugar crystals. In the process described by William Clark, concentrated syrup went into cooling vessels and was stirred while crystallising; the resulting mass was subsequently packed into draining barrels. Readers need that intermediate step to understand why molasses drains away while sugar remains. [Clark’s contemporary description](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/12866-314ec8475ef18ce/), [St. Croix Landmarks Society’s production account](https://www.stcroixlandmarks.org/history/plantation-life/).

7. **Mislabelled population figure — high confidence.** “By 1860 their descendants numbered four million.”  
   Approximately four million was the **enslaved** population of the United States. The census counted about 4.44 million Black people altogether, including approximately 488,000 free people. “Their descendants” obscures the distinction, particularly when the passage compares this number with Jamaica’s population at emancipation. Specify that the comparison concerns enslaved populations. [U.S. Census Bureau, 1860 table](https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2002/demo/pop-twps0056/tableA-19.pdf).

8. **Doubtful account of language formation — high confidence.** “They responded by making new languages, built from African grammar and European words, which their descendants still speak.”  
   Caribbean creoles developed through contact among African and European languages over generations. Their grammar cannot generally be assigned to Africa and their vocabulary to Europe as separate components; European grammatical contributions also matter, and accounts of their formation remain debated. The preceding sentence also makes deliberate ethnic mixing sound like a uniform policy, which this explanation does not establish. [Salikoko Mufwene, “Pidgins and Creoles”](https://mufwene.uchicago.edu/mufw_pdgcreo.html).

9. **Chronological conflation — medium-high confidence.** “Grocers advertised sugar from India instead, and potters sold sugar bowls with a message painted on the side to say that the contents had not been made by slaves.”  
   Its placement immediately after “Within a year” assigns the bowls to 1791–92. The familiar surviving examples belong to the renewed campaign of the 1820s: London Museum dates its ceramic bowl to 1825, and the V&A dates its glass example to about 1820–30. Supply evidence for an earlier bowl or distinguish the later campaign. [London Museum](https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/v/object-44419/bowl-sugar-bowl/), [V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/museum-life/an-anti-slavery-sugar-bowl).

10. **Overgeneralisation about the Maroons — high confidence.** “In return the Maroons agreed to send back any future runaways, and they kept the bargain.”  
    Returning fugitives was a treaty obligation, and Maroons did capture and return many. But their conduct varied between communities and over time; runaways also joined the Trelawny Town Maroons during the war of 1795–96. “They kept the bargain” turns a complicated relationship into uninterrupted collective compliance. [University of Southampton, *After the treaties*](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/423482/).

11. **Chronological error — high confidence.** “In the 1600s, Europeans acquired three hot drinks within a single lifetime.”  
    Chocolate had reached Spain during the sixteenth century, with documented commercial imports by 1585. The seventeenth-century convergence works better for England than for Europe collectively. Name the relevant population rather than treating European adoption as one event. [Harvard’s *ReVista*, history of chocolate’s arrival](https://revista.drclas.harvard.edu/between-america-and-europe/).

12. **Overgeneralised growing time — high confidence.** “It takes well over a year to ripen.”  
    Cane’s growing period varies with climate, variety and whether it is newly planted or regrowing after harvest. FAO gives a usual initial period of 14–18 months but about twelve months for the subsequent regrowth crop, with a wider overall range. “Well over a year” should not describe every crop. [FAO crop profile](https://ecocrop.apps.fao.org/ecocrop/srv/en/cropView?id=1884).

13. **Reader orientation.** “The French did the same on Martinique, Guadeloupe and, largest of all, the western end of the island that Columbus had first settled.”  
    Name Hispaniola and the French colony of Saint-Domingue, now Haiti. Chapter 44 does not give readers enough geographical grounding to decode this description reliably. “Largest of all” also leaves them unsure whether the comparison concerns territory, production or wealth.

14. **Reader orientation.** “The work was done four thousand miles away, by people the customer would never meet, and what arrived in the shop was a clean white cone wrapped in blue paper.”  
    The production account has left readers with sugar and molasses in barrels. They now encounter a white cone without any explanation of refining or moulding. A brief bridge explaining that raw sugar was refined and formed into sugarloaves would connect the plantation to the shop.

15. **Style: unsupported historical superlative.** “No other food in history has gone so far so fast, from the table of a king to the cupboard of a pauper.”  
    This asserts a measurable world-historical record without defining speed, social distance or the comparison set. The problem is the universal claim, not the confident voice. State sugar’s striking transformation directly without claiming that no other food matched it.

16. **Style: signpost sentence.** “The figures are stark.”  
    This announces how readers should receive the evidence instead of giving it. The next sentence already supplies the point. It falls under the prohibition on announcing an approaching point or surprise.
