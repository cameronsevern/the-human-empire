# Chapter 53 — Why do we eat so much sugar?

**Line under the title:** The Caribbean and the tables of Europe, from about 1450 to 1800.
**Length:** 3,600 to 4,400 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Sugar was a rare spice until Europeans learned to grow it on tropical islands with enslaved labour. The plantation that made it was something new: land, borrowed money and forced workers joined in a round-the-clock routine set by the cane, which spoils within a day of being cut. It was a factory before there were factories, and it killed its workers faster than they could be replaced. What it produced, together with tea, coffee, chocolate and tobacco, were the first goods from other continents that ordinary Europeans bought every week. A luxury of kings became the cheap fuel of the poor, and the people who stirred it into their tea did not have to see how it was made.

## The story

1. **Opening: a tablecloth made of sugar.** Answer first. A royal banquet in Venice in 1574 where everything on the table is sugar.
2. **The reed that gives honey.** Where sugar came from, why it was rare, and why it needs so much labour and fuel.
3. **The island.** From the Atlantic islands to Brazil to Barbados, which went from tobacco and English servants to sugar and African slaves in one generation.
4. **Before the juice sours.** How sugar is made: the cane's clock, the mill, the boiling house. A factory in a field.
5. **Cheaper to buy.** The plantation as an investment. Why the islands consumed people: a million landed in Jamaica, three hundred thousand alive at the end.
6. **Sunday market.** What the enslaved made for themselves: gardens, markets, languages, religion, and the free villages in the mountains.
7. **The bitter cup.** Tea, coffee, chocolate and tobacco. The coffee house. How much sugar the English ate in 1700, 1800 and 1900. A labourer's breakfast.
8. **Ending: two ounces.** The boycott of 1791 and what the customer had not had to see.
