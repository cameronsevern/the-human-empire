## Changed facts

No existing number, name, date or quotation was changed or removed. However, several connecting sentences add factual claims or strengthen existing ones. For additions without an old equivalent, I quote the relevant old context.

1. **The beginning of falling prices**
   - **Old:** “They found the third on the African mainland, where captives could be bought.”
   - **New addition:** “With all three in hand, the price of sugar could at last begin to fall.”
   - Adds a chronological claim: prices could not begin falling before this combination existed.

2. **Access to land on Barbados**
   - **Old:** “The men who could afford mills bought out their neighbours.”
   - **New:** “At those prices, only a man with a great deal of money could buy a field, and sugar also needed a mill. The men who could afford mills therefore bought out their neighbours.”
   - Adds an absolute wealth requirement for purchasing any field and makes the causal explanation explicit.

3. **The disappearance of opportunities for poor settlers**
   - **Old:** “The small farmers left, and the English servants, when their terms ran out, left too, since there was no land for them.”
   - **New addition:** “Sugar had made the island rich, and it had left no room there for a poor Englishman.”
   - Broadens the existing claim about departing farmers and servants to poor English people generally.

4. **Transport limitations**
   - **Old:** “The mill and the boiling house had to stand in the middle of the fields, and during the harvest, which lasted about five months, they could not stop.”
   - **New:** “The cane could not be carted to some distant town, so the mill and the boiling house had to stand in the middle of the fields.”
   - Adds a categorical transport restriction.

5. **The final production stage**
   - **Old:** “The juice ran down a gutter into the boiling house.”
   - **New:** “The juice ran down a gutter into the boiling house, where the last and most skilled part of the work was done.”
   - Introduces both a ranking of skill and a claim that this was the last stage.

6. **Duration of the working regime**
   - **Old:** “Sixteen or eighteen hours of work in the twenty-four was common.”
   - **New addition:** “This went on for the five months of the harvest.”
   - Explicitly applies the described working regime throughout the harvest. The five-month figure itself was already present elsewhere.

7. **Universal financial pressure and absentee supervision**
   - **Old:** “Borrowed money has to earn its interest, and the managers were judged on the size of the crop.”
   - **New additions:** “The arrangement put every estate under pressure.” / “Neither the lender in London nor the owner in his country house was on the island to see how the crop was got.”
   - Extends the arrangement to every estate and adds physical absence as an explanation.

8. **Replacement of the workforce**
   - **Old:** “Women worked in the cane gangs beside the men and bore few children who lived.”
   - **New additions:** “Left to itself, such a population shrinks, and it could be kept up only by buying more people.” / “It did not raise its workforce. It bought one, again and again.”
   - Adds an exclusive claim about workforce replacement, going beyond births being insufficient.

9. **Population decline**
   - **Old:** “Jamaica alone received about a million people from the ships. When slavery ended there, there were just over three hundred thousand.”
   - **New addition:** “On the mainland, in other words, the number had grown many times over, while on the sugar island it had fallen by more than half.”
   - Introduces a numerical inference absent from the old text. It is invalid; see factual flag 1.

10. **Provision grounds and payment**
    - **Old:** “Planters did not want to pay for food, so most of them gave their workers plots of rough ground on the edge of the estate and one day a week to tend them.”
    - **New addition:** “The planters had meant only to save money, but the plots turned into something larger.”
    - **Old:** “Over time the enslaved became the islands’ main suppliers of fresh food.”
    - **New:** “Over time the enslaved became the islands’ main suppliers of fresh food, and they were paid for it.”
    - Adds an exclusive motive and makes payment explicit.

11. **Direction of the rum trade**
    - **Old:** “Rum went the other way.”
    - **New:** “While sugar and the hot drinks came into Europe, rum went the other way.”
    - Turns an ambiguous transition into an explicit geographical contrast.

12. **Earlier imports**
    - **Old:** “These were the first goods from other continents that ordinary Europeans bought week after week.”
    - **New:** “Taken together, these were the first goods from other continents that ordinary Europeans bought week after week. Earlier imports had not reached such people.”
    - Strengthens a claim about regular purchases into a claim that earlier imports never reached ordinary people.

Other additions explain or restate facts already present—for example, sugar remaining in the barrels, the growth of consumption, and boycott readers acting on the pamphlet. They do not alter the underlying dates or quantities.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — cumulative arrivals are mistaken for an earlier population.**

   > “On the mainland, in other words, the number had grown many times over, while on the sugar island it had fallen by more than half.”

   The million people are arrivals accumulated over centuries, not Jamaica’s population at a starting date. Comparing that total with a population at emancipation cannot establish a percentage decline. One demographic account gives approximately 354,000 enslaved people in 1808 and 311,070 in 1834: a decline of about 12%, not more than half. The broader contrast in demographic experience remains valid. [Deason and colleagues, “Interdisciplinary approach to the demography of Jamaica”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1471-2148-12-24).

2. **Major — ordinary Europeans consumed intercontinental imports long before these commodities.**

   > “Taken together, these were the first goods from other continents that ordinary Europeans bought week after week. Earlier imports had not reached such people.”

   Roman consumers depended on grain imported from Egypt and North Africa. This was not confined to luxury consumption or free distributions: much of the imported grain entered the market. The defensible distinction concerns the expansion of particular colonial consumer goods in early modern Europe, not the first arrival of overseas goods in ordinary households. [Peter Temin, “The Economy of the Early Roman Empire”](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/pdfplus/10.1257/089533006776526148).

3. **Minor — poor white inhabitants did remain on Barbados.**

   > “Sugar had made the island rich, and it had left no room there for a poor Englishman.”

   Sugar displaced many smallholders and encouraged emigration, but poor white communities remained, including in marginal rural areas unsuitable for intensive sugar cultivation. “No room” converts displacement and restricted opportunity into total exclusion. [Matthew C. Reilly, “‘Poor Whites’ on the Peripheries”](https://www.academia.edu/30137346/_Poor_Whites_on_the_Peripheries_Poor_White_and_Afro_Barbadian_Interaction_on_the_Plantation).

4. **Minor — the rum trade did not run in the opposite direction to European imports.**

   > “While sugar and the hot drinks came into Europe, rum went the other way.”

   Caribbean rum also travelled to Europe, alongside shipments to North America and Africa. If the intended point is its use in purchasing enslaved people in Africa, that destination needs naming; the present contrast misrepresents the trade’s geography. [Oxford Bibliographies, “Rum”](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62333/reference-article-abstract/574178468).

5. **Minor — the wording gives two words the same etymological root.**

   > “The word ‘sugar’ comes from the Indian word for those crystals, by way of Persian and Arabic, and so does the word ‘candy’.”

   “And so does” makes *candy* derive from the same Indian word. *Sugar* derives from Sanskrit *śarkarā*; *candy* comes through Persian *qand* and Arabic *qandī*, probably ultimately from Sanskrit *khaṇḍa*. Both have an Indian linguistic ancestry, but different roots. [“Sugar”](https://www.etymonline.com/word/sugar), [“Candy”](https://www.etymonline.com/word/candy).

## Clarity flags

- **New signposting rather than explanation:**  
  > “However, it had advantages that mattered to working people.”  
  This announces the advantages immediately before listing them. The former direct transition was clearer.

- **An announced surprise with an unclear comparison:**  
  > “It might be expected that the rich led this climb. In fact, the poor took to it hardest.”  
  This stages a surprise without explaining whether “hardest” means greater consumption, faster growth, or greater dietary dependence.

- **Padding:**  
  > “What followed was one of the fastest transformations that any society has undergone, and it happened one step at a time.”  
  “One step at a time” adds nothing. It also weakens the force of the preceding claim about speed.

- **Vague anticipation:**  
  > “Nothing about the place suggested that it would soon be famous.”  
  “Famous” does not identify the coming change, and “nothing” is an unnecessary absolute. This adds suspense rather than orientation.

- **A production sequence that contradicts its own label:**  
  > “The juice ran down a gutter into the boiling house, where the last and most skilled part of the work was done.”  
  Cooling, crystallising and draining still follow. “Last” makes the otherwise clear sequence harder to understand.

- **A new absolute that contradicts the preceding explanation:**  
  > “It did not raise its workforce. It bought one, again and again.”  
  The preceding sentences describe surviving children, though too few to replace deaths. The distinction is between insufficient natural replacement and none.

- **Repetition around one demographic point:**  
  > “Left to itself, such a population shrinks, and it could be kept up only by buying more people.”  
  > “The slave ships, in other words, were part of how a plantation was run.”  
  > “It did not raise its workforce. It bought one, again and again.”  
  These explain substantially the same consequence three times. One explanation would give the reader enough room.

- **An unnecessary absolute about motive:**  
  > “The planters had meant only to save money, but the plots turned into something larger.”  
  “Only” claims exhaustive knowledge of motives. The earlier explanation that planters wanted to avoid food costs already establishes the point.

- **An unbroken existing absolute:**  
  > “Sugar is the taste with nothing else attached, and nobody who has tried it has ever needed to be persuaded to want more.”  
  “Nobody … ever” violates the style sheet’s explicit rule. A general human preference for sweetness does not mean every individual invariably wants more.

- **Unexplained terminology:**  
  > “The planter needed the land, the mill, the coppers, the cattle and, most expensive of all, the people.”  
  “The coppers” means the copper boiling kettles, but the term has not been introduced. A general reader may read it as money.

- **An ambiguous statistical restatement:**  
  > “In each century, in other words, the English ate several times as much as in the one before.”  
  The preceding figures are annual consumption per person at three dates, not totals for successive centuries. The added sentence obscures the measurement.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit cross-references. The earlier check’s corrected flags are not repeated here.

## Overall

The revision generally improves continuity, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. The population inference is the most serious new error; several other bridges introduce absolutes, misleading contrasts or repetition. The chapter’s central interpretation does not need weakening. No files were edited.
