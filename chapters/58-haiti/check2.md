## Changed facts

The diff changes no existing dates, named people, historical quotations, or numerical totals. It does, however, introduce factual claims and causal connections. The substantive changes are below; purely stylistic expansions and restatements are excluded. For additions, “no counterpart” means there was no equivalent sentence in the old text.

1. **Who received the wealth; population becomes ownership.**

   **Old:** “Nearly half a million enslaved people did the work. The colony had some forty thousand whites.”

   **New:** “The people who made this wealth shared in none of it.” And: “The owners, in other words, lived among a population that outnumbered them by more than ten to one.”

   This adds an absolute about economic participation and turns a comparison of population groups into a comparison involving owners.

2. **African birthplace becomes a claim about childhood and attitudes.**

   **Old:** “As a result, most of the enslaved people in Saint-Domingue in 1791 had been born in Africa. They remembered being free.”

   **New:** “As a result, most of the enslaved people in Saint-Domingue in 1791 had been born in Africa. They had not grown up in slavery and learned to think of it as the way of the world. They remembered being free.”

   The middle sentence adds a biographical and psychological generalisation.

3. **The Assembly’s authorship and motives become explicit.**

   **Old:** “In Paris the Assembly dithered.” And: “It granted rights to some free men of colour and then took them back.”

   **New:** “The National Assembly in Paris had written the words that both sides were quoting, and it could have said what they meant.” And: “Caught between the two, it granted rights to some free men of colour and then took them back.”

   This adds authorship and makes the conflict between revolutionary principles and colonial income an explicit explanation of policy.

4. **European unfamiliarity with irregular warfare.**

   **Old:** “French officers trained for European war found it baffling.”

   **New:** That sentence remains, preceded by “The fires of August were the start of a war, and it was not the kind of war that European soldiers knew,” and followed by “They were used to an enemy who lined up in a field and could be beaten there. This enemy would not stay in one place long enough to be beaten.”

   A local difficulty becomes a general claim about European military experience.

5. **Abolition becomes the exclusive means of securing the rebels’ allegiance.**

   **Old:** “In August he issued a decree declaring all the slaves in the north free.”

   **New:** “The one thing that could bring them over to France was the thing they were fighting for. In August, therefore, he issued a decree declaring all the slaves in the north free.”

   The added sentence asserts exclusivity, beyond the old account of military necessity.

6. **Disease’s unequal effects become explicit.**

   **Old:** “Soldiers fresh from England had no such protection.”

   **New:** That sentence remains, followed by “The disease therefore struck one army and largely passed over the other.”

   **Old:** “The rains came, and with them the fever. The French commander died of it.”

   **New:** “The rains came, and with them the fever. The French soldiers, like the British before them, had grown up far from it and had no protection. The French commander died of it.”

   These add an army-level comparison and extend the earlier claim about British susceptibility to French soldiers generally.

7. **Toussaint’s surrender and arrest acquire explicit causes.**

   **Old:** “Then several of his generals went over to the French, and he made peace and retired to his farm.”

   **New:** “Then several of his generals went over to the French, and with his army breaking up he made peace and retired to his farm.”

   **Old:** No counterpart.

   **New:** “The peace did not protect him, because the secret orders had not changed.”

   Army disintegration and the continued operation of secret instructions are now expressly asserted.

8. **Toussaint’s death is placed before fulfilment of his prediction.**

   **Old:** “He was taken to a fortress in the mountains near the Swiss border, nine hundred metres up, and locked in a stone cell.”

   **New:** The same sentence is preceded by “He did not live to see whether he was right.”

9. **News from Guadeloupe directly causes the officers’ coalition.**

   **Old:** “Black officers and officers of mixed descent, who had fought each other for a decade, joined forces.”

   **New:** “The same news closed an old division. Black officers and officers of mixed descent, who had fought each other for a decade, now joined forces against the French.”

   The revision identifies the news as the cause and makes the coalition’s opponent explicit.

10. **Rochambeau’s terror becomes a change of policy.**

    **Old:** “The general who took over the command tried terror.”

    **New:** “The general who took over the command tried terror instead.”

    “Instead” introduces a contrast with his predecessor.

11. **Louisiana becomes the first overseas consequence.**

    **Old:** “The effects ran far beyond the island.”

    **New:** “The effects ran far beyond the island, and the first of them was felt on the mainland of North America.”

    **Old:** “He had no further use for Louisiana, and he sold it to the United States for fifteen million dollars, which nearly doubled the size of that country overnight.”

    **New:** “Louisiana had been meant to feed Saint-Domingue, and without Saint-Domingue it had no purpose. He had no further use for it, and he sold it to the United States for fifteen million dollars, which nearly doubled the size of that country overnight.”

    The first claim is new; the second strengthens Napoleon’s assessment into an absolute statement about the territory’s purpose.

12. **British abolition gains an explicit demographic explanation and success claim.**

    **Old:** “Later it supplied the campaigners with their strongest argument, since Haiti had shown what a colony packed with newly landed Africans could become.”

    **New:** That sentence gains “however” and is followed by “The rebels of 1791 had mostly been born in Africa and carried over by the trade. A colony that went on buying captives was therefore filling itself with people who remembered freedom and might fight to get it back.”

    **Old:** “In 1807 Parliament made it illegal for British ships to carry slaves, and the navy began stopping the ships of other nations.”

    **New:** The same sentence is preceded by “The argument worked.”

    The African-born majority is now attributed specifically to the rebels, and the argument’s contribution to legislative success is expressly asserted.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — birthplace does not establish a free childhood or acceptance of slavery.**

   > “They had not grown up in slavery and learned to think of it as the way of the world.”

   African-born captives included people enslaved during childhood; African birth cannot establish the childhood described here. The implied contrast also assigns acceptance of slavery to people raised enslaved, despite the chapter’s own account of Toussaint.

   **Correct fact:** Many captives remembered freedom, but African birthplace, age at enslavement, and attitudes toward enslavement were distinct matters. [Oxford Handbook chapter, “Child Slavery in Africa”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter-abstract/553436079).

2. **Major — the revision invents a European unfamiliarity with irregular warfare.**

   > “The fires of August were the start of a war, and it was not the kind of war that European soldiers knew.”

   Europeans already knew skirmishing, ambushes, dispersed fighting, and warfare using natural cover. These were not unfamiliar African alternatives to an exclusively European practice of lining up in fields.

   **Correct fact:** European forces could struggle with the rebels’ tactics and local conditions despite possessing experience of irregular warfare. British light companies, for example, developed scouting, skirmishing, and cover-based fighting during the Seven Years War, decades earlier. [National Army Museum, “Seven Years War”](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/seven-years-war).

3. **Minor — the new sentence creates an internal chronological contradiction.**

   > “He did not live to see whether he was right.”

   The next section opens:

   > “By then his prophecy had come true.”

   That section illustrates fulfilment through events beginning in summer 1802, before Toussaint’s death in April 1803. Imprisonment might have prevented him from learning what happened, but that is different from not living long enough.

   **Correct fact:** Renewed resistance and the officers’ return to rebellion preceded his death; independence followed it. [Philippe Girard, BnF, account of the expedition](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-ameriques/lexpedition-saint-domingue-1802-et-vente-louisiane).

4. **Minor — “instead” falsely distinguishes Rochambeau’s policy from Leclerc’s.**

   > “The general who took over the command tried terror instead.”

   Leclerc had already used mass drowning and advocated extermination before his death. Rochambeau continued and intensified terror.

   **Correct fact:** Terror was a continuing French policy, not a new alternative introduced by the successor. [Girard, BnF](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-ameriques/lexpedition-saint-domingue-1802-et-vente-louisiane).

5. **Minor — Louisiana was not the first overseas effect.**

   > “The effects ran far beyond the island, and the first of them was felt on the mainland of North America.”

   In context, this introduces the Louisiana Purchase of 1803 as the first effect. The revolution had already produced international consequences during the 1790s—including the British abolitionist setback described later in this chapter.

   **Correct fact:** Louisiana was a major later consequence. Refugees were already affecting the United States in 1793, a decade before the purchase. [U.S. Office of the Historian, “The United States and the Haitian Revolution”](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1784-1800/haitian-rev).

6. **Minor — the apprenticeship correction still leaves the wrong implied endpoint.**

   > “In 1833 it voted to end slavery itself in the Caribbean colonies, and after a further four years of compulsory unpaid work for their old masters, the people were free.”

   Read normally, this gives 1837. The earlier check’s distinction between legislation and implementation has been partly addressed, but the missing year still matters.

   **Correct fact:** Parliament passed the act in 1833; it took effect in August 1834; apprenticeship ended in August 1838. [UK Parliament, “The West Indian colonies and emancipation”](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/parliament-and-empire/parliament-and-the-american-colonies-before-1765/the-west-indian-colonies-and-emancipation/).

7. **Minor — the constitutional exception is now explained inaccurately.**

   > “His constitution declared that no white man could own land in Haiti, with an exception for a few hundred Poles and Germans who had deserted from the French army to the Haitian side.”

   Adding exceptions fixed the earlier blanket prohibition, but the explanation conflates Polish soldiers with an established German settler population. The constitution did not define the exception by desertion or a numerical limit.

   **Correct fact:** Article 13 exempted naturalised Germans and Poles, alongside naturalised white women and their children. German settlement at Bombardopolis dated to the 1760s. [Constitution of 1805, Articles 12–14](https://haitidoi.com/constitutions/1805-2/); [Augusta Elmwood, “The Germans in Saint-Domingue”](https://www.agh.qc.ca/articles/?id=40).

8. **Minor — the ownership fractions remain an unresolved earlier flag.**

   > “Free people of colour owned about a third of the colony's plantations and a quarter of its slaves, and they supplied most of the militia that hunted runaways.”

   The earlier check’s evidential objection has not been fixed by changing the plantation fraction. Garrigus explicitly finds the familiar one-third/one-quarter estimates exaggerated against colonial contracts. “Plantations” also substitutes a count of estates for the familiar claim about land or property.

   **Correct fact:** Free people of colour included substantial plantation owners and slaveholders; these colony-wide fractions should not be presented as established totals. [John Garrigus, “Saint-Domingue’s Free People of Color and the Tools of Revolution,” pp. 49–50](https://www.academia.edu/28707786/3_SAINT_DOMINGUES_FREE_PEOPLE_OF_COLOR_AND_THE_TOOLS_OF_REVOLUTION).

## Clarity flags

- **Explicit signposting:**  
  > “One more fact about the colony shaped everything that followed.”

  This announces an important fact before giving it, contrary to the style sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences. “Everything” also claims more than the following explanation needs.

- **Population and ownership become muddled:**  
  > “The owners, in other words, lived among a population that outnumbered them by more than ten to one.”

  The preceding figures count whites and enslaved people, not owners and nonowners. The next paragraph then identifies additional owners among free people of colour. The arithmetic works as a population comparison; “the owners” changes its meaning.

- **The added transition disguises a backward jump:**  
  > “Abolition did not end the fighting, because the foreign armies had not gone away. The British came anyway, and stayed for five years.”

  Coming immediately after spring 1794, this makes the British arrival seem subsequent to that point while also saying foreign armies were already present. The reader needs the beginning of the British occupation located clearly.

- **Unexplained metaphor and absolute:**  
  > “Nobody needed the gesture explained.”

  A general reader may need precisely that explanation: the removed white stripe represents white colonial power, while the remaining colours represent the new alliance. “Nobody” also asserts universal understanding unnecessarily.

- **Exclusive motive:**  
  > “The one thing that could bring them over to France was the thing they were fighting for.”

  “The one thing” is an exception-breaking absolute. It also sits awkwardly beside the earlier account of leaders negotiating for personal freedom and limited concessions rather than universal emancipation.

- **Padding that repeats adjacent sentences:**  
  > “The British losses were dreadful.”  
  > “Freedom came with a payment, and the payment went in one direction.”  
  > “The sum was far beyond what the country could pay.”

  Each previews the immediately following evidence without adding orientation or explanation. The payment sentence especially duplicates “All of it went to the owners.”

- **An unnecessarily vague description of forced labour:**  
  > “He also faced a problem with no good answer, and it concerned what the freed people would now do with their days.”

  “Do with their days” initially sounds like a question about leisure or personal choice. The actual problem is how Toussaint would finance government and defence while former slaves sought control over their labour.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions, or explicit cross-references. Most added transitions are legitimate explanation under the style sheet; they should not be removed simply because they are transitions.
