# 58. How did the slaves of Haiti win their freedom?

*Saint-Domingue, the French colony that became Haiti, from 1789 to 1825*

They won it by war. In 1791 the enslaved people of France's richest colony rose against their masters, and over the next thirteen years they defeated every army sent to put them back in the fields, including the largest force that Napoleon ever sent across an ocean. France abolished slavery because their rebellion had left it no choice. No other slave rebellion in history has founded a country.

On the night of 22 August 1791, the sugar estates in the north of the French colony of Saint-Domingue began to burn. The signal started on one plantation and was taken up by the next. Gangs of field workers killed their overseers, set fire to the cane and the boiling houses, and moved on to the neighbouring property, where the workers were waiting to join them.

The northern plain was the most intensively farmed land in the Americas, a carpet of cane fifty miles long with a mill every mile or so. Within a week almost all of it was on fire. In the port city on the coast, ash fell in the streets like snow, and at night the glow in the sky was bright enough to read by. Some two hundred sugar estates and hundreds of coffee plantations were destroyed in two months.

It had been planned. Eight days earlier, drivers and coachmen from estates across the plain, the trusted men who could travel without being questioned, had met on a Sunday at one of the plantations to fix the date. By the end of September the rebels numbered tens of thousands. They had picked the moment well, because the people who owned them were busy fighting each other.

## The pearl

Saint-Domingue occupied the western third of the island of Hispaniola, an area about the size of Belgium. In 1789 it was the most profitable piece of land on Earth.

It produced about two fifths of all the sugar eaten in Europe and more than half the coffee. A large part of France's overseas trade depended on it. Its produce filled the warehouses of Bordeaux and Nantes, and the fortunes it made paid for a good many of the fine houses in both cities. The French called it the pearl of the Antilles.

Nearly half a million enslaved people did the work. The colony had some forty thousand whites. Between the two stood a third group, about thirty thousand strong, who were free and of mixed or African descent. Most were the children or grandchildren of French planters and enslaved women. Many were rich. Free people of colour owned about a third of the colony's plantations and a quarter of its slaves, and they supplied most of the militia that hunted runaways. However, the law barred them from the professions and from public office, told them what clothes they could wear, and forbade them to sit with whites in church.

The colony consumed its workers at a rate that was high even for the Caribbean. So many died, and so few children were born and lived, that in the 1780s the ships were delivering thirty thousand or more new captives every year to keep the numbers up. As a result, most of the enslaved people in Saint-Domingue in 1791 had been born in Africa. They remembered being free. A large share of them came from the lands of Kongo, where decades of civil war had made soldiers of much of the male population before it made them captives.

## Which men?

When news of the French Revolution reached the colony in the autumn of 1789, each group read it in its own way.

The white planters heard that the king's power was broken, and saw a chance to run the colony themselves, free of the royal officials and the trade rules that forced them to sell only to France. They elected assemblies and pinned the red, white and blue ribbon of the Revolution to their hats. They did not suppose that the rights of man applied to anybody's property.

The free people of colour heard that all men were born equal in rights. They were men, they were free, they paid taxes and owned land, and they asked for the vote. The whites answered with lynchings. In 1790 a wealthy free man of colour who had been in Paris came home, raised a few hundred followers, and demanded the rights that the National Assembly seemed to have promised. He was caught, and the colonial court had his arms and legs broken on a scaffold in the main square, and left him to die on a wheel.

In Paris the Assembly dithered. It was full of men who had made fine speeches about liberty, and a number of them owed their incomes to the colonial trade. It granted rights to some free men of colour and then took them back.

Neither side in this quarrel was talking about the half million. They talked in front of them, however. Slaves served at the tables where planters cursed the government and drove the carriages in which free men of colour discussed the Declaration. In the ports they unloaded the ships and heard the sailors' news. A rumour spread through the estates that the king had granted the slaves three free days a week and that the masters were hiding the decree.

## The opening

In its first months the rising was a war of bands. Each had its own leader, and many of the leaders were African-born men who fought as they had been taught at home. They avoided standing battles. They struck in small groups from the hills and the cane, fired a volley, scattered, and came together somewhere else. French officers trained for European war found it baffling.

The rebels' aims were not yet settled either. In the first negotiations, some of their leaders offered to send their followers back to work in exchange for their own freedom and a ban on the whip. The planters refused to bargain with slaves at all.

Among the men who joined in those months was a small, wiry, plain-featured man of nearly fifty. He had been born a slave on an estate on the northern plain, where he had looked after the livestock and driven his master's coach. He had been freed in the 1770s. He could read, he knew the healing uses of plants, and he had rented a small coffee farm worked by a dozen slaves. He is said to have joined the rebels as a doctor.

Within two years he was their best general. He drilled his followers into regiments that could fight in the European manner as well as the African one, he rode enormous distances, and he kept his plans to himself. About this time he began to sign himself Toussaint Louverture. The surname means "the opening".

## A decree in the north

What turned a rebellion into an emancipation was France's own war.

In 1793 the French republic executed its king and found itself fighting Britain and Spain as well as the powers of central Europe. Both had colonies next door to Saint-Domingue. Spain, which held the eastern two thirds of the island, armed the black rebels and gave their leaders Spanish commissions. Britain, invited in by the white planters, prepared to invade from Jamaica and take the richest colony in the world for itself.

The French republic's representative on the island was a young lawyer who had been sent out with a few thousand soldiers to restore order. By the summer of 1793 he was facing the planters, the Spanish and the British at once, and his soldiers were dying of fever. There was one large body of fighting men on the island, and it was the people in revolt. In August he issued a decree declaring all the slaves in the north free. He had no authority to do it. By the end of October his fellow commissioner had done the same in the rest of the colony.

He then sent three delegates to Paris to explain himself: a white man, a man of mixed descent, and a black former slave. They arrived in February 1794. When they told the assembly what had been done, a member rose to propose that slavery be abolished in every French colony, and it was voted at once by acclamation.

France thus became the first European power to abolish slavery. It did so because the slaves of its largest colony had made the old order impossible to restore, and because it needed them. That spring, Toussaint left the Spanish and brought his army over to the republic.

## Fever

The British came anyway, and stayed for five years. They took the main ports and never controlled the interior. Their real enemy was a mosquito.

Yellow fever is carried by a mosquito that breeds in standing water around houses, and it kills quickly and horribly. People who catch it as children, as most of those raised in West Africa or the Caribbean did, usually recover and are immune for life. Soldiers fresh from England had no such protection. Of about twenty thousand men whom Britain sent to Saint-Domingue, more than twelve thousand died there, most of them of fever. In 1798 the British commander negotiated his withdrawal with Toussaint in person, as with a head of state.

By 1801 Toussaint was master of the whole island. He had beaten the British and his rivals among the free men of colour, and when Spain handed its eastern two thirds of the island to France by treaty, he marched in and took possession himself. He governed in the name of France while taking orders from nobody. He issued a constitution that named him governor for life and declared that slavery was abolished for ever.

He also faced a problem with no good answer. The colony's wealth had come from sugar and coffee grown for export, and sugar above all needs the plantation, with its gangs and its timetable. Exports were the means of buying the muskets and powder to keep the island free. However, the people who had burned the plantations had not done so in order to go back to cutting cane. What they wanted was a plot of their own, where a family could grow its food and answer to no one.

Toussaint chose sugar. He ordered the former slaves back to the estates as paid labourers, entitled to a quarter of the crop, forbidden to leave without a pass, and supervised by the army. The whip was banned. To many of the labourers it felt much like the old system with a new master, and there were risings against him, which he crushed.

## The trunk of the tree

In France, meanwhile, a general had made himself ruler. Napoleon Bonaparte had plans for an empire in the Americas, with Saint-Domingue restored as its engine and the vast territory of Louisiana, on the mainland, supplying its food. A black general who made his own treaties was not part of the plan.

At the end of 1801 he sent out an expedition of more than thirty thousand veteran soldiers under his own brother-in-law. Its public orders were to restore French authority. Its secret orders, which survive, were to win the black generals' trust, then arrest and deport them, and then disarm the population. After that the old order could be brought back.

The French landed early in 1802. Toussaint fought them for three months, burning the towns as he retreated. Then several of his generals went over to the French, and he made peace and retired to his farm. In June a French general invited him to a meeting to discuss troop movements. He went with a small escort and was seized, bound, and put on a ship for France.

On the deck he said something to the captain that was written down and remembered. In overthrowing him, he said, they had cut down only the trunk of the tree of liberty. It would grow again from the roots, for they were many and deep.

He was taken to a fortress in the mountains near the Swiss border, nine hundred metres up, and locked in a stone cell. Napoleon did not answer his letters. The winter there is severe, and Toussaint had lived all his life in the tropics. He was found dead in his cell in April 1803.

## Roots

By then his prophecy had come true. In the summer of 1802 news reached Saint-Domingue from another French island, where the same government had just restored slavery by decree. Nobody on the island had any further doubt about what the expedition was for.

Bands in the hills rose first. The black generals who had gone over to the French spent some weeks hunting them down on French orders, and then, in October, changed sides themselves. Their leader was Jean-Jacques Dessalines, a former field slave with the scars of the whip on his back, who had been Toussaint's most ruthless commander. Black officers and officers of mixed descent, who had fought each other for a decade, joined forces. At a meeting in 1803 Dessalines is said to have taken the French flag of blue, white and red and torn the white out of it.

The rains came, and with them the fever. The French commander died of it. So did most of his army. France kept sending reinforcements, and they kept dying, until of the tens of thousands of soldiers and sailors sent to the island more than fifty thousand were dead. The general who took over the command tried terror. He drowned prisoners by the shipload in the bay, and imported hunting dogs from Cuba to tear captives apart in an arena. It made no difference to the outcome.

By then France and Britain were at war again, and British warships had cut the French off from supplies and reinforcements. In November 1803 Dessalines stormed the last French strongpoint outside the northern port. The survivors sailed out of the harbour and surrendered to the British squadron waiting offshore. On 1 January 1804 he proclaimed independence. The new country dropped its French name and took the one that the island's first inhabitants had used before Columbus arrived. They had called it Haiti, the land of mountains.

Dessalines did not mean to leave any chance of a return. In the following weeks he ordered the killing of nearly all the French colonists who remained, several thousand men, women and children. His constitution declared that no white man could own land in Haiti, with an exception for a few hundred Poles and Germans who had deserted from the French army to the Haitian side. All Haitians, whatever their colour, would be known as black.

## The bill

The effects ran far beyond the island. By the spring of 1803 Napoleon could see that the colony which was to have been the point of his American empire was lost, and that war with Britain was coming. He had no further use for Louisiana, and he sold it to the United States for fifteen million dollars, which nearly doubled the size of that country overnight.

Slave owners everywhere were terrified. For two generations, the word "Haiti" was the slave owners' answer to any proposal for freeing slaves in the American South, and enslaved people from Virginia to Brazil took courage from it. Refugee planters carried their knowledge of sugar to Cuba, which imported more slaves than ever and became the new pearl. The United States refused to recognise Haiti for fifty-eight years.

In Britain the news cut both ways. At first it frightened Parliament into shelving a campaign against the slave trade that had seemed close to success. Later it supplied the campaigners with their strongest argument, since Haiti had shown what a colony packed with newly landed Africans could become. In 1807 Parliament made it illegal for British ships to carry slaves, and the navy began stopping the ships of other nations. In 1833 it voted to end slavery itself in the Caribbean colonies, and after a further four years of compulsory unpaid work for their old masters, the people were free. Parliament voted twenty million pounds in compensation, which was two fifths of the government's yearly budget. All of it went to the owners.

France found a way to collect compensation as well. In 1825 a French squadron of fourteen warships anchored off the Haitian capital with an offer from the king. France would recognise Haiti's independence, and the country would be able to trade without fear of invasion. In return Haiti would pay a hundred and fifty million francs to the former planters for the property they had lost. The property included the land, the mills, and the people who had freed themselves.

Haiti's president signed. The first instalment alone was about six times the government's yearly income, and to pay it Haiti had to borrow from a bank in Paris. The amount was later reduced, but the loans, and the loans taken out to repay the loans, weighed on the country's budget for more than a century. The last payment connected with them was made in 1947.
