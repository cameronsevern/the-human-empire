I found the following issues, ranked by seriousness. No files were edited.

1. **Factual overstatement — high confidence.** “France abolished slavery because they had already ended it themselves.” ([Line 5](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:5))

   The rebellion made emancipation possible and urgently necessary, but slavery had not already disappeared throughout Saint-Domingue, much less France’s other colonies. Insurgents remained confined to parts of the north; abolition emerged from their resistance, foreign invasion and the commissioners’ decisions. The distinction matters to the chapter’s central explanation. [David Geggus, “Slave uprisings in French colonies,” BnF](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-ameriques/en/slave-uprisings-french-colonies).

2. **Factual error — high confidence.** “A few weeks later he extended it to the whole colony.” ([Line 51](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:51))

   The unnamed commissioner is Sonthonax, who proclaimed emancipation in the north. His colleague **Étienne Polverel** extended emancipation through the west and south through separate measures in September and October 1793. Combining them into one man changes who did what. [Robert Stein, “The Abolition of Slavery in the North, West, and South of Saint Domingue”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/abs/abolition-of-slavery-in-the-north-west-and-south-of-saint-domingue/DFA670BC56CD4053F478DA6679C4AA67).

3. **Factual error and prohibited absolute — high confidence.** “The whole population rose.” ([Line 85](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:85))

   Resistance expanded unevenly. Dessalines and other black officers initially helped the French suppress insurgents after news from Guadeloupe arrived; Dessalines and Pétion changed sides in October 1802. This was a developing coalition, not a simultaneous universal rising. The compressed version also removes a consequential part of Dessalines’s story. [Carolyn Fick, “Jean-Jacques Dessalines and the Independence of Haiti,” BnF](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-ameriques/en/jean-jacques-dessalines-and-independence-haiti).

4. **Factual error and prohibited absolute — high confidence.** “His constitution declared that no white man could ever own land in Haiti, and that all Haitians, whatever their colour, would be known as black.” ([Line 91](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:91))

   Article 13 expressly exempted naturalised Germans and Poles, as well as naturalised white women and their children, from Article 12’s property prohibition. The final clause accurately reflects Article 14, but the blanket landownership claim omits exceptions written into the next article. [Haiti’s 1805 constitution, Articles 12–14](https://haitidoi.com/constitutions/1805-2/).

5. **Misleading economic explanation — high confidence.** “That wealth was the only means of buying the muskets and powder to keep the island free.” ([Line 65](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:65))

   “That wealth” refers specifically to sugar. Export income was essential, but coffee and cotton also supplied it; coffee recovered much more strongly than sugar under Toussaint. The defensible argument concerns restoring **export agriculture**, not sugar as the sole possible source of arms. This also meets style.md’s warning about exception-breaking absolutes. [Mats Lundahl, “Accidental socialism: a natural experiment in Haiti 1796–1820”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/accidental-socialism-a-natural-experiment-in-haiti-17961820/1DF3966E265CB8DC2629512DF10E3513).

6. **Misleading account of the final victory — high confidence.** “In November 1803 Dessalines stormed the last French strongpoint outside the northern port, and the survivors sailed away.” ([Line 89](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:89))

   This compresses Vertières, the capitulation and the evacuation into an apparently straightforward departure. Britain’s renewed war with France and naval blockade cut French supplies and reinforcements; many evacuating survivors became British prisoners. Because the chapter explains *how Haiti won*, that contribution deserves a sentence. “Last” also needs a geographical qualification: French forces remained in eastern Hispaniola. [Philippe Girard, BnF’s account of the expedition and its defeat](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-ameriques/lexpedition-saint-domingue-1802-et-vente-louisiane).

7. **Factual overstatement — high confidence.** “In 1833 Britain abolished slavery itself in its colonies.” ([Line 99](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:99))

   Parliament passed the act in 1833; it took effect in August **1834**, and most former slaves entered compulsory “apprenticeship,” which lasted until 1838. The act also excluded East India Company territories, Ceylon and St Helena. Distinguish legislation from implementation and avoid implying universal, immediate freedom. [National Archives, the act and compensation claims](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/georgians/1833-abolition-of-slavery-act-and-compensation-claims/); [the enacted legislation](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Will4/3-4/73/contents/enacted).

8. **Chronology and causation — high confidence.** “Napoleon, having lost the colony that was to have been the point of his American empire, had no further use for Louisiana.” ([Line 95](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:95))

   Louisiana was sold before France’s final defeat in Saint-Domingue. The expedition’s deteriorating prospects helped persuade Napoleon to sell; impending renewed war with Britain also mattered. “Having lost” reverses the apparent sequence, while “no further use” makes a strategic decision sound mechanically inevitable. [Philippe Girard, BnF](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-ameriques/lexpedition-saint-domingue-1802-et-vente-louisiane).

9. **Doubtful causal sequence — medium confidence.** “A few months later, when the news had crossed the ocean, Toussaint left the Spanish and brought his army over to the republic.” ([Line 55](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:55))

   His change of allegiance in spring 1794 is secure; whether news of the February decree reached him before his decision, and directly prompted it, is disputed. David Geggus’s investigation challenges the conventional sequence. Keep the change of sides, but do not make receipt of the news its established trigger. [Geggus, “From His Most Catholic Majesty to the godless Republic”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/outre_0300-9513_1978_num_65_241_2145).

10. **Doubtful figures — medium-high confidence.** “Free people of colour owned perhaps a quarter of the colony’s plantations and of its slaves, and they supplied most of the militia that hunted runaways.” ([Line 19](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:19))

    The ownership fractions are not secure census findings. John Garrigus traces the familiar large estimates to political spokesman Julien Raimond and finds them exaggerated against colonial contracts. “Perhaps” does not solve the underlying evidential problem. Their substantial property and slaveownership can be stated without these fractions. [Garrigus, “Saint-Domingue’s Free People of Color and the Tools of Revolution”](https://www.academia.edu/28707786/3_SAINT_DOMINGUES_FREE_PEOPLE_OF_COLOR_AND_THE_TOOLS_OF_REVOLUTION).

11. **Doubtful conflation — medium-high confidence.** “Eight days earlier, drivers and coachmen from estates across the plain, the trusted men who could travel without being questioned, had met by night in a wood to fix the date.” ([Line 11](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:11))

    This combines the August 14 planning meeting at the Lenormand de Mézy estate with the disputed dating and setting of the Bois Caïman ceremony. Scholarship distinguishes the planning assembly from a religious gathering possibly held nearer the uprising. The safe fact is that plantation delegates met beforehand to organise it; the precise nocturnal woodland scene needs stronger support. [Discussion of Geggus’s reconstruction](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_1993_num_293_1_1595_t1_0560_0000_2).

12. **Over-simple historical interpretation — medium confidence.** “In Britain the revolution helped the opponents of the slave trade, who had been campaigning for twenty years.” ([Line 99](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:99))

    Haiti supplied abolitionists with arguments, but news of the revolt also strengthened their opponents and helped delay abolition during the 1790s. The paragraph presents only the helpful effect and moves directly to 1807. A brief distinction between the initial backlash and later arguments would preserve the point without making the influence uniformly positive. [UK Parliament, “Drive for abolition slowed by external events”](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/tradeindustry/slavetrade/overview/drive-for-abolition-slowed-by-external-events/).

13. **Factual overstatement and prohibited absolute — high confidence.** “For two generations, the word ‘Haiti’ was enough to end any argument about freeing slaves in the American South, and enslaved people from Virginia to Brazil took courage from it.” ([Line 97](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:97))

    Virginia’s legislature extensively debated emancipation in 1831–32. Fear of Haiti was powerful, but it did not end “any argument.” The first clause needs narrowing; the second is not the problem. [Encyclopedia Virginia, “The Virginia Slavery Debate of 1831–1832”](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/virginia-slavery-debate-of-1831-1832-the/).

14. **Internal contradiction — high confidence.** “They were owned by some thirty thousand whites.” ([Line 19](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:19))

    “They” means the entire enslaved population. The same paragraph then says free people of colour owned a substantial share of that population. It also turns the approximate white population into a count of slaveowners. Distinguish population totals from ownership.

15. **Reader orientation: the island changes scope.** “By 1801 Toussaint was master of the whole island.” ([Line 63](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:63))

    Previously Spain holds the eastern two-thirds. The reader needs a bridge explaining Toussaint’s takeover of that territory, then another distinguishing his island-wide rule from the western territory that became independent Haiti. Otherwise “island,” “colony” and “country” appear interchangeable. French troops still occupied the east after Haitian independence. [Carolyn Fick, BnF](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-ameriques/en/jean-jacques-dessalines-and-independence-haiti).

16. **Style: unsupported absolute.** “The planters refused to bargain with slaves at all, and so lost the only chance they would have.” ([Line 39](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:39))

    “The only chance” asserts an unprovable counterfactual: that this settlement could have succeeded and no later settlement could. The refusal itself carries the point. This is a specific breach of style.md’s rule against brittle absolutes, rather than an objection to the narrator taking a view.

17. **Style and accessibility: unexplained term.** “They elected assemblies and put on the red, white and blue cockade.” ([Line 27](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md:27))

    Neither this chapter nor the earlier chapters supplies “cockade.” Explain that it was a ribbon badge identifying supporters of the Revolution, or omit the term, as style.md requires.
