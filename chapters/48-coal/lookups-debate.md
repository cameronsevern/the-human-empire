## 1. Robert Allen: high wages, cheap energy

**Argument.** Allen (*The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective*, CUP 2009) says the Industrial Revolution "was invented in Britain in the eighteenth century because it paid to invent it there" (p. 2, as quoted by Crafts). International wage and price comparisons show Britain with high wages, cheap capital and very cheap energy. Developing a "macro-invention" into a working machine has high fixed costs, so it is worth doing only where the machine will be profitable to adopt and the market is big. Allen computes adoption returns for the spinning jenny, Arkwright's mill and coke smelting, and finds adoption rational at British prices but not French ones. For the jenny c. 1780 he gets a rate of return of 38% in England against 2.5% in France and minus 5.2% in India, with a required return of 15% (Crafts's summary of Allen's table). Later improvements were Hicks-neutral rather than labour-saving, so the technologies eventually paid elsewhere and Britain's edge faded. Allen concedes supply-side factors (human capital, a cultural revolution) may explain "1800 rather than 1400."

**Evidence.** Welfare ratios: family income divided by the cost of a fixed consumption basket, compared across London, Amsterdam, Florence, Vienna, Beijing, Delhi. London and Amsterdam were high-wage cities. Beijing and Delhi were near subsistence in the 18th century (read, Allen 2013).

**Main criticism.**
- Humphries and Schneider, "Spinning the Industrial Revolution," *Economic History Review* 72(1), 2019, pp. 126-155. They built wage and productivity evidence for women and children from the late 16th to early 19th century. They find spinning was widespread, low-productivity, low-wage work, with wages not rising substantially before the jenny and water frame, and low even compared with other women's work. Their conclusion is that the motive for mechanizing "must be sought elsewhere." (Abstract, secondary.) The jenny and mill were the machines that replaced women spinners, so the wage that matters is theirs, not the male breadwinner's.
- Allen's reply, "Spinning their wheels," *EHR* 73(4), 2020, pp. 1128-1136. As summarized: Humphries and Schneider's data do not know how many hours spinners worked, so they cannot show whether the hourly wage was high or low; and much of their production data came from schools and charity programmes whose participants were selected because they were unproductive. He concludes the high-wage hypothesis is unimpaired. **Secondary**: I read only abstracts and a search summary of this paper.
- Humphries and Schneider's rejoinder, "Losing the thread," *EHR* 73(4), 2020, pp. 1137-1152. They say Allen's reply rests on an ahistorical account of spinners' work and similarly weak wage evidence, and that an augmented dataset confirms their findings. (Abstract, secondary.)
- Earlier round: Allen, "The High Wage Economy and the Industrial Revolution: A Restatement," Oxford Economic and Social History Working Paper 115 (2013) (read). He answers Humphries on three points. (1) He accepts her point that his baskets had too few calories; with a higher-calorie basket the international comparisons are "virtually unaffected." (2) He argues the male-breadwinner family was the predominant type. (3) He claims rises in women's wages around 1700 greatly increased the profitability of spinning machinery. He frames the disagreement as one of perspective: global comparison (his) against the viewpoint of contemporary social critics (hers).
- Mokyr's critique (as summarized by Crafts, read). Firms try to save all costs, not just the dear ones. Little evidence of a labour-saving bias overall: Macleod's patent data suggest only 21% of inventions late in the century were aimed at saving labour. Factor prices might set the direction of invention but not its intensity.
- Crafts's own assessment: the theory is defensible (it fits Acemoglu's models), but at English prices the jenny would be profitable even at French wages. The French gap came partly from the jenny costing more in France, not only from wages.

**Status of the exchange.** Unresolved. Both sides have published at least two rounds. I cannot say who has the better of the data without the papers.

## 2. Joel Mokyr: the Industrial Enlightenment

**Argument.** Mokyr (*The Enlightened Economy*, 2009; *A Culture of Growth*, 2016) says Britain led "because, more than any other European economy, it was able to take advantage of its endowment of human and physical resources thanks to the great synergy of the Enlightenment: the combination of the Baconian program in useful knowledge and the recognition that better institutions created better incentives" (p. 122, quoted by Crafts). The Baconian programme meant experiment, a focus on practical problems, and wide dissemination of results. Britain's edge was on the supply side, since the Netherlands was richer, France larger and Spain had more colonies (p. 99). The key was a large pool of skilled craftsmen who could turn ideas into working machines and make the continuous small "micro-inventions." Access costs to useful knowledge fell through print, societies and lectures. Mokyr also holds that Enlightenment ideology reduced rent-seeking and shifted institutions. The outline's phrase "belief that nature could be understood and put to use" is a fair gloss. Note that Mokyr's thesis includes institutions (see flags).

**Evidence.** Books on science and technology, lectures, societies, biographies of mechanics. Mokyr himself concedes the institutional channel is hard to quantify and that the evidence "will not strike everyone as compelling."

**Main criticisms.**
- Allen's chapter 10 tests the model on 79 inventors, 10 of whom made the macro-inventions (read via Crafts). Links to Enlightenment science were present for about half and common in steam, but "infrequent for textiles or metals which had much greater economic impact," so "the Industrial Enlightenment did not matter much" (Allen p. 252). Merchants, lawyers and capitalists were 4.6% of the population but 32.8% of inventors; but many inventors' fathers were artisans. Experimentation was long-standing.
- Crafts's reply: Allen's test of macro-inventors is partial, because the craftsmen's micro-inventions are anonymous and cannot be counted. But Crafts also notes the link between artisans and the Enlightenment is "elusive and, at best, indirect, as Mokyr admits," and that falling access costs may reflect tacit knowledge spreading through factories and towns, not manuals or societies. Mokyr offers no quantification of institutional quality.
- Gregory Clark's *JEL* review (2012) (read): the Enlightenment was pan-European, yet northern Italy's output per head fell 1760-1855 and East Prussia stayed feudal; the Dutch were the richest and scientific in the 17th century; dating the Enlightenment from 1637 or the 1660s leaves a gap of 3-4 generations; textile innovators, about half of productivity growth, had little Enlightenment connection; and the book is not quantitatively tested. Publication data show science and technology books went from 5.4% to 9.0% of titles, but literary works rose from 19.4% to 30.3%.
- Reviewer caution: Mokyr estimates the movement directly touched only the top 10-15% of the population (p. 39, via Clark).

**Source for "France had the science too."** The outline's weak point is supported in spirit by Clark's Italy/Netherlands/Prussia points, not specifically France. Mokyr himself answers the French question with craftsmen and institutions. I did not find a source that makes the France comparison against Mokyr in those words. **UNVERIFIED as a named critic's argument.**

**Sources.**
- Crafts (above).
- Clark's review of *The Enlightened Economy* (JEL 2012): https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/Book_Reviews/Mokyr%20JEL%202012.pdf
- TSEG review of *A Culture of Growth* (seen in search results only): https://tseg.nl/article/view/8164/8795

## 3. Kenneth Pomeranz and E. A. Wrigley

**Pomeranz's argument.** *The Great Divergence* (Princeton, 2000) says the most advanced parts of China (the Yangzi Delta, "Jiangnan") and of Europe were broadly comparable before about 1800, and that the divergence came in the 19th century. Per the Wikipedia summary (secondary): life expectancy and per-capita calorie intake comparable, modest per-capita growth in both, no Chinese stagnation. Two "lucky" factors then separated them.
- Coal. Britain had coal near its industry and near water transport, and its wet mines pushed it to invent the Newcomen engine. China's largest deposits lay in the north and northwest, far from the Yangzi region. A reviewer calls this "a good example of why generalizing about whole subcontinents is misleading" (Bactra review of Pomeranz).
- Land overseas ("ghost acres"). The New World supplied cotton, sugar and timber that would otherwise have required land Britain lacked.

**Ghost-acre figures (secondary; not checked against Pomeranz's text or Appendix D).**
- Cotton replaced with wool: about 9 million acres of sheep pasture for 1815, over 23 million for 1830, using model-farm ratios, p. 275-6. 23 million exceeds Britain's total crop and pasture land. (Search summary attributing this to Pomeranz pp. 275-6 and Appendix D; one other source dates the 9 million to 1800.)
- Cotton, sugar and timber combined for c. 1830: 25 to 30 million acres (Journal of Global History article; the Wikipedia Great Divergence page says "23 to 25 million").
- Baseline: England had about 17 million acres of arable (Wikipedia). Ridley's popular summary of Pomeranz gives about 17 million arable, 25 million pasture and under 2 million forest in 1830, with West Indian sugar equal to at least 2 million acres of wheat and Canadian timber equal to 1 million acres of woodland. Ridley is a popularizer. I could not confirm the sugar and timber figures against Pomeranz.
- Coal: Wrigley's illustration is that coal in 1800 supplied energy equal to 15 million acres of woodland (search summary). One source attributes 15 million acres of coal-equivalent to the 1830 comparison. The year is unclear. **Do not print a year for the 15 million without checking Wrigley (2010) or Pomeranz.**
- The appendix figures are reportedly lower than the main-text figures (Riello-type caution in a search summary). Check before quoting.

**Main objections.**
- Dating. Allen, Bassino, Ma, Moll-Murata and van Zanden (*EHR* 64, 2011, pp. 8-38): in the 18th century Asian building workers' real incomes were similar to the backward parts of Europe, far behind NW Europe; Chinese real wages stagnated 1738 to the late 19th century (abstract, read). Broadberry and Gupta (*EHR* 2006) argue silver wages in India and China were far below NW Europe's, reflecting higher productivity in tradable goods in Europe. Per Wikipedia (secondary): Broadberry and Zhai date a Yangzi/England divergence to about 1700; Broadberry puts England's silver wages at about three times the Yangzi Delta's by 1600 (not checked against the paper).
- Coal counterfactual. Clark and Jacks and McCloskey say coal could have been imported or industry moved (via Wikipedia, secondary). The Bactra reviewer notes Chinese mines tended to fill with gas and steam ventilation might have helped.
- Ghost acres. Hudson (LSE GEHN paper, read): counting sheep only makes sense as a counterfactual if breeding and yields never improve and domestic wool is the sole source; sheep can graze marginal land at near-zero opportunity cost. Other fibres could substitute. Theodoridis, Warde and Kander find actual land-embodied trade gives much lower estimates; a Journal of Global History study finds Britain was a net exporter of embodied land except in the early 19th century (when potash was the largest land-demanding North American import), and that coal ("vertical" expansion) was generally larger than overseas land ("horizontal").
- Brenner and Isett: land tenure and market pressure in England, not coal or ghost acres alone (Wikipedia, secondary).

**Wrigley.** *Continuity, Chance and Change* (1988) and *Energy and the English Industrial Revolution* (2010). In an "organic economy" nearly all energy and materials came from current plant growth, so energy supply was tied to acreage and growth ran into an "areal" constraint. Coal, a stock accumulated over geological time, removed that constraint. By the early 18th century about half of England and Wales's energy came from coal (VoxEU summary of Wrigley's tables, secondary). The outline's description is accurate. But Wrigley's argument is about coal and the organic economy; the overseas-land argument is Pomeranz's. He is not a co-author of the ghost-acre claim.

**Sources.**

- Wikipedia, Great Divergence (first 100k characters read): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence

- Broadberry and Gupta 2006 (as cited): https://cpi.stanford.edu/_media/pdf/Reference%20Media/Broadbery%20and%20Gupta_2006_History%20of%20Inequality.pdf

## 4. Institutions: North and Weingast; Acemoglu and Robinson

**North and Weingast (1989).** "Constitutions and Commitment," *Journal of Economic History* 49(4), pp. 803-832. After 1688 Parliament's constraints on the crown made the crown's financial promises credible. That made public borrowing cheap and secured property rights more generally. Evidence: the Bank of England (1694), falling interest rates, growth in stock trading (Pincus and Robinson's account of N&W's evidence). The paper is about public finance and credible commitment; it does not itself argue the mills followed.

**Acemoglu and Robinson.** In "The Rise of Europe" (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson, AER 2005) and *Why Nations Fail* (2012): Atlantic trade strengthened merchant groups where the monarchy was not absolutist, and England's 1688 settlement gave "inclusive" institutions that allowed creative destruction a century later. Their illustration of extractive suppression of technology is the stocking frame. The "one century" gap between 1688 and the factories is A&R's own framing, per a review.

**Standard criticisms.**
- The Glorious Revolution as a break. Gregory Clark and Sussman and Yafeh argue interest rates did not fall discontinuously after 1688. Cox (*JEH* 2012) groups them as a "no structural break" school and defends 1688 as a watershed that affected a narrower set of transactions than N&W claimed. Other work finds that the risk premium on British debt stayed high for decades and that productivity and incomes were rising before 1688. Pincus and Robinson side with N&W that 1688 mattered but find N&W's mechanism poorly supported.
- Timing. A century separates 1688 from the mills.
- Monocausality and anecdote. A review of *Why Nations Fail* (IRSH 2013) calls it "very well-articulated mono-causal" and asks whether 1688 was a "juncture or rather a conjuncture." Another review calls the evidence anecdotal.
- The Dutch. DeLong's critique of AJR argues Britain had no large constitutional edge over Iberia in 1500 and that England's use of Atlantic earnings for naval power and carrying trade is part of the story. Mokyr (via Crafts) notes the Netherlands was richer than Britain, and Clark notes the Netherlands was the 17th-century centre of the new science and commerce. **The outline's exact claim, that the Dutch had similar institutions earlier and did not industrialize first, I could not find stated by a named critic.** It is a natural point but I have no citation. The Dutch point is stated as a puzzle for the Enlightenment thesis (Clark) and for demand-side arguments (Mokyr), not as an institutions objection. A&R treat the Netherlands as one of the Atlantic traders.

**Sources.**
- North and Weingast 1989: https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-my/wp-content/uploads/sites/138/2016/12/14091657/NorthWeingast-1989-Constitutions-and-Commitment.pdf
- Cox 2012: https://people.bu.edu/chamley/365-24/CoxJEH12.pdf
- Pincus and Robinson: https://people.bu.edu/chamley/HSFref/PincusRobinson11.pdf

## 5. Williams, Engerman, Berg and Hudson, Inikori, Parthasarathi, and India

**Williams (1944).** *Capitalism and Slavery* has two claims. (1) Profits from the slave trade and the West Indian plantations financed British industrial capital formation. (2) The West Indian economy declined from about 1783, and abolition (1807, 1833) followed from that decline and the rise of industrial interests, with moral motives secondary (Wikipedia, secondary). A review in the search results notes Williams "devotes very little attention" to America or cotton. **This matters for the outline: cotton as a Williams argument is wrong.**

**Standard criticism.**
- Engerman 1972, *Business History Review*, tested the profit claim. Per Wikipedia, Engerman found slave-trade and plantation profits were under 5% of the British economy even without subtracting costs or reinvestment (secondary; the outline's "small ratios" is fair).
- Eltis and Engerman, "The Importance of Slavery and the Slave Trade to Industrializing Britain," *JEH* 60(1), 2000 (read). Caribbean sugar added less than 2.5% of national income and about 3.7% of the labour force. Slave-trade ships were under 3% of British tonnage. From 1700 to 1760 the extra growth of plantation output was 0.6% of national income. They cite Solow quoting Engerman: slave-trade profits alone could have been "one half of 1 percent of national income, nearly 8 percent of total investment, and 39 percent of commercial and industrial investment," ratios she called "enormous." Eltis and Engerman answer that these are extreme upper bounds that assume all profits were invested and that other sectors could equally have funded industry. Raw cotton's price fall accounted for only 14% of the fall in yarn prices 1784-1830. They conclude sugar and slavery were "in the end none were indispensable in the strict sense" and that the Caribbean's impact on Britain was more "ideological" than economic. They also say Williams may not have had the mechanisms right but few now treat the events as unrelated.
- Wikipedia lists Pares (1937), Drescher (*Econocide*, 1977, on abolition), Richardson (1998: slave-trade profits under 1% of domestic investment). O'Brien: I found only a citation (Eltis and Engerman cite O'Brien, "Inseparable Connections," as a recent restatement of the importance of overseas trade). **I could not verify O'Brien's 1982 argument.**
- The outline's "profits modest beside the whole economy" is therefore right for national income, but incomplete: for investment, the same estimates give large ratios. State both.

**Recent restatements.**
- Berg and Hudson, *Slavery, Capitalism and the Industrial Revolution* (Polity, 2023). Per the reviewer: war and state power backed trade (Britain at war about 70 of 155 years, 1660-1815); naval spending as stimulus; compensation to slave owners in the 1830s fed railways and utilities; slavery spurred financial innovation, agricultural transfers and accounting (a "Reconstructed Williams Thesis"). One quoted passage says: "We do not argue that slavery caused the industrial revolution" and slavery was not necessary for British industrial capitalism (via a review). The IEA review (Niemietz) is from a free-market think tank and makes these criticisms: costs of defence counted as benefits; compensation payments were zero-sum; net gains too small; the reconstruction ends up close to the domestic-factors account. Goldman's critical review says they exaggerate the Atlantic economy's weight. Engelsberg Ideas says the case is solid for capital and markets, hard to show for technology.
- Inikori, *Africans and the Industrial Revolution in England* (CUP 2002): widens the case to Atlantic trade as a whole, with Africa as market and supplier, not only the plantations. Described as Williams's best modern defender (search summary). **I could not verify his figures** (cotton export shares, market shares).

**Parthasarathi.** *Why Europe Grew Rich and Asia Did Not* (CUP 2011): advanced regions of Europe and Asia were alike in the 17th and 18th centuries; Mughal India was sophisticated and its cottons in demand worldwide; British industrialization was policy-driven (protection, import substitution), the Calico Act of 1721 spurred British cotton mechanization, and the Raj denied Indian goods access to Britain and after 1820 forced Lancashire cloth into India; British coal grew with Hanoverian price regulation and protected coastal shipping (as described by the reviewer, Mokyr).
- Criticism, from Mokyr's review (EH.Net; Mokyr is a rival, so weigh accordingly): Clark's mill data show Indian wages about a sixth of British, but Indian labour was much less efficient, so no real cost advantage; the Calico Act had many exemptions and weak enforcement; Britain's coal industry, turnpikes and canals were mostly private; Persia, Siam and Imperial China also did not industrialize; the book is thin on tables. A second review notes high weaver wages in south India because of demand, which complicates grain-wage comparisons.
- The outline's tariff claim ("tariffs that shielded British cotton from Indian cloth") rests on Parthasarathi and the Calico Acts. It is his thesis, not a settled fact. Note the dispute over how much the Acts mattered.

**India's deindustrialization: main positions (partial).**
- Clingingsmith and Williamson (NBER w11730): by the mid-19th century India lost all its export market and much of its domestic market; they weigh British productivity gains against Mughal decline and climate shocks, and call the explanations complementary (abstract, secondary).
- Broadberry and Gupta (*EHR* 2009): high British silver wages meant British makers could not copy labour-intensive Indian methods, so they mechanized; productivity gains then reversed the cost position; Napoleonic-war raw cotton costs delayed this to after 1815 (abstract, secondary).
- Parthasarathi: policy and Company rule, as above.
- Indian historians (a passage cited Roy 2002 as emphasizing the domestic-employment side). **I could not verify the positions of Roy, Habib or Bagchi.**
- **UNVERIFIED:** textile export and import figures for 1800-1850.

## 6. Andreas Malm, *Fossil Capital* (Verso, 2016)

**Argument.** Malm rejects three accounts: the Malthusian/Ricardian one (scarce land and fuel, e.g. Wrigley), the "Anthropocene" one (a species-wide propensity to burn), and technological superiority. He says water was neither scarce nor dearer than steam, nor technically backward; capital chose steam anyway. Reasons, per the Antipode review (read):
- Water is a "flow" that owners had to share. Engineers proposed reservoir systems (for example on the Irwell to supply Manchester); millowners rejected them. Owners distrusted collective management and feared free-riding. Malm quotes the historian Louis Hunter that owners found water power restrained "their independence of action," and that steam meant "the millowner could do as he pleased."
- Place. Water mills sat in remote valleys. Steam let mills sit in towns "where labour is easily procured" (Malm p. 121), with a surplus pool of labour, which weakened workers. Rural mills relied on women and children or company towns.
- Time. Water varied with drought and frost; steam gave constant power under the owner's control (Chapter 8).
- Labour militancy was the proximate trigger of the switch in his account. The transition was "a protracted contest" over decades, not a quick victory (Ecologist review).

The outline's "control over labour and time, not cheapness" is a fair summary. Add that he also argues against the cost story, and that the shift was slow.

**Criticisms.**
- The Antipode reviewer (Huber, sympathetic overall): the advantages of steam that Malm treats as social could also be real material superiority, since water power was subject to droughts and freezes and was local; Marx himself said so (Capital I, quoted in the review), and Malm does not acknowledge this until p. 275. Fossil fuels also gave heat for chemicals, steel and cement, which water could not.
- Christophers (Guardian, as relayed by the LSE review): steam was adopted because it was more profitable, not because it was cheaper. This is a refinement, not an objection.
- A Business History review (as relayed): "capital" and "labour" are too coarse to capture the detail.
- **UNVERIFIED:** named economic historians' rebuttals (Kanefsky, von Tunzelmann, Hills and others). My searches turned up no direct response from them. Malm himself cites von Tunzelmann and Crafts and challenges their conclusions (LSE review). If the chapter says "historians of the cost data dispute him," that needs a citation I do not have.

## 7. Newcomen and natural philosophers

**What is known.**
- The old story: John Robison's article "Steam Engines" in the 3rd edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* (1797) says papers at the Royal Society showed Robert Hooke corresponded with Newcomen, discouraged his first scheme, and pointed him to Papin's vacuum-under-a-piston method. Smiles repeated it.
- Rhys Jenkins (Newcomen Society, 1936-37) found no trace of any Hooke-Newcomen correspondence in the Royal Society's papers, which predate Robison and are well preserved. He raised the possibility that the correspondent was Newton, mis-transcribed. (Search summaries of Jenkins; Nature abstract.) Hooke died in 1703, before any Newcomen engine is documented. DNB says the item "cannot now be found."
- A weaker link: Jenkins thought Newcomen might have read an account of Papin's work in the *Philosophical Transactions*. This is a possible link, not a documented one.
- Newcomen's own background: an ironmonger/blacksmith in Dartmouth, working with John Calley (plumber and glazier). The IMechE piece says he may have been taught as a boy by John Flavell. Savery's patent covered raising water by fire, so Newcomen and Calley worked under it and paid a licence; the patent was extended by statute to 1733 (Wikipedia). Some say Newcomen may have seen a Savery machine. The two machines were fundamentally different.
- The interpretive dispute. Mokyr treats the atmospheric engine as a case of propositional knowledge about air pressure and the void feeding technique. Allen's inventor sample (via Crafts) finds Enlightenment-science links "quite general" for steam, against textiles and metals. Neither shows Newcomen in contact with philosophers.

**Safe wording.** The science of the vacuum (Torricelli, Guericke, Boyle, Papin) was available and the engine uses it. A documented personal channel from natural philosophers to Newcomen has not been found; the Hooke story is a late tradition that archival checks did not support. Whether Newcomen got the idea from Papin's published work is plausible but not proved. The outline's section 3 line "the science of the atmosphere came first and the engine followed" is true as a sequence, but should not read as a causal chain.

**Bonus, from the same pages (not requested; secondary).** Earliest firmly evidenced engine 1712 at Tipton near Dudley Castle (IMechE), though Wikipedia places it at Conygree Coalworks and one local historian disputes the site; Black Country Museum replica 1986; about 125 engines by 1733 and about 600 by 1775 (Wikipedia); one source gives 120 gallons a minute from 153 ft (not corroborated). I did not find strokes per minute or efficiency.

## 8. Why Song China did not industrialize

**Frame.** Eric Jones called Song China the first credible candidate for an industrial revolution and asked why China "was unable to repeat its Song success" (Deng's paper, quoting Jones). Hartwell argued that eleventh-century mining and iron output exceeded what England achieved in the late 16th and early 17th centuries. His output estimate (about 115,000 tonnes) is contested: Liu about 10,000, Qi-Wang about 70,000; Wagner judged the order of magnitude plausible (LSE paper).

**Two standard explanations.**
1. Loss of the northern core. The Yellow River heartland, the farming and industrial centre, was lost to the Jurchen in 1127 (Deng 2013, read); the Song had already paid heavy annual tribute to the Khitan and Tangut. The iron districts (Cizhou, Xingzhou) were northern. Whether Hartwell himself ties the iron decline to this I could not confirm. **UNVERIFIED.**
2. Mark Elvin's "high-level equilibrium trap" (*Pattern of the Chinese Past*, 1973): cheap labour and very efficient traditional methods removed the pressure to mechanize, and an unfavourable man-to-land ratio absorbed surplus. Critics: Justin Yifu Lin (1995) argues against it with population and acreage data; some say man-land ratios improved after the Song. Note that Elvin's trap is mainly a Ming-Qing argument, so it does not straightforwardly explain the Song.

**Which explanation survives the comparison (for the chapter's last bullet).** Not my call. Note that Pomeranz's coal-location argument and Allen's high-wage argument can both be run against Song China, and Deng's paper reports that mainstream scholarship still calls Song growth an "economic revolution."

## Flags: where the outline misstates or overreaches

1. Section 8.5 (Williams): the outline folds Indian-cloth tariffs and "why cotton led" into Williams's thesis. Those belong to Parthasarathi, Inikori and Berg-Hudson. Williams barely discusses cotton. Also, "profits modest beside the whole economy" needs the investment-share qualification: Engerman's upper-bound slave-trade profits were about 0.5% of national income but about 8% of total and 39% of commercial and industrial investment.
2. Section 8.3 (Pomeranz and Wrigley): "richest parts of China and Europe were about level until around 1800" is Pomeranz's contested claim and should be attributed, not asserted. Wrigley's argument is about coal, not overseas land. The "millions of extra acres" should be given as Pomeranz's counterfactual: about 25-30 million acres for cotton, sugar and timber combined, with the wool counterfactual alone 9 million (1815) and 23 million (1830), against about 17 million acres of arable. These are second-hand and criticized by Hudson and others.
3. Section 8.1 (Allen): fair, but incomplete. Add Allen's reply (hours unknown; biased charity and school data) and Mokyr's objection (only 21% of patents labour-saving). The jenny result also depended on cheaper English jennies, not wages alone. The Newcomen engine claim is the energy half of the thesis.
4. Section 8.2/8.4 (Mokyr; institutions): Mokyr's thesis includes institutions (Enlightenment-driven), so sorting it purely under "ideas" is off. "France had the science too" and "the Dutch had similar institutions earlier" are plausible points, but I found no named critic who makes either in those words. Better-sourced weak points: Allen's inventor study and Clark's points for Mokyr; Clark, Sussman-Yafeh and Cox's "no structural break" debate and the evidence of pre-1688 growth for institutions.
5. Section 3 (Newcomen): "the science of the atmosphere came first and the engine followed" is true in sequence, but the Hooke-Royal Society link behind the usual story is a 1797 tradition that Jenkins could not corroborate. State the contact as unproven.

Other: Section 7 says steam "cost more per unit of power for decades" and water was "cheap." That is the contested point in the Malm debate, and I found no source here settling it. Attribute it. The Song bullet should note that Elvin's trap is a later (Ming-Qing) argument.
