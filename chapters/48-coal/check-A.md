## Factual flags

Assessment based only on the draft and my own knowledge; no external sources checked.

1. **“Every great civilisation before 1700 had lived under that ceiling” / “Every society before this one that grew rich had, sooner or later, run short of land to grow its energy on.”**  
   Land constrained economies dependent on plants for food and fuel, but it did not invariably end their periods of growth. The chapter turns an important constraint into a universal explanation of economic stagnation, overlooking other causes and societies’ ability to expand trade, cultivation and productivity. The later assertion that growth always stopped “after a generation or two” is also false.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

2. **“Most of the heat still went up the chimney.”**  
   Low efficiency does not mean that most heat escaped through the chimney. Steam engines also rejected substantial heat through condensation, cooling water and other losses; these are particularly important to the explanation being given here. Describe most of the fuel’s energy as failing to become useful mechanical work, without assigning it predominantly to the chimney.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

3. **“Every ton came by ship from Wales.”**  
   Cornwall depended on imported coal, much of it from South Wales, but also received coal from other British coalfields. “Every ton” makes an unnecessary and inaccurate claim about its origin.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

4. **“Two years after mending the model, Watt had his answer.”**  
   Watt’s separate-condenser insight is conventionally dated to 1765, following his work on the model in the winter of 1763–64. That is roughly a year afterward, rather than two. The subsequent eleven-year interval to commercial engines in 1776 fits the 1765 date.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

5. **“In 1774 an ironmaster built a new machine for boring out the barrels of cannon, one that held its cutting tool rigid at both ends.”**  
   This appears to combine details of John Wilkinson’s cannon-boring machinery with his subsequent cylinder-boring arrangement. The arrangement with a boring bar supported at both ends is associated with engine-cylinder boring; the cannon-boring design involved rotating the gun against a cutting tool. The connection between weapons manufacture and Watt’s cylinders is sound, but this precise mechanical description needs checking.  
   **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

6. **“No skill was needed. A child could mind it.”**  
   Roller spinning reduced dependence on the hand spinner’s skill, but tending the machinery still required learned tasks, including handling fibres and repairing broken threads. Children’s employment does not establish that the work required no skill; setting up and maintaining the machinery required additional expertise.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

7. **“All of this, the machines and the clock and the children, ran on rivers.”**  
   The passage has just described the spinning jenny, water frame and mule. Jennies and early mules were operated by human power, often in homes or small workshops; they did not all run on rivers or immediately impose the water-powered factory system. The claim wrongly merges several different paths of mechanisation into Arkwright’s model.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

8. **“An engine had none of these habits” / “in drought and frost alike” / “it asked for no agreement with the neighbours.”**  
   Steam offered much greater freedom over location and working hours, but Watt’s condensing engines required substantial water supplies. Drought, freezing conditions and access to water could therefore still matter. These absolutes misleadingly imply complete independence from water and local resource arrangements.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

9. **“Water could have carried the cotton industry a good deal further than it did. It could not have gone much beyond that. Nobody has ever run a railway on a millstream.”**  
   Water power supported substantial industrial development beyond cotton, including engineering, paper and metalworking, and later supplied electricity to industry and railways. Water-powered and water-balanced rail systems also existed. Water’s geographical and seasonal limitations help explain steam’s advantages, but the railway assertion does not establish a hard limit to water-powered industrialisation.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

10. **“In the 1820s [the power loom] was finally made practical.”**  
    Commercially workable power looms existed before the 1820s, with important improvements and adoption during the 1800s and 1810s. The 1820s were a period of accelerating adoption, rather than the decade in which the loom first became practical.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

11. **“The mill child went home at night and would one day be free to leave.”**  
    This describes some children but excludes the parish apprentices already mentioned in the chapter. Such children could live in accommodation controlled by the mill owner and be legally bound to serve for years. Their status differed fundamentally from slavery, but going home each night is not a valid general distinction.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

12. **“Lancashire’s thread and cloth began arriving in India’s own markets, carried by a British trading company that had made itself the ruler of much of the country.”**  
    This assigns the commercial role too broadly to the East India Company. Private British merchants were central to the expansion of Lancashire exports after the Company’s Indian trade monopoly ended in 1813, and the Company ceased commercial trading in 1833. Its governing role and the merchants’ exporting role should be distinguished.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

13. **“Spain, Portugal and France had colonies and slaves as well, and no factories to show for them.”**  
    These countries did have factories, particularly France, which developed mechanised textile production and other industries during the period covered. Their industrialisation differed in pace and scale from Britain’s. The defensible comparison is that possessing colonies and enslaved labour did not automatically produce Britain’s industrial breakthrough.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

14. **“Cotton mills without an ocean of slave-grown cotton and a world of customers would have been a curiosity in a few Derbyshire valleys.”**  
    Slave-grown cotton was central to the industry’s actual nineteenth-century expansion, but the statement presents an unprovable counterfactual as a demonstrated necessity. Cotton also came from producers using other labour systems, and alternative supplies could have supported an industry, albeit with different costs and growth. The draft can establish the importance of slavery to the expansion that occurred without claiming that substantial cotton manufacturing was otherwise impossible.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

15. **“In the 1800s [atmospheric carbon] began to climb. The chimneys of Manchester were where that began.”**  
    The sustained industrial increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide is generally traced from the eighteenth century, and Manchester was one contributor rather than its place of origin. Coal burning elsewhere and emissions from land-use changes also contributed. Manchester works as an emblem of the process, but this wording makes it a literal starting point.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

## Clarity flags

- **“The rods lift a load of water out of the mine.”**  
  The preceding description of horses hauling buckets makes it easy to picture the engine raising another bucket. Explain that the rods operate pumps in the shaft, forcing water upward through pipes, often in stages.

- **“When the valve opened, the steam rushed across into the cold chamber and collapsed there.”**  
  The account never explains how the condenser remains usable as water and air accumulate inside it. A short mention of the pump that removes condensate and air would complete the cycle.

- **“In the 1780s Watt worked out how to make the rocking beam turn a shaft.”**  
  The chapter carefully explains the pumping engine but skips the mechanism responsible for its central transition to factory power. A sentence explaining the sun-and-planet gearing and the flywheel would make that transition understandable.

- **“One early machine” / “The machine that mattered most” / “a third machine.”**  
  Name the spinning jenny and water frame when introducing them. Otherwise readers must remember two anonymous machines to understand what the mule combined: roller drawing with a moving carriage carrying spindles.

- **“The amount each British worker produced rose by nearly half” / “The rate of profit roughly doubled.”**  
  Explain that the first is an estimate of output per worker across the economy, rather than an individual mill worker’s production. Define the second as profit relative to capital invested, since readers may otherwise understand it as the total amount of profit.

## Overall

The chapter’s central sequence—from mine drainage through improved steam engines to expanding factory production—is broadly accurate. Its most misleading claims turn limits and historical advantages into universal necessities, particularly concerning land, water power and slave-grown cotton. The treatment of workers and colonial producers conveys important connections, but it sometimes collapses distinct labour arrangements and commercial institutions. Readability is weakened where unnamed inventions must be remembered and where the explanations omit how pumps, condensers and rotary machinery complete their cycles.
