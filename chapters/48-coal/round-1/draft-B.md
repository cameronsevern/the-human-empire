# 48. Why did the factory run on coal?

*Britain, about 1700 to 1850, with links to the American South and Bengal*

## Water in the mine

Every mine is a hole that the ground tries to fill. Rain soaks down through the soil and rock, and water that has been moving underground for centuries finds the new space and pours into it. A shallow pit can be bailed out by hand. A deeper one needs machinery, and around 1700 the coal and metal mines of Britain were getting deeper every decade. The seams near the surface had been worked for generations. To reach the coal below them, miners sank shafts further down, and the further down they went, the faster the water came in.

The machinery of the day was old and simple. Buckets on a chain, or a chain of leather discs pulled up through a pipe, could lift water out of a shaft. Something had to turn the drum that pulled the chain. Where a stream ran nearby, a waterwheel could do it. Elsewhere it was horses, walking in a circle around a vertical axle hour after hour, a machine called a horse gin. Horses had to be bought, fed, stabled and replaced, and a large mine needed many. How many is hard to pin down. The most often repeated figure comes from the natural philosopher John Theophilus Desaguliers, who reported that 500 horses were kept constantly at work at a colliery at Griff in Warwickshire. He was writing about a proposal to drain that mine with an engine, and his accuracy has been questioned since, so the number is better read as a sign of what contemporaries believed than as a count. Below a certain depth no number of horses could keep up, and a flooded mine had to be abandoned.

The first machine to solve this problem at scale went to work in 1712 at the Conygree coalworks at Tipton, in Staffordshire, near Dudley Castle. Its builder was Thomas Newcomen, an ironmonger, a dealer and worker in iron goods, from Dartmouth in Devon, born about 1663 or 1664. His partner was John Calley, whose trade is variously recorded as plumber and glazier. The Dudley engine is generally accepted as the first that worked successfully, though there may have been earlier attempts, one near Wolverhampton and perhaps one at the Wheal Vor mine in Cornwall around 1710. One account gives the Dudley engine a cylinder about 53 centimetres across, lifting water from about 47 metres down at twelve strokes a minute, some 45 litres a stroke, or more than 500 litres a minute. That account comes from a single popular history and has not been checked against the engraving of the engine made in 1719, so it is a reported figure, not a measured one.

Newcomen was not the first to try raising water with fire. In 1698 a military engineer named Thomas Savery had been granted a patent, a royal grant of the sole right to use an invention for a fixed term, for a device that did it, and in 1702 he published a book to sell it, *The Miner's Friend*. Savery's machine had no piston. Steam filled a closed vessel and was cooled until it condensed; the vacuum left behind sucked water up a pipe, and fresh steam at high pressure then pushed it further up. Two flaws made it nearly useless in a mine. Suction can lift water only about 9 metres, because that is as high as the weight of the air can push it, so the machine had to sit deep in the shaft. And the high-pressure steam burst the joints that the craftsmen of the time could make. At one installation steam was reported to have torn the whole machine to pieces.

Savery's patent nonetheless shaped what came next. It covered raising water by fire in general, and in 1699 an Act of Parliament had extended it to 1733. Newcomen's engine worked on a different principle, but it raised water by fire, so he had to work under Savery's patent and share the proceeds. After Savery died in 1715, the rights passed to a company called the Proprietors of the Invention for Raising Water by Fire, with Newcomen on its committee. Newcomen died in 1729, four years before the patent ran out. By 1733 somewhere between about 100 and 125 of his engines had been built, a few of them abroad.

Here is the puzzle at the centre of this chapter. Measured by how much of the energy in its fuel it turned into useful work, the Newcomen engine was one of the most wasteful machines ever put into regular service. It threw away more than ninety-nine parts in every hundred of the heat in its coal. Yet mine owners bought it, and kept buying it for seventy years. Why? The answer explains where the coal-burning factory came from, and why it appeared first in Britain.

## An economy that ran on sunlight

Before coal, nearly all the energy any society used came from the sunlight of the past year or so. People and draught animals ran on food, which was plants or animals that had eaten plants. Houses were warmed and iron was smelted with wood or charcoal, which is wood partly burned in a low-oxygen kiln to drive off water and leave nearly pure carbon. Wind turned some mills and falling water turned others, and both were driven by the sun's heat moving the air and lifting water into clouds. The historian E. A. Wrigley called this the "organic economy": one in which energy and most raw materials came from things that grew.

An organic economy has a ceiling, and the ceiling is land. A field could grow wheat for people, or oats and hay for horses, or trees for fuel and timber, but not all three at once. Every new use of energy competed with every other for the same acres. A society could grow richer for a while, but sooner or later more fuel meant less food. Chapter 26 described Song China, whose iron and coal output was enormous for its time; why it did not break through is a question this chapter returns to.

England had begun to slip under that ceiling long before the engine. Coal is the remains of plants that grew hundreds of millions of years ago, compressed and buried, so burning it draws on the sunlight of a vast stretch of past time rather than the current year's growth. By one widely used estimate, Britain was mining roughly 3 million tonnes of coal a year around 1700. Wrigley's figures, as reproduced by later scholars, suggest that coal already supplied about half of all the energy used in England and Wales in the first decade of the eighteenth century, and about four-fifths a century later. Much of it went to London, which had largely switched from wood to coal for its houses and workshops. The coal came by sea from the coalfield around Newcastle in the northeast. By one secondary estimate the east-coast coal trade already took about half the tonnage of the English merchant fleet by 1700, and by 1800 one account counts some 600 ships, called colliers, carrying about 1.35 million tons a year into the Thames.

So by 1700 Britain had a large and growing coal industry, and that industry had a drainage problem. The engine was invented to serve an industry that already existed. Coal did not follow the steam engine into British life. The steam engine was born inside a coal economy.

## What people knew

The Newcomen engine is often called a steam engine, but steam did not push it. It worked by making a vacuum and letting the weight of the air do the pushing, and that idea came out of half a century of argument among natural philosophers, as the people who studied nature were then called, about whether air had weight and whether empty space could exist at all.

In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli, a student of Galileo, filled a glass tube with mercury, closed the end and turned it upside down into a dish of mercury. The column fell until it stood about 76 centimetres high, and stayed there. Torricelli explained that the weight of the atmosphere pressing on the open dish held the column up, and that the space at the top of the tube held nothing. His tube was the first barometer. Otto von Guericke, mayor of Magdeburg in Germany, built a pump that could draw the air out of a vessel. In 1654 at Regensburg, before the Holy Roman Emperor, and again at Magdeburg in 1656, he fitted two copper hemispheres together, pumped out the air between them, and had teams of horses try to pull them apart. They could not; only the air outside held the halves together. In 1690 the French physicist Denis Papin built a small model in which steam under a piston was condensed by cooling, so that the air pushed the piston down into the vacuum. That is, in miniature, the principle of Newcomen's engine.

How much of this Newcomen knew is uncertain. A story first printed in 1797, in John Robison's article on steam engines for the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, held that papers at the Royal Society showed Robert Hooke corresponding with Newcomen and pointing him towards Papin's method. When the historian Rhys Jenkins searched the Royal Society's well-kept papers in the 1930s, he found no trace of any such letters, and Hooke died in 1703, before any Newcomen engine is documented. Jenkins thought Newcomen might have read an account of Papin's work in the Royal Society's journal, the *Philosophical Transactions*. That is plausible and unproved. A study published in 2022 reopened the question of possible links and called its own conclusions provisional. What can be said is that the science of the vacuum existed before the engine, and the engine uses it.

The other half of what people knew is what they did not know. No one in 1712 knew what heat was. There was no concept of energy, nothing to say that the heat in a lump of coal and the work of lifting water were two forms of the same thing, and no law setting a limit on how much work a given amount of heat could produce. The French engineer Sadi Carnot first worked out that limit in 1824, in a study of the motive power of fire, and he did it by thinking about engines that had been running for a century. In this story the science of air pressure came before the machine, though no one has shown a direct line from one to the other, and the science of heat came after the machine and was built by thinking about it. The book's running argument, over whether ideas or material conditions drive history, has no single answer even inside one machine.

## How the atmospheric engine worked

Newcomen's machine was called the atmospheric engine, because the atmosphere did its work. Its parts were a boiler, a vertical cylinder open at the top with a piston inside it, a great wooden beam pivoted at its middle like a seesaw, and a set of pump rods hanging down the mine shaft.

The boiler sat under the cylinder over a coal fire and made steam at barely more than the pressure of the surrounding air. One end of the beam was chained to the piston. The other end was chained to the pump rods, which went down the shaft to pumps at the bottom. Those rods were heavy, and their weight pulled their end of the beam down, which lifted the other end and drew the piston up to the top of the cylinder. As the piston rose, a valve let steam from the boiler flow into the cylinder beneath it.

Then the engine man, or later a set of valves worked by the beam itself, closed the steam valve and opened a second one that sprayed a jet of cold water into the cylinder. The cold water condensed the steam almost at once. Steam takes up far more room than the water it is made from, by a factor of more than a thousand at ordinary pressure, so when it turned back into water the cylinder was left nearly empty. That was the vacuum.

Now the air above the piston was pressing down on it, and almost nothing below was pressing up. The atmosphere presses on everything at the Earth's surface with a force of about 10 tonnes on every square metre. A piston half a metre across carries roughly two tonnes of air on its top face, and with a good vacuum underneath, most of that force drives it down. The piston fell, pulling its end of the beam with it. The beam rocked, the far end rose, and the pump rods lifted a load of water up the shaft. The condensed steam and the injection water drained out of the bottom of the cylinder. The weight of the pump rods pulled the piston back up, steam was let in again, and the cycle repeated, ten or twelve times a minute.

[DIAGRAM: One stroke of the Newcomen engine in four steps: steam fills the cylinder as the weight of the pump rods lifts the piston; cold water is sprayed in and the steam condenses; air pressure drives the piston down; the beam rocks and lifts water from the mine. An eighteenth-century engraving of a Newcomen engine may serve if a clear, freely licensed copy is found.]

The engine's flaw was built into its method. The same cylinder had to be hot to hold steam and cold to condense it. Each jet of cold water chilled not only the steam but the iron or brass walls of the cylinder and the piston. When the next charge of steam came in, much of it condensed against the cold metal before it could fill the space, and the boiler had to keep sending steam until the walls were hot again. Most of the coal was burned to reheat the cylinder, stroke after stroke, twelve times a minute, all day and all night.

Engineers at the time measured an engine's economy by its "duty": how much weight of water it could raise how far for each bushel of coal it burned, a bushel being a fixed measure of volume. Later historians have converted those duty figures into thermal efficiency, the share of the heat in the fuel that ends up as useful work. The average Newcomen engine in the Newcastle district in 1769 works out at somewhat under 1 percent. In the early 1770s the engineer John Smeaton, who studied engines systematically and redesigned their proportions, built improved engines that reached about 1.4 percent. Over ninety-nine of every hundred parts of the coal's energy went up the chimney or into warming the cylinder and the cooling water.

Why did anyone pay for such a machine? Because at a coal mine the fuel was almost free. Mining produced large amounts of small coal and slack, the dust and fragments that householders and ships' buyers would not take. It could not be sold, but it could be burned under a boiler at the top of the shaft. Against an engine eating unsaleable coal stood the alternative of dozens of horses, with their fodder, their keepers and their limits, or a drowned pit. The engine's waste of fuel hardly mattered when the fuel cost next to nothing. By about 1775 roughly 600 Newcomen engines had been built, most of them at collieries.

Where fuel was not free, the arithmetic turned. The tin and copper mines of Cornwall, at the southwestern tip of Britain, were deep and wet, and the ore was valuable. But Cornwall had almost no coal. Every ton for its engines came by sea from Wales and was expensive, and the fuel bill held back the use of Newcomen engines there even after they began to spread in the 1720s. Cornwall was where saving coal first paid handsomely, and it was where the next improvement found its first big market.

## What had to change: Watt and after

In 1763 or 1764 the University of Glasgow gave a model Newcomen engine to its instrument maker, James Watt, to repair. Watt made and mended scientific instruments; he was not a professor, but he worked among them. The model would run only a few strokes before stopping. In working out why, Watt saw where the heat went. A small cylinder has a large surface for its volume, so the model lost proportionally even more heat to its walls than a full-size engine, and its boiler could not keep up.

The answer came to him in 1765. Keep the cylinder hot all the time, and condense the steam somewhere else. Watt connected the cylinder by a pipe to a separate vessel, the condenser, kept cold by a bath of water. When a valve between them opened, the steam rushed out of the hot cylinder into the cold condenser and turned to water there, leaving the vacuum behind in the cylinder without chilling it. A pump drew off the water and any air from the condenser. He also closed the top of the cylinder and surrounded it with a jacket of steam, so that its walls never cooled. The cylinder stayed as hot as the steam; the condenser stayed as cold as the water. Nothing had to be reheated at every stroke.

[DIAGRAM: The Newcomen cylinder beside Watt's, showing the cold water sprayed straight into the Newcomen cylinder, and in Watt's engine the steam drawn off through a pipe into a separate cold condenser while the cylinder stays hot inside a steam jacket.]

Watt patented the separate condenser in 1769. It then took him most of a decade to make an engine that worked at full size, and a large part of the delay was a problem of manufacture. For the vacuum to hold, the piston had to fit the cylinder closely along its whole length, and the iron cylinders that foundries could produce were not round or straight enough. The answer came from the arms trade. In 1774 the ironmaster John Wilkinson patented a machine for boring cannon in which the cutting tool was carried on a bar supported at both ends, running right through the casting, so it did not wander as older borers did. Adapted to boring engine cylinders, it produced a bore true enough to seal. Wilkinson made the cylinder for Boulton and Watt's first commercial engine and won the contract to supply their cylinders. The precision the engine needed came from a machine designed to make guns.

In 1775 Watt went into partnership with Matthew Boulton, a Birmingham manufacturer of buttons, buckles and other small metal goods whose works at Soho outside the town were among the largest of their kind. The same year Parliament extended Watt's patent to 1800. Boulton supplied money, workshops and salesmanship, and he understood what the partnership was selling. James Boswell, visiting Soho in 1776, recorded Boulton telling him, "I sell here, Sir, what all the world desires to have, – POWER." Boswell is the only source for the line, printed in his *Life of Samuel Johnson* in 1791.

The firm's way of charging shows where the value of the invention lay. Boulton and Watt did not mainly sell engines; customers often had the engines built locally under their supervision. What the firm charged for was the right to use the condenser, and the charge was one third of the value of the coal the customer saved each year, compared with a Newcomen engine doing the same work. The saving was large. Against the ordinary Newcomen engine of the 1760s, a Watt engine burned roughly a quarter to a third as much coal for the same pumping, a saving of about two-thirds to three-quarters; against Smeaton's best improved engines it was smaller, roughly half to two-thirds. In thermal terms the Watt engine reached about 2 percent, against under 1 percent for the average Newcomen engine. The fee was worth paying only where coal was dear, which meant Cornwall, the firm's first big market. Between 1777 and 1801 Boulton and Watt erected 49 pumping engines there, and by one account all but one of Cornwall's Newcomen engines had been displaced by 1783. On the coalfields, where fuel was nearly free, the old engines carried on. Of the roughly 2,000 to 2,500 steam engines of all kinds built in Britain during the eighteenth century, fewer than 500 were Boulton and Watt engines.

So far every engine only rocked up and down. That suits a pump, but nearly all other machinery, from millstones to spinning frames, needs a shaft that turns, and Boulton pressed Watt in the early 1780s to make the engine drive one. A crank was the obvious device, but someone else held a patent on its use with engines, so in 1781 Watt patented a way round it, the sun-and-planet gear, in which a toothed wheel on the end of the connecting rod travelled round a second toothed wheel on the shaft and turned it. In 1782 he patented a double-acting engine, in which steam pushed the piston in both directions, and in 1788 he fitted a centrifugal governor, a pair of weighted balls on a spinning shaft that flew outward as the engine sped up and closed the steam valve, holding the speed steady. With these the engine could drive a factory.

Watt's patent ran until 1800. Whether it held back better engines is debated. The economists Michele Boldrin and David Levine argue that the patent, and especially its extension in 1775, delayed the spread of steam power and kept improvers from building on Watt's design; other historians dispute how much it mattered. The clearest case is high-pressure steam. Watt's engines used steam at little more than the pressure of the air, relying on the vacuum for their force, and he regarded high pressure as dangerous. The Cornish engineer Richard Trevithick was working with high-pressure steam from 1797, partly because an engine without a condenser stayed clear of Watt's patent. Steam at several times atmospheric pressure could push a piston directly, with enough force that the engine could be made small and light. Trevithick's high-pressure road carriage ran in 1801, and on 21 February 1804 his locomotive at Penydarren in South Wales hauled 10 tons of iron and about 70 men along a tramway. Boulton and Watt made much of a Trevithick engine that exploded at Greenwich in 1803, killing four people. The work on high pressure began before the patent lapsed; its commercial spread came after.

An engine also needed coal brought to it, and coal is heavy. Carried by packhorse or cart over the rutted roads of the eighteenth century, it grew dearer with every mile from the pit. Water carriage was far cheaper. In 1761 the Duke of Bridgewater opened a canal from his mines at Worsley to Manchester, crossing the river Irwell on an aqueduct at Barton. Within about a year the price of coal in Manchester had fallen by about half. A wave of canal building followed, and the map of where it was cheap to burn coal began to spread outwards from the coalfields.

Even so, the simple story of steam replacing everything after Watt is wrong, and the correction matters for the question in this chapter's title. The historian John Kanefsky estimated the power capacity installed in Britain, mainly in mining and manufacturing. In 1800 he counted about 35,000 horsepower of steam against about 120,000 of water: water still supplied about seven-tenths of the total, steam about a fifth. Only around 1830 did steam match water, at roughly 160,000 horsepower each. By 1870 steam had about 2 million horsepower and water about 230,000. The economist Alessandro Nuvolari, who reproduces these estimates, warns that they count engines installed rather than power used, and probably overstate steam before 1830 because many small waterwheels escaped the count; on his reading steam became the general source of industrial power only from the 1840s. In cotton, the steam engine became the usual power for mills from around the 1820s, and by 1860 Lancashire's cotton mills used about 300,000 horsepower, of which only about 18,500 came from water. Recent studies pull in different directions. Sean Bottomley argued in 2025 that steam was more widely used in manufacturing than older estimates suggest, and a study led from the University of Glasgow in 2024 found water power abundant across most of Britain, with Greater Manchester a notable exception.

So the first factories did not run on coal. They ran on water, and the switch took two generations.

## Steam meets cotton

The industry that made the factory began with an imported fashion. In the late seventeenth century the English East India Company, whose rule in Bengal chapter 44 followed, shipped home printed and painted cotton cloth from India, called calico. It was light, washable, brightly coloured and cheap, and it was wildly popular. Wool and silk manufacturers, powerful in Parliament, wanted it stopped. An Act of 1700 banned the import of dyed or printed calicoes from India, China and Persia, though plain white cloth could still come in and be printed in England. From 1721 a second Act went further and banned wearing or using printed or painted cotton cloth at all, foreign or domestic. Cloth woven with a linen warp and a cotton weft, called fustian, was allowed, and the ban on printed cottons was repealed only in 1774. How much these Calico Acts protected a British cotton industry is debated. What they show is that British buyers wanted a product that India made better and more cheaply than anyone in Britain could.

Making cloth involves two main tasks. Spinning turns a loose mass of cleaned fibres into yarn: the fibres are drawn out into a thin, even strand, and twisted so that they grip one another and the strand holds together. Weaving interlaces two sets of yarn, the lengthwise warp and the crosswise weft, on a loom. Spinning was the bottleneck: a weaver needed several spinners to keep busy, and hand spinning was slow work done mostly by women and girls at home.

A sequence of British machines broke the bottleneck. James Hargreaves's spinning jenny, invented around 1764 or 1765 and patented in 1770, was a hand-turned frame that let one spinner work several spindles at once. Richard Arkwright's water frame, patented in 1769, drew out the cotton between pairs of rollers turning at different speeds, each pair faster than the last, before twisting it, and it was too heavy for human muscle. Arkwright built a mill for it at Cromford in Derbyshire in 1771 and 1772, driven by a waterwheel on a stream, and ran it day and night in two twelve-hour shifts. Many historians treat Cromford as the first factory in the modern sense: many machines driven from a single power source, with workers gathered in one building and kept to the clock. Samuel Crompton's mule of 1779 combined the roller of the water frame with the moving carriage of the jenny and made yarn both fine and strong. A hand-worked mule could carry about 100 spindles. Driven by power, a single mule carried more than 1,200 by the early 1830s.

Why did the number of spindles matter so much? Because one power shaft running the length of a mill could turn every machine on every floor, and each machine could turn hundreds of spindles, while the workers' job shrank to feeding, watching and mending broken threads. The labour in each pound of yarn fell away. One set of estimates, compiled from the work of the historian Harold Catling, gives the hours of work needed to spin 100 pounds (about 45 kilograms) of fine yarn as about 2,000 on Crompton's first mule in 1780, 1,000 on a 100-spindle mule in 1790, 300 on a power-assisted mule in 1795, and 135 on an automatic mule in 1825. The economic historian Robert Allen sets beside these a figure of about 50,000 hours for the best Indian hand spinners, a number whose original source has not been traced and whose yarn is not exactly comparable, but which shows the order of the change. Prices followed. Yarn is graded by its count, the number of standard lengths in a pound, so a higher number means finer yarn. On the economic historian Knick Harley's series, the real price of a medium warp yarn fell nearly eightfold between the early 1780s and the early 1820s; coarse weft yarn fell about fourfold; and very fine yarn fell about fifteenfold by the late 1820s. Most of the fall came after 1790.

Weaving took longer to mechanize. Edmund Cartwright patented a power loom in 1785, but it was not commercially successful. Better looms followed, one by William Horrocks in 1813, and the loom of Sharp and Roberts, from 1822, made power weaving reliable. Allen counts about 2,400 power looms in Britain in 1813, about 14,000 in 1820, and about 100,000 by 1833. The last step in spinning came from a strike. During a spinners' strike in 1824, manufacturers asked the engineer Richard Roberts to build a mule that would run without the skilled men who guided its carriage. He patented a self-acting mule in 1825, and an improved one in 1830.

None of this would have mattered without raw cotton, which cannot be grown in Britain's climate. In the eighteenth century British mills drew it from the West Indies, the eastern Mediterranean and, increasingly, Brazil, which supplied about two-fifths of the raw cotton reaching Liverpool between 1791 and 1801. The United States was barely a supplier before the 1790s. The cotton that grew easily across the American South was a short-staple variety whose sticky green seeds clung to the fibre, and cleaning it by hand was so slow that it hardly paid to grow. In 1793 Eli Whitney built a saw gin, from "engine," in which wire teeth on a turning drum pulled the fibre through slots too narrow for the seeds; he patented it in 1794. Roller gins had long cleaned the smooth-seeded long-staple cotton of the coast, but Whitney's design suited the upland crop. An early gin cleaned about a quarter of a bale a day. Steam-driven gins of the late 1850s cleaned five or six.

Cotton spread across the South and west into Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana and Texas. The American crop grew from a few thousand bales in 1790 to nearly 4 million bales, each of about 227 kilograms, in 1860. US cotton was a minority of British imports around 1800 and a majority by about 1810. It made up about three-fifths of British cotton imports by weight in 1820, about four-fifths in 1830, and between three-quarters and four-fifths through the 1850s. The labour that grew and picked it was enslaved. The United States counted about 700,000 enslaved people in its first census in 1790, and 3,953,760 in 1860, the last census to count them.

[MAP: The cotton chain about 1850: plantations of the American South to the port of New Orleans, across the Atlantic to Liverpool, by rail and canal to the mills of Manchester and Lancashire, and on as cloth to markets in Europe, Latin America and India.]

The mill and the plantation grew together. A child piecing broken threads in a Manchester mill and an enslaved woman picking cotton in Mississippi worked at two ends of one chain, joined by merchants in Liverpool and New Orleans and by the credit that flowed between them. Chapter 43 described the plantation's timetable in Caribbean sugar; chapters 51 and 52 follow cotton slavery west and to the war it caused.

The chain had a third end, in India. Indian spinners and weavers, whose cloth had set the standard for the world, lost their export markets. On the economic historian Michael Twomey's figures, India sent about 2.2 million pieces of cotton cloth a year to Britain in the 1790s and fewer than 300,000 in the 1830s. Bengal's shipments peaked in the first decade of the nineteenth century at over 1.3 million pieces a year and had almost vanished by the 1830s. British cloth then pushed into India's own market. How far that went is disputed. Estimates collected by the economic historians Stephen Broadberry and Bishnupriya Gupta put British goods at under 4 percent of Indian cloth consumption in the early 1830s, about 10 percent in 1850 and about 35 percent by the late 1850s, so on their reading Indian producers supplied most of their home market until at least the 1860s. They attribute Britain's victory mainly to productivity: their estimate of Lancashire's labour cost per unit of cloth fell from more than twice India's in 1770 to about a third of India's by 1820. Prasannan Parthasarathi argues instead that British policy decided the contest, through tariffs on Indian cloth in Britain and Company rule that opened India to Lancashire goods. David Clingingsmith and Jeffrey Williamson treat British productivity, the decline of Mughal power and climate shocks in India as complementary causes. Chapter 49 returns to the Indian cloth maker.

## Every party's perspective

**The mine owner** faced a decision that could be written as a sum. Drain the pit deeper, at the cost of an engine and its upkeep, or abandon it. At a colliery the fuel was waste coal, and the engine paid. In Cornwall the fuel was carried by sea, and an engine that saved two-thirds of it paid a third of the saving to Boulton and Watt and still came out ahead.

**The mechanic** turned ideas into machines that worked. Newcomen was an ironmonger, Watt an instrument maker, Wilkinson an ironmaster, Trevithick the son of a mine manager; Roberts was a machinist. Around them were millwrights, smiths, clockmakers and fitters who built the engines, kept them running and made a thousand small improvements that no one patented. Britain had an unusually large number of such people, and their role is one of the explanations of why industrialization began there, discussed below.

**The mill owner** had to choose a source of power. Water was the established choice and, many historians think, the cheaper one for decades. But it tied a mill to a fast stream, usually in a valley in the hills and often far from any town, and it failed in drought and froze in hard winters. Rural mill owners took in children from workhouses and built housing to attract families. A steam mill could stand in a town where workers were plentiful, and it ran when the owner chose. Andreas Malm argues, in *Fossil Capital* (2016), that this is why owners switched. On his account water was neither scarce nor dearer than steam, nor technically backward; owners chose coal for the control it gave them over where and when people worked. A river's water had to be shared among the mills along it, and engineers who proposed reservoirs to regulate its flow found owners unwilling to accept common management. Steam put mills where labour was easy to find and hard to organize, and Malm treats workers' strikes as the trigger for a switch that he describes as a slow contest over decades. His critics accept much of the history and question the framing. Matthew Huber, reviewing the book sympathetically, points out that the advantages Malm calls social were also material: water really did fail in droughts and frosts, and coal supplied heat for chemicals and iron, which no waterwheel could. Others say steam was chosen because it was more profitable, which is not the same as cheaper. And the Glasgow study's finding that Manchester was unusually short of water power offers a material reason for the move to steam in the very place Malm studies.

**The mill worker** was very often a woman or a child. In 1835 children under 13 made up roughly one in six of the workforce in textile factories, and people under 18 roughly two in five; estimates differ with the source and the age limits used. The day was set by the engine. When the power came on, every machine in the building started, and the work kept pace with it until the power went off. Fines punished lateness, talking and spoiled work. Cotton spinning rooms were kept hot and damp so that the threads would not snap, and the air was full of fibre.

The most vivid evidence comes from parliamentary inquiries. In 1832 a committee of the House of Commons under Michael Sadler, a campaigner for limiting children's hours, took evidence on a bill to restrict children's labour in the mills. One witness was Matthew Crabtree, then 22, who had started work at eight in a woollen mill making blankets and stayed four years. His examination, as printed, ran:

> Will you state the hours of labour at the period when you first went to the factory, in ordinary times?--From 6 in the morning to 8 at night.
> Fourteen hours?--Yes.
> With what intervals for refreshment and rest?--An hour at noon.
> When trade was brisk what were your hours?--From 5 in the morning to 9 in the evening.
> Sixteen hours?--Yes.

Later he explained how the children were kept awake as the day wore on. The overlooker, called the slubber, was himself driven by the machine:

> the machine turns off a regular quantity of cardings, and of course, they must keep as regularly to their work the whole of the day; they must keep with the machine, and therefore however humane the slubber may be, as he must keep up with the machine or be found fault with, he spurs the children to keep up also by various means but that which he commonly resorts to is to strap them when they become drowsy.

[IMAGE: A page of printed testimony from the Sadler Committee's report, Parliamentary Papers 1831–32, volume XV; a published record of sworn evidence, which cannot show how witnesses were chosen or what was left out.]

This evidence has limits that need stating. Sadler's committee was assembled to make the case for a bill, the questions were often leading, and witnesses were chosen with that purpose. Crabtree's mill made woollens, not cotton. His testimony describes the 1820s in a recollection given years later. The historian Hans-Joachim Voth, working from other kinds of records, finds that the working year lengthened by about a fifth between the 1750s and the 1830s, but mostly because people took fewer days off, not because the working day grew longer. A second inquiry, the Factory Commission of 1833, was set up partly to test the Sadler evidence. Parliament acted that year. The Factory Act of 1833 applied to textile mills driven by water or steam. It banned the employment of children under nine, except in silk mills; limited children under 13 to nine hours a day and 48 a week, phased in over two and a half years, with two hours of schooling a day; limited those aged 13 to 18 to twelve hours a day and 69 a week, with no night work; and appointed four paid inspectors with power to enter mills and take evidence on oath. Adult men were not covered.

**The handloom weaver** lived the whole arc of the machine age in one working life. When spinning was mechanized and weaving was not, cheap machine yarn created a flood of work for weavers at their looms at home. On Allen's figures the number of handloom weavers in cotton rose from about 37,000 in 1780 to about 240,000 around 1820. They were skilled men and their families, working at home on their own time, and for a generation they did well. One series, compiled early in the twentieth century by G. H. Wood, puts a weaver's weekly earnings in 1806 at about twice those of a factory worker. Then the power loom arrived. It did not make the weaver's work easier; it ended it. By the same series, weavers' money earnings fell by more than half by 1820 and further by 1830, by which time a weaver earned less than two-thirds of the steady factory wage. Falling prices for food and goods softened the fall in what that money could buy. By 1850 only about 40,000 to 50,000 handloom weavers remained in cotton, and power looms did nearly all the weaving. Chapter 49 follows the weavers and other workers who broke the machines that replaced them.

**The enslaved cotton picker** worked under a regime that made the mills' raw material cheap. Solomon Northup, a free Black man from New York who was kidnapped in 1841 and held as a slave in Louisiana for twelve years, described cotton picking in *Twelve Years a Slave* (1853):

> When a new hand, one unaccustomed to the business, is sent for the first time into the field, he is whipped up smartly, and made for that day to pick as fast as he can possibly. At night it is weighed, so that his capability in cotton picking is known. He must bring in the same weight each night following. If it falls short, it is considered evidence that he has been laggard, and a greater or less number of lashes is the penalty.

Northup gives an ordinary day's quota as 200 pounds, about 90 kilograms. His account is a published narrative, written after his rescue with the help of a white editor, and he describes one region in the 1840s and 1850s; but much of what he says can be set against plantation records.

Those records show that the amount an enslaved picker gathered in a day rose sharply over the period. Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode, working from the records of 142 plantations, find that the average daily picking rate roughly quadrupled between 1801 and 1862. They credit mainly new varieties of cotton with larger bolls that were easier to pick, and note that picking rates hardly rose for long-staple cotton, which did not get those varieties. Edward Baptist, in *The Half Has Never Been Told* (2014), argues that the rise came from what he calls a pushing system: quotas raised year after year and enforced by the whip, with enslaved people's own techniques taken over by planters. Olmstead and Rhode reply that output per slave in rice and tobacco did not rise in the same way, which is hard to square with a general increase in violence. The dispute is unresolved and has been heated. Neither side disputes that the whip was used, or that the gains went to the owners.

**Did ordinary people gain?** For the first two generations, not clearly. Robert Allen, using standard estimates of national output and of wages, calculates that output per worker in Britain rose by about 46 percent between 1780 and 1840, while the average real wage, what a week's pay could buy, rose by only about 12 percent. The gain went mostly to profits. After 1840 the pattern reversed: from 1840 to 1900 output per worker rose by about 90 percent and real wages by about 123 percent. Allen calls the long lag "Engels' pause," after Friedrich Engels, whose *Condition of the Working Class in England in 1844* described Manchester at its grimmest. Optimists, notably Gregory Clark, argue that real wages rose faster than these figures show, and a 2020 recalculation found a smaller gap, output per worker up about 38 percent and wages about 18. The evidence of bodies points towards the pessimists. Life expectancy at birth in the central districts of Manchester and Liverpool around 1840 was about 25 to 28 years, against an average of about 33 for a group of large English towns; historians dispute whether it had fallen in the preceding decades, not whether it was low. Studies of army recruits and other records suggest that the average height of children born from about the 1820s and 1830s fell and did not recover until after mid-century, though that evidence too has been challenged.

## Why Britain?

Historians largely agree on the facts set out above. They disagree about why they happened in Britain, and why in the eighteenth century, and not in the Netherlands, France, India or China, all of which had sophisticated economies. This is one of the most argued questions in economic history. Five main explanations follow, each with the evidence it explains best and its weakest point.

**High wages and cheap energy.** Robert Allen argues, in *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective* (2009), that the industrial revolution happened in Britain because it paid to invent it there. Comparing what wages could buy in cities across Europe and Asia, he finds that labour in London and Amsterdam was expensive and labour in Beijing and Delhi was cheap, while British coal was the cheapest energy anywhere. Turning a promising idea into a working machine is costly, so inventors and investors did it only where the machine would pay. Machines that replaced expensive labour with cheap coal and capital paid in Britain and not elsewhere. Allen calculated that around 1780 a spinning jenny returned about 38 percent a year on its cost in England, against about 2.5 percent in France and a loss in India. The Newcomen engine is the energy half of the same argument: a machine that burned enormous quantities of coal to replace horses made sense only where coal was almost free.

The weak point is the wage that matters. The jenny and the water frame replaced women and children spinning by hand, not the male breadwinners whose wages Allen mainly compares. Jane Humphries and Benjamin Schneider, in a study published in 2019, assembled evidence on spinners' earnings and found the work widespread, unproductive and poorly paid, with wages that had not risen before the machines arrived. If the labour replaced was cheap, the high-wage explanation loses its force for cotton. Allen replied in 2020 that their data could not show how many hours spinners worked, and so could not show hourly wages, and that much of their evidence came from schools and charities whose spinners were unusually unproductive. They answered that his reply rested on an unhistorical picture of spinners' work. The exchange is unresolved. Mokyr adds a broader objection: firms try to save every cost, not just the dearest, and on Christine MacLeod's study of patents only about a fifth of late eighteenth-century inventions aimed at saving labour. Nicholas Crafts, who finds Allen's theory defensible, notes that the jenny's poor showing in France came partly from its costing more to build there.

**A culture of useful knowledge.** Joel Mokyr argues, in *The Enlightened Economy* (2009) and *A Culture of Growth* (2016), that Britain led because of what he calls the Industrial Enlightenment: the conviction, inherited from the seventeenth-century philosopher Francis Bacon, that nature could be studied by experiment and the results put to practical use, and that such knowledge should be spread as widely as possible. Books, scientific societies and public lectures made it cheaper to find out what was known. Above all Britain had a deep pool of skilled craftsmen who could turn an idea into a working machine and keep improving it. Mokyr's account includes institutions too: he argues that Enlightenment ideas also changed how Britain was governed, reducing the scope for interest groups to block change. The explanation fits the stream of small improvements that made the engines and mills work.

Its weak point is that it is hard to measure and hard to confine to Britain. Allen examined 79 important inventors and found that links to Enlightenment science were common in steam but rare in textiles and metals, which mattered more to the economy. Gregory Clark, reviewing Mokyr's book, points out that the Enlightenment was a European movement, yet northern Italy grew poorer and East Prussia remained a society of lords and serfs; that the Dutch were richer and at the centre of the new science a century earlier; and that the gap between the start of the movement and the factories was several generations. Mokyr himself concedes that the evidence for parts of his case will not convince everyone. In his defence, Crafts notes that craftsmen's small improvements were rarely recorded, so a count of famous inventors will miss them.

**Coal in the right place and land overseas.** Kenneth Pomeranz argues, in *The Great Divergence* (2000), that the most advanced regions of China, above all the Yangzi Delta, and of Europe were broadly comparable in living standards until the late eighteenth century, and diverged in the nineteenth. Two pieces of luck, in his account, made the difference. The first was coal: Britain's lay close to its industry and to water transport, and its wet mines pushed it towards the Newcomen engine, while China's largest deposits lay in the north and northwest, far from the Yangzi. The second was the Americas, which supplied cotton, sugar and timber that Britain could not have grown. Pomeranz estimates that replacing imported cotton with home-grown wool would have needed some 9 million acres of sheep pasture around 1815 and over 23 million around 1830, when England had roughly 17 million acres of arable land; an acre is about 0.4 hectares. Estimates for cotton, sugar and timber together around 1830 run from about 23 to 30 million acres. These are his "ghost acres," land Britain used without having it at home; the figures reach this chapter second-hand and have been criticized in detail. E. A. Wrigley's separate argument explains why coal mattered so much: it lifted the ceiling that land set on energy. Together these explain the timing of the divergence and why it came through coal.

The weak points are the dating and the counterfactual. Robert Allen and several co-authors, comparing the real incomes of building workers, found in 2011 that eighteenth-century Chinese wages were close to those of the poorer parts of Europe, far below those of the northwest, and stagnant from the 1730s. Broadberry and Gupta found Indian and Chinese wages far below northwest Europe's, and later work by Broadberry dates the gap between England and the Yangzi region to about 1700 or earlier. Gregory Clark and David Jacks argue that coal could have been carried to industry or industry moved to coal, so its location was less decisive than Pomeranz claims. Pat Hudson points out that the wool calculation assumes sheep farming never improved and no other fibre could substitute, and studies of the land actually embodied in Britain's trade find much smaller figures, with coal generally counting for more than land overseas.

**Institutions.** Douglass North and Barry Weingast argued in 1989 that the Glorious Revolution of 1688, in which Parliament replaced James II with William of Orange and Mary and limited the crown's power over taxation, made the government's promises to repay its debts believable. Lenders trusted a state that Parliament held in check, so it could borrow cheaply, and property in general became more secure. Their own paper is about public finance, but others have extended it. Daron Acemoglu, James Robinson and Simon Johnson argue that Atlantic trade strengthened merchant groups in countries without absolute monarchs, and in *Why Nations Fail* (2012) Acemoglu and Robinson argue that 1688 gave England institutions open to everyone, which allowed new industries to destroy old ones a century later. Chapter 45 followed how the British state borrowed for its wars. This explanation fits Britain's ability to fund a navy that protected its trade and the general security of investment.

Its weak point is timing and evidence. A full century separates 1688 from the cotton mills. Gregory Clark, and separately Nathan Sussman and Yishay Yafeh, find no sudden fall in interest rates after 1688, and other work finds that English productivity and incomes were already rising before it. Gary Cox has defended 1688 as a turning point, but one that affected a narrower range of dealings than North and Weingast claimed. Reviewers of *Why Nations Fail* have called its argument single-cause and its evidence anecdotal.

**Empire, slavery and the state.** In *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944) the historian Eric Williams, later prime minister of Trinidad and Tobago, made two claims: that profits from the slave trade and the Caribbean plantations helped finance British industry, and that Britain abolished the slave trade and then slavery once the Caribbean economy had declined and industrial interests no longer needed it. His subject was the sugar islands; he wrote very little about cotton. The first claim has been tested hardest. David Eltis and Stanley Engerman calculated that Caribbean sugar added less than 2.5 percent to British national income, and that the falling price of raw cotton explained only about a seventh of the fall in yarn prices between 1784 and 1830. The economist Barbara Solow answered that measured against investment rather than income, the numbers look large: on Engerman's upper estimates, slave-trade profits alone were about half of one percent of national income but nearly 8 percent of all investment and about 39 percent of investment in commerce and industry. Eltis and Engerman reply that these are extreme upper bounds that assume every penny went into industry.

Recent work has widened the argument. Joseph Inikori argues that Atlantic trade as a whole, with Africa as both market and supplier, drove English industry. Maxine Berg and Pat Hudson, in *Slavery, Capitalism and the Industrial Revolution* (2023), point to state power and war backing trade, financial and commercial techniques developed in the slave economy, and the compensation paid to slave owners in the 1830s, part of which went into railways; they state that they do not argue slavery caused the industrial revolution. Prasannan Parthasarathi argues that policy decided the matter: protection against Indian cloth, beginning with the Calico Acts, and later Company rule in India, made the British cotton industry possible. Together these explain why cotton, a fibre grown abroad by enslaved people and sold in markets opened by force, led the way. The weak point is size and proof. Critics argue that the gains were small beside the whole British economy, that counting naval spending and compensation as benefits ignores their costs, and that the case is easier to make for capital and markets than for technology. Mokyr, reviewing Parthasarathi, notes that the Calico Acts had many exemptions and were weakly enforced, and that Britain's mines, roads and canals were built mostly by private investors.

These explanations are not exclusive, and most historians now combine several of them while disagreeing about the weights. They also divide along the line of this book's running dispute. High wages and cheap coal, coal's location and ghost acres, and the gains of empire and slavery are explanations from resources and material conditions. Useful knowledge and institutions are explanations from ideas and rules, although Mokyr's version joins the two. Steam and cotton needed both: cheap coal and a market for cloth gave the reasons to build the machines, and a society of tinkerers with secure property and access to knowledge gave the means.

China supplies the hardest test. Chapter 26 described Song China in the eleventh century, with its markets, its printing and its iron and coal. The historian Robert Hartwell estimated that northern Song iron output, about 115,000 tonnes a year, exceeded England's in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries; other estimates run from about 10,000 to about 70,000 tonnes. The economic historian Eric Jones called Song China the first credible candidate for an industrial revolution. It did not have one. One standard explanation is loss: in 1127 the Song lost their northern heartland, where the iron districts lay, to Jurchen invaders. Another, Mark Elvin's "high-level equilibrium trap," holds that traditional methods were so efficient and labour so plentiful that there was no pressure to mechanize; it was built mainly to explain the later Ming and Qing dynasties, and Justin Yifu Lin and others have challenged it. The coal-location and high-wage arguments both run easily against China, whose later commercial heartland lay far from its coal and whose labour was cheap. Arguments from culture and institutions have more trouble with a society that had printing, scholarship and markets on a vast scale, though their defenders point to what China lacked.

## Consequences

The first consequence was a new kind of city. Manchester was a market town of about 23,000 people in 1773. It had about 75,000 in 1801 and about 300,000 in 1851, more if neighbouring Salford and the suburbs are counted. It became the world's first industrial city, and the place Engels described.

The second was coal on a scale never seen. Britain mined roughly 3 million tonnes around 1700, somewhere between 10 and 15 million around 1800, and 40 to 50 million by 1850; the estimates differ by source. Cotton goods became Britain's largest export, on one widely reproduced estimate about two-fifths of all exports by value in the first decade of the nineteenth century and between two-fifths and a half through the 1830s and 1840s.

The third was the railway. Trevithick's high-pressure engine made a locomotive possible, and the first public railway to use steam locomotives, the Stockton and Darlington, opened on 27 September 1825 to carry coal from the collieries of County Durham to the river Tees. Its first train was hauled by *Locomotion No. 1*, built by Robert Stephenson and Company, though parts of the line used fixed engines and horses, and passengers rode in horse-drawn coaches until 1833. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened on 15 September 1830, was the first to rely entirely on steam locomotives. It carried goods and passengers between the port that received the cotton and the town that spun it. Chapter 50 follows what railways did to the making of nations.

The longest consequence was a new kind of economy. For the first time, a society's energy use no longer competed with its food supply for land. Coal from deep underground fed the boilers, and the land could feed people. That is why growth could continue for generations instead of running into the old ceiling. It is also why the carbon in that coal, laid down over millions of years, went into the air in a few centuries. Measurements of air trapped in Antarctic ice show carbon dioxide at about 275 to 284 parts per million before industrialization, and beginning to climb in the nineteenth century. Chapter 82 takes up what the human empire now runs on.

What persisted is as important as what changed. Waterwheels, horses and hand work continued for decades alongside steam. In 1851, Stephen Broadberry, Bruce Campbell and Bas van Leeuwen find, the number of people working in traditional trades untouched by the new machines far exceeded the number in factories, and farming was still the largest single occupation. The factory was the new thing, but it was not yet the typical workplace.

## A plausible alternative

Water was the alternative that people actually built. In 1825 the Shaws Water Company was formed at Greenock, on the Firth of Clyde in western Scotland, to carry out a scheme by the engineer Robert Thom, who already ran a water-power system at his cotton mills on the island of Bute. It opened on 16 April 1827. A large reservoir in the hills, later named Loch Thom, fed an aqueduct about 9 kilometres long, the Greenock Cut, that carried water to the town both for drinking and to turn waterwheels. Those wheels drove paper, cotton and woollen mills, sugar refineries, ropeworks and an iron foundry. James Watt and the engineer John Rennie had earlier judged such a scheme impractical. It worked, and the aqueduct stayed in service until 1971. Thom's later plans for Edinburgh and Glasgow were largely never built.

Malm argues that Thom and others proposed similar reservoir schemes for the rivers of Lancashire, including the Irwell that runs through Manchester, and that water was consistently the cheaper power. On his account, the schemes required mill owners to coordinate their use of water, accept planning and share the cost of construction, and under free competition those demands could not be met, so the largest schemes were never built. This is Malm's interpretation, and the finding that Greater Manchester was unusually short of water power gives a different reason for the same outcome.

What would staying with water have required? Mill owners along a river would have had to agree rules for sharing its flow and pay jointly for dams and reservoirs. Workers would have had to be brought to the valleys, or towns built there. Greenock shows it could be done in one place. Whether it could have been done across Lancashire is where history becomes speculation. Two things are not speculative. Water could have carried the textile industry further than it did; it powered most of it until about 1820. But a waterwheel cannot run on rails, cannot cross an ocean, and cannot be multiplied without limit, because the number of good streams is fixed. Coal had no such ceiling until the air itself became one.

## The answer, and what it left open

The working answer was that early engines were wasteful but ran where coal was almost free, and that the cause of Britain's lead is argued among several explanations. The first part held. The Newcomen engine turned less than one percent of its fuel into work, and it spread because at the pithead its fuel was waste. Watt's condenser was valuable exactly where coal was not free, and his firm charged by the coal it saved. The second part also held: the "why Britain" question remains open, and each explanation accounts for some of the evidence and fails on some.

But the working answer needs one revision, and it bears on the chapter's title. The factory did not at first run on coal. It ran on water, from Cromford in the 1770s until about the 1820s in cotton and later still across industry as a whole. Coal took over the mills slowly, over two generations, and why it did is itself disputed: because it was cheaper or more profitable, because it freed owners from rivers and placed mills where workers were, or because the place that led the industry ran short of water. Coal pumped the mines first and only later drove the mills and the railways. Whatever the reasons owners switched, its lasting effect was to remove the ceiling that land had set on every economy before it.

The scale of dependence had changed. A yard of cotton cloth sold in London in 1850 joined a miner hewing coal in Northumberland, an engine fitter in Manchester, an enslaved family in Mississippi working to a nightly weighing, a child at a mule in a hot spinning room, a merchant in Liverpool extending credit, and a weaver in Bengal who had lost his market. None of them knew the others, and each depended on the rest. The machines made far more cloth for each hour of work than had ever been made before. The question they left was who would get the gain: the owner who bought the engine, the worker who tended it, or the people at the far ends of the chain. Who should set the price of cloth, and of a day's work, is the argument of the next chapter.
