# 48. Why did the factory run on coal?

*Britain, about 1700 to 1850, with links to the American South and Bengal*

## Water in the mine

A coal mine is a hole dug below the level at which the ground is soaked with water, and water runs into it for as long as it stays open. Around 1700 this was the main limit on British mining. The seams near the surface, which could be drained by cutting a sloping tunnel out to a valley side, had been worked for generations and many were exhausted. The coal that remained lay deeper, and every extra metre of depth meant more water to lift and a longer distance to lift it.

The lifting was done by muscle or by a stream. Where a stream ran nearby, a waterwheel could work the pumps. Elsewhere horses walked in a circle, turning a drum that hauled up buckets or an endless chain of them. Horses eat every day whether the mine sells coal or not, and a deep, wet pit needed a great many. One writer of the time, the lecturer John Theophilus Desaguliers, reported that a colliery at Griff in Warwickshire kept 500 horses constantly at work on its water. His accuracy has been questioned and no independent record confirms the number, so it is best read as a sign of how the problem looked to contemporaries, not as a measurement. The choice facing an owner is clear enough without it. He could pay for more horses, or he could let the lower workings flood and lose the coal in them.

In 1712 a third option began work at the Conygree Coalworks at Tipton in Staffordshire, within sight of Dudley Castle. It was built by Thomas Newcomen, an ironmonger, meaning a dealer in iron goods and tools, from the port of Dartmouth in Devon, with his partner John Calley. It is generally accepted as the first steam engine that worked well enough to earn its keep, though there are traces of earlier attempts, one possibly at a tin mine in Cornwall in 1710. An engraving made in 1719 shows the Dudley engine. The figures usually quoted for it come from later accounts and rest on thin sourcing: a cylinder about 53 centimetres across, twelve strokes a minute, and each stroke raising about 45 litres of water from a depth of about 47 metres.

Newcomen was not the first to try. In 1698 Thomas Savery had patented a device for raising water by fire, and in 1702 he published a book about it called *The Miner's Friend*. Savery's machine had no moving piston. It filled a vessel with steam, cooled the vessel so that the steam turned back to water and left an empty space, and let that empty space suck water up a pipe. Suction can raise water only about nine metres, so the machine had to sit deep in the shaft, and to push the water any higher Savery used steam at pressures the metalwork of the day could not hold. At one installation the steam blew the joints apart, and at Wednesbury in Staffordshire it was said to have torn the whole machine to pieces. The miner's friend drained very few mines. But Savery's patent was worded broadly, Parliament extended it until 1733, and Newcomen had to go into partnership with him and sell his own, quite different engine under it.

Newcomen's engine did the job. It also wasted nearly all the coal it burned. For every hundred shovelfuls that went under its boiler, the useful work of less than one came out at the pump. The question of this chapter starts there. Why did anyone buy a machine so wasteful, and how did its descendants come to drive the factories of the first industrial country?

## An economy that ran on sunlight

To see why the engine mattered, start with where energy came from before it. In 1700 almost everything people did was powered by that year's sunlight, or the sunlight of a few recent years. Plants captured it. People and animals ate the plants and turned them into muscle power. Wood, grown over a few decades, gave heat for cooking, brewing, smelting metal and firing bricks. Wind and falling water, both driven by the sun's heating of the atmosphere, turned mills.

The historian E. A. Wrigley called this an "organic economy", meaning one in which nearly all energy and raw materials come from living plants. Its defining feature is that every use of energy competes for land. A field can grow wheat for people, or oats for horses, or timber for fuel, or grass for the sheep whose wool makes cloth. It cannot do all four. A society in this position can grow richer for a time through better farming, more trade and finer division of work, but each gain draws harder on a fixed area of land, and eventually the land runs short. This is the ceiling that Song China, the richest and most inventive society of its time, was working beneath in chapter 26.

Coal is a way through that ceiling. It is ancient plant matter, the sunlight of millions of years, stored underground and available without planting anything. A country that burns coal for heat frees its woodland for other uses, in effect gaining land it does not have.

England had started doing this long before any engine existed. London in particular heated its houses and ran its breweries, glassworks and forges on coal shipped down the east coast from the mines around Newcastle. By 1700 that coastal trade is said to have employed about half the tonnage of England's merchant ships. British mines were producing roughly 3 million tons a year at that date. By Wrigley's reckoning, coal already supplied about half of all the energy used in England and Wales in the first decade of the eighteenth century.

So the order of events is the reverse of what one might expect. Britain did not invent the steam engine and then go looking for coal to feed it. It had a large coal industry first, that industry had a drainage problem, and the engine was built to solve it.

## What people knew, and what they did not

The engine drew on one piece of recent science. During the seventeenth century, experimenters had established that air has weight and that the atmosphere presses on everything beneath it. In 1643 the Italian Evangelista Torricelli made the first barometer, a glass tube of mercury in which the weight of the air holds up a column of the liquid, and concluded that people live at the bottom of a sea of air. In 1654 Otto von Guericke, the mayor of the German city of Magdeburg, pumped the air out of two copper half-spheres fitted together and showed that teams of horses could not pull them apart. Nothing held them but the air pressing from outside. In 1690 the Frenchman Denis Papin built a small model in which steam under a piston was allowed to cool and condense, leaving a partial vacuum, so that the atmosphere pushed the piston down.

That is the principle of Newcomen's engine, which did not push with steam at all. It used steam only to make an empty space, and let the weight of the atmosphere do the work. The science of the vacuum came first and the engine came after.

Whether the science reached Newcomen is another matter. A story first printed in 1797 says he corresponded with Robert Hooke of the Royal Society, London's society of natural philosophers, who pointed him to Papin's method. Historians who searched the Society's papers found no such letters, and Hooke died in 1703. Newcomen may have read a published account of Papin's work, which is plausible and unproven. What can be said is that the idea was available in print, that an ironmonger in a Devon port built the machine that used it, and that no one has shown how the one got to the other.

On a second point the order ran the other way. Nobody in 1712, or in 1780, knew what heat was. There was no concept of energy and no rule for how much work a given quantity of heat could be made to yield. That rule was first worked out in 1824 by a French engineer, Sadi Carnot, and he arrived at it by thinking about steam engines that had by then been running for a century. In this case the machine came first and the science followed. The running argument of this book, over whether ideas or material things drive change, gets evidence for both sides from the same engine.

## How the atmospheric engine worked

Picture a see-saw the size of a house: a heavy wooden beam pivoted at its centre on a tall masonry wall. From one end of the beam, a chain hangs down the mine shaft to the pump rods. From the other end, a chain hangs down to a piston, a flat disc that slides up and down inside a vertical cylinder open at the top. Below the cylinder sits a boiler, in effect a large covered kettle over a coal fire, which makes steam at barely more than the pressure of the surrounding air.

One stroke of the engine has four steps.

First, the pump rods are heavy, so when the engine is at rest their weight pulls their end of the beam down and lifts the piston to the top of the cylinder.

Second, as the piston rises, a valve opens and steam from the boiler flows in beneath it and fills the cylinder. The steam does not lift anything. It simply occupies the space.

Third, the steam valve closes and a jet of cold water is sprayed into the cylinder. Steam takes up well over a thousand times the volume of the water it came from, so when the spray cools it back into water, almost nothing is left in the cylinder. For a moment there is a partial vacuum under the piston.

Fourth, the atmosphere presses down on the open top of the piston with a force of about one kilogram on every square centimetre, and now there is very little underneath to push back. On a piston half a metre across, that would be more than two tonnes if the vacuum were perfect, and a good deal less in practice. The piston is driven down, the beam rocks, and the far end hauls up the pump rods and a load of water with them.

Then the weight of the rods pulls the piston up again, steam flows in, and the cycle repeats, about ten to twelve times a minute. At first the valves were worked by hand. Soon they were opened and closed by pegs on a rod hanging from the beam, so the engine ran itself and needed only tending.

Caption: One stroke of an atmospheric engine, redrawn for the book; the drawing shows the principle and not the dimensions of any one engine.]

The flaw is in the third step. The cold spray condensed the steam, and it also chilled the cylinder walls. On the next stroke, fresh steam entering the cold cylinder condensed at once against the metal, and went on condensing until the whole cylinder had been heated back to the temperature of boiling water. Only then could the cylinder fill. So on every stroke, ten times a minute, the engine heated a large mass of metal and then deliberately cooled it again. Most of the coal went into that.

Engineers of the time measured an engine by its "duty": how many pounds of water it could raise one foot for each bushel of coal burned, a bushel being a standard measure of volume. The average for Newcomen engines around Newcastle in 1769 was 5.6 million. Converted into modern terms, that means less than 1 percent of the heat in the coal became useful work. The best atmospheric engines, as improved by the engineer John Smeaton in the 1770s, managed a little over 1 percent.

It paid anyway, because of where it stood. A coal mine produces, alongside the lumps it can sell, a great deal of small coal and dust that was hard to sell at any price. At the pithead, the mouth of the mine, this was the engine's fuel. Its coal was close to free, and the alternatives were a stable full of horses or a drowned mine. Under those conditions waste hardly mattered, and what counted was that the engine kept working. About 100 to 125 engines were built by the time Savery's patent ran out in 1733, and about 600 by 1775, most of them at coal mines.

The same arithmetic shows where the engine did not pay. Cornwall, in the far southwest, had deep mines of tin and copper and plenty of water in them, but no coal. Every ton came by sea from Wales and was expensive when it arrived. Newcomen engines were used there from the 1720s, and their appetite for fuel was a constant burden. Cornwall was the place where a mine owner had a strong reason to pay more for an engine that burned less.

## What had to change

### The separate condenser

In 1763 or 1764 James Watt, a maker of scientific instruments employed by the University of Glasgow, was given a small model of a Newcomen engine to repair. He got it running and was struck by how much steam it used for its size. He traced the loss to the heating and cooling of the cylinder on every stroke.

His answer, which came to him in 1765, was to stop cooling the cylinder at all. He added a second, smaller vessel beside it, joined to it by a pipe with a valve, and kept that vessel cold. When the piston reached the top of its stroke, the valve opened. Steam rushes toward any place where the pressure is lower, so it flowed out of the hot cylinder into the cold vessel and condensed there. The vacuum formed in the cylinder just as before, but the cylinder itself stayed hot, and the next charge of steam was not wasted reheating it. The cold vessel is the separate condenser. A small pump, worked by the engine, kept it cleared of water and air.

[DIAGRAM: Watt's separate condenser beside a Newcomen cylinder. Caption: In the Newcomen engine the steam is condensed inside the working cylinder, which is cooled on every stroke; in Watt's, it is drawn off into a cold vessel and the cylinder stays hot.]

Watt patented the idea in 1769. It then took most of a decade to make an engine that worked at full size, and the obstacle was metalworking. A Newcomen engine could tolerate a badly fitting piston, because a layer of water on top sealed the gap. Watt's hot cylinder could not be sealed with cold water. It needed a cylinder bored so accurately that a piston would fit it closely along its whole length, and nobody could make one.

The answer came from the arms trade. In 1774 the ironmaster John Wilkinson patented a machine for boring iron cannon. Its cutting tool was mounted on a rigid bar supported at both ends, so that it could not wander as it cut, and a version of it bored the cylinder for Watt's first commercial engine. Governments paying for accurate guns had, without intending it, paid for the tool that made the efficient steam engine possible.

### Selling power

In 1775 Watt went into partnership with Matthew Boulton, a Birmingham manufacturer with money and a large workshop, and Parliament extended the patent to 1800. The way the partners charged for their engine shows what they understood themselves to be selling. A customer paid for the parts and the building work, and then paid Boulton and Watt each year one third of the value of the coal the engine saved compared with a Newcomen engine of the same power. Against an ordinary Newcomen engine the saving was roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the fuel. The first Watt pumping engines achieved a duty of about 18 million, three times the old average, and by 1792 engines in Cornwall were reaching 32 to 35 million. That was still only a few percent of the heat in the coal.

A pricing scheme based on coal saved is worth most where coal is dear, and Cornwall became the firm's first big market. Boulton and Watt put up 49 pumping engines there between 1777 and 1801.

When the writer James Boswell visited Boulton's works in March 1776, he recorded his host as saying: "I sell here, Sir, what all the world desires to have, – POWER." The line survives only in Boswell's account, published fifteen years later.

### From pumping to turning

Up to this point every engine did one thing: it rocked a beam up and down. That suits a pump. It does not suit a mill, where machinery is driven by a turning shaft. An engine that pulled only on the downstroke also delivered its force in jerks.

In the early 1780s, pushed by Boulton, who saw that the market for pumping engines was limited, Watt made a series of changes. In 1781 he patented a gear that converted the beam's rocking into rotation. A rival held a patent on the obvious device, the crank, so Watt used a small gearwheel fixed to the end of the rod and made it circle a second gearwheel on the shaft, an arrangement called the sun-and-planet gear. In 1782 he patented the double-acting engine, in which the cylinder was closed at both ends and steam was let in on each side of the piston in turn, with the opposite side opened to the condenser, so the piston was driven both up and down. In 1788 he added the governor, a pair of metal balls spinning on a spindle driven by the engine. When the engine ran too fast the balls swung outward and, through a lever, partly closed the steam valve. When it slowed they dropped and opened it. The engine now held a steady speed without anyone touching it.

An engine that turned a shaft smoothly at a constant speed could drive a factory.

### After the patent

Watt's patent ran until 1800, and he used it to stop rivals. Whether that held back better engines is disputed. The economists Michele Boldrin and David Levine have argued that it did, pointing to improvers who could not legally sell their designs until it expired. Others dispute this reading.

The main line of improvement was high pressure. Watt's engines, like Newcomen's, used steam at close to the pressure of the air and relied on the vacuum. Watt distrusted anything stronger, with some reason, since boilers could explode. The Cornish engineer Richard Trevithick began working with high-pressure steam in 1797 and patented a high-pressure engine in 1802. If the steam itself pushes hard enough, the engine needs no condenser and no great beam, and it can be made small. Trevithick put one on a road carriage in 1801, and in February 1804 one of his engines hauled 10 tons of iron and about 70 men along a tramway at Penydarren in Wales. That was the first steam locomotive to run on rails.

### Getting coal to the engine

An engine away from the pithead needs coal brought to it, and coal is heavy and cheap, so the cost of carrying it over land by horse and cart could exceed the cost of mining it. In 1761 the Duke of Bridgewater opened a canal, an artificial waterway on which a single horse could tow a loaded barge, from his mines at Worsley into Manchester. Within about a year the price of coal in Manchester had fallen by about half. Canal building spread across the country in the decades that followed.

### A slow takeover

None of this happened quickly. The fullest estimates of Britain's installed power, compiled by the historian John Kanefsky, are given in horsepower, the unit Watt devised to compare his engines with the animals they replaced, equal to about three-quarters of a kilowatt. In 1800, a quarter of a century after Boulton and Watt set up, steam engines in mining and manufacturing amounted to about 35,000 horsepower. Waterwheels amounted to about 120,000. Steam drew level with water only around 1830, at about 160,000 horsepower each. By 1870 steam stood at about 2 million and water at 230,000. These are estimates of capacity installed, not of power used, and they probably miss many small waterwheels, which would make the early lead of water larger still. One recent reassessment argues that steam in manufacturing was more widespread than these figures suggest.

Roughly 2,000 to 2,500 steam engines of all kinds had been built in Britain by 1800, fewer than 500 of them by Boulton and Watt. Many of the rest were still Newcomen engines at coal mines, burning waste coal as before. The industry that first took steam into the factory in a large way was cotton, and there too the change came late. The first cotton mills ran on water. Steam became the usual power for new cotton mills only from about 1820. By 1860 the mills of Lancashire used about 300,000 horsepower, and only about 18,500 of it came from water.

## Steam meets cotton

### A cloth worth copying

Britain cannot grow cotton, and before the eighteenth century it made very little cotton cloth. The cloth that British buyers wanted came from India. Indian weavers produced light, washable cottons, and Indian dyers and printers decorated them with colours that did not run. Brought to Europe by the East India Company of chapter 44, they sold so well that makers of English wool and silk cloth demanded protection. Parliament obliged with two laws known as the Calico Acts, calico being a name for plain cotton cloth. The Act of 1700 banned the import of dyed or printed cottons from India, China and Persia. The Act of 1721 went further and banned the wearing or use of printed cotton cloth in Britain altogether, with exemptions for fine muslins and for fustian, a mixed cloth of linen and cotton. The ban on printed cottons was lifted in 1774.

How much these laws helped a British cotton industry to grow is argued over, and the argument returns later in the chapter. What is not in doubt is that British makers were trying to match a product that India made better and more cheaply.

### Breaking the spinning bottleneck

Cloth is made in two stages. Spinning turns loose fibre into yarn, and weaving turns yarn into cloth. Spinning by hand has two motions. The spinner draws out a loose rope of fibres until it is thin, and twists it so the fibres grip one another and hold. A spinning wheel helps with the twisting, but it makes one thread at a time. It took several spinners to keep one weaver supplied, and spinning was the work of women and children in cottages all over the country.

A run of machines changed this within two generations. About 1764 James Hargreaves, a Lancashire weaver, devised the spinning jenny, in which one person turning a handle worked a row of spindles at once, drawing out and twisting several threads together. In 1769 Richard Arkwright patented a machine that drew out the fibres by passing them between pairs of rollers, each pair turning faster than the one before. It needed more force than a person could supply, and at Cromford in Derbyshire in 1771 and 1772 Arkwright built a mill where a waterwheel drove it, which gave it the name water frame. In 1779 Samuel Crompton combined the rollers of the water frame with the moving carriage of the jenny in a machine called the mule, after the cross between a horse and a donkey. It could spin yarn fine and strong enough to match Indian muslin.

The economics of these machines lie in the single shaft. Once spinning is done by spindles in a frame, one source of power can turn a hundred of them as easily as ten, and the number one worker can mind keeps rising. A hand-driven mule was limited to about 100 spindles. A powered one had passed 1,200 by the early 1830s. One set of estimates gives the working hours needed to spin 100 pounds (45 kilograms) of fine yarn as 2,000 on Crompton's first mule in 1780, 300 on a power-assisted mule in 1795 and 135 on an automatic mule in 1825. A figure often quoted for the best Indian hand spinners is 50,000 hours, though its source is hard to trace and the yarns compared are not identical.

Prices followed. Measured against other goods, a coarse cotton yarn cost about a quarter as much in the 1820s as in the early 1780s, a medium yarn about an eighth, and the finest yarns about a fifteenth.

Cromford is often called the first factory in the modern sense, and the label is an interpretation, but the features are there. The machines were too large and too costly for a cottage, and they needed a power source. So the workers came to the machines, and since the wheel turned all the machines at once, they all started and stopped together. Cromford ran day and night in two shifts of twelve hours.

Weaving was mechanized more slowly. Edmund Cartwright patented a power loom, a loom driven by a shaft instead of by the weaver's hands and feet, in 1785, but it did not work well. Usable versions came in the 1810s and commercial success in 1822. Britain had about 2,400 power looms in 1813, about 14,000 in 1820 and about 100,000 by 1833.

The last of the series shows how the machines were bound up with relations between owners and workers. Mule spinners were skilled men, well paid and organized, and in 1824 they struck. Manufacturers asked the engineer Richard Roberts to build a mule that would carry out the spinner's movements by itself. He patented a first version in 1825 and a successful one in 1830. It was called the self-acting mule.

### Where the cotton came from

Every bale of raw material for this industry came from overseas. In the 1780s and 1790s it came from the Caribbean, the Ottoman lands and Brazil, which supplied about 40 percent of the raw cotton landed at Liverpool in the 1790s. American cotton was not imported regularly before 1791.

The obstacle in North America was the plant. The cotton that grew well across the inland South, called short-staple or upland cotton, has sticky seeds tangled in its fibre, and picking them out by hand was too slow to be worth doing on a large scale. In 1793 Eli Whitney, a New Englander staying on a Georgia plantation, built a machine in which wire teeth pulled the fibre through slots too narrow for the seeds. He patented this cotton gin, the word being short for engine, in 1794. Older gins using rollers had existed for other kinds of cotton. Whitney's was the one that suited the upland plant.

American output went from a few thousand bales in 1790 to nearly 4 million in 1860, a bale by then weighing about 225 kilograms. The United States supplied a minority of Britain's raw cotton around 1800, 59 percent of it by weight in 1820, and 80 percent in 1830. It stayed between about three-quarters and four-fifths until the American civil war.

That cotton was grown and picked by enslaved people. The first United States census, in 1790, counted about 700,000 enslaved people. The census of 1860 counted 3,953,760. Cotton pulled slavery westward into Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana and Texas, onto land taken from the Native nations of chapter 51, and hundreds of thousands of enslaved people were marched or shipped there from the older states. The mill and the plantation grew together, linked by the merchants of Liverpool, who bought the cotton, and by the credit that let planters buy land and people against next year's crop.

### The other end of the chain

The cloth that Lancashire had set out to imitate now faced Lancashire's competition. India's exports of cotton cloth to Britain ran at about 2.2 million pieces a year in the 1790s. By the 1820s they were about 430,000, and in the 1830s and 1840s about 300,000. Bengal, the province whose taxes the East India Company had been collecting since chapter 44, sent about 1.3 million pieces a year in the first decade of the century and about 6,000 a year in the 1830s.

India's export trade in cloth to Britain collapsed, then, and the timing is known. The effect inside India is less clear. One nineteenth-century estimate puts British goods at about 4 percent of India's own cloth market in the early 1830s and about 35 percent in the late 1850s, which means Indian spinners and weavers still clothed most Indians at mid-century. The cause is disputed too. The historian Prasannan Parthasarathi stresses policy: British duties on Indian cloth, and Company rule that opened India to Lancashire's goods. Stephen Broadberry and Bishnupriya Gupta stress the machines: by their estimate, the labour cost of making cotton goods in Lancashire fell from more than twice the Indian level in 1770 to about a third of it in 1820. Other economic historians treat the two as working together, alongside the disorder that followed the decline of the Mughal empire. Chapter 49 returns to the Indian cloth maker.

## The view from each position

### The mine owner

For the owner of a deep coal mine, the engine was a calculation with a clear answer. The fuel was his own unsaleable small coal and the alternative was losing the mine. He had little reason to care about efficiency, which is why wasteful Newcomen engines went on being built at coal mines long after Watt's patent.

### The mechanic

Newcomen sold ironware and Watt made instruments for a university. Wilkinson cast cannon, Hargreaves was a weaver, and Roberts built machine tools. Behind them stood men whose names are mostly lost: millwrights, who built and repaired mills, and smiths, clockmakers and instrument makers, who put up the engines, adjusted them, and made the many small improvements that turned a workable idea into a reliable machine. Britain had an unusually large number of such people. One of the explanations considered below treats that as the heart of the matter.

### The mill owner

An owner choosing between water and steam in the 1820s was weighing several things. A waterwheel, once built, ran on a free supply. But it tied the mill to a particular stretch of a particular river, often in a hill valley far from any town, and it slowed in a summer drought and stopped in a hard frost. A steam engine needed coal every day. It could be set up in a town, and it ran whenever the owner chose to run it.

The historian Andreas Malm has argued that cost was not what decided the matter. On his reading of the records, water remained the cheaper source of power through the years when owners moved to steam, and it was neither scarce nor technically backward. What steam offered was control. A mill in a remote valley had to recruit workers and house them, and they were hard to replace. A mill in a town could draw on a crowd of people looking for work, which weakened the hand of those already employed. And steam let the owner fix the working day himself instead of following the river. Malm points out that the move to steam was slow and that it sped up when workers were organizing and striking.

The argument has critics. Even a reviewer sympathetic to Malm noted that the advantages he treats as social were also plain physical ones, since a power source that does not freeze or run dry, and can be placed anywhere, is a better power source. A study published in 2024 found that water power was abundant across most of Britain but was fully used around Manchester, precisely where the cotton industry was thickest, which would have pushed mills there toward steam whatever their owners thought about labour. Whether steam cost more than water for a given amount of power, and for how long, is the disputed point, and it is not settled.

### The mill worker

Many of those who worked the machines were women and children. In textile factories in 1835, by the best available counts, about one worker in six was under thirteen and about two in five were under eighteen.

The fullest evidence for their days comes from inquiries held by Parliament. In 1832 a committee of the House of Commons chaired by Michael Sadler, who was promoting a bill to limit children's hours, took sworn evidence from mill workers. One was Matthew Crabtree, aged 22, who had started in a woollen mill at the age of eight and lived about two miles from it. Asked his hours in ordinary times, he answered, "From 6 in the morning to 8 at night," with an hour's break at noon, and when trade was brisk, "From 5 in the morning to 9 in the evening." Asked what happened if he arrived late, he said, "I was most commonly beaten." He then explained why the beating continued through the day. The children he describes worked between a machine that turned wool into loose ropes called cardings and an adult called a slubber, who drew the cardings out into coarse thread:

> the machine turns off a regular quantity of cardings, and of course, they must keep as regularly to their work the whole of the day; they must keep with the machine, and therefore however humane the slubber may be, as he must keep up with the machine or be found fault with, he spurs the children to keep up also by various means but that which he commonly resorts to is to strap them when they become drowsy.

This testimony has limits. Sadler assembled his committee to make a case, he chose the witnesses, and his questions were often leading. Crabtree was describing a woollen mill in the 1820s, not a cotton mill. But the central point of his answer is not a matter of opinion. In a workshop a tired worker slows down. In a mill the machine sets the pace, and it does not tire.

Parliament passed a Factory Act in 1833. It applied to most textile mills driven by water or steam. It barred children under nine from working in them, limited those under thirteen to nine hours a day and 48 a week with two hours of schooling, and limited those from thirteen to eighteen to twelve hours a day and 69 a week, with no night work. It appointed four inspectors with the right to enter mills and take evidence. Adult men were not covered.

### The handloom weaver

For a time the new spinning machines were good for weavers. Cheap, plentiful yarn had to be woven by someone, and until the power loom worked, that meant by hand. The number of handloom weavers in the British cotton trade rose from about 37,000 in 1780 to about 240,000 around 1820. Most worked at home on their own looms, at hours they set for themselves, and for some years they were well paid. Around 1806 a weaver earned about 20 shillings a week, roughly twice what a factory worker earned.

The power loom then became a practical machine. By 1820 a weaver's weekly earnings had fallen to about 8 shillings and by 1830 to about 6, while a factory worker's held steady at about 10. Prices were falling in those years, which softens the drop without changing its direction. Power looms wove about a tenth of Britain's cotton cloth in 1820, more than half in 1832 and 95 percent in 1845. About 40,000 to 50,000 handloom weavers remained in 1850, and about 10,000 in 1860. The machine did not make the weaver's work easier or better paid. It did the work in his place. Chapter 49 takes up those who responded by breaking machines.

### The cotton picker

Solomon Northup was a free Black man from New York who was kidnapped in 1841 and held as a slave in Louisiana for twelve years. After his release he published an account, *Twelve Years a Slave*, in 1853. He describes how a picker's daily quota was set and enforced:

> When a new hand, one unaccustomed to the business, is sent for the first time into the field, he is whipped up smartly, and made for that day to pick as fast as he can possibly. At night it is weighed, so that his capability in cotton picking is known. He must bring in the same weight each night following. If it falls short, it is considered evidence that he has been laggard, and a greater or less number of lashes is the penalty.
>
> An ordinary day's work is two hundred pounds. A slave who is accustomed to picking, is punished, if he or she brings in a less quantity than that.

Two hundred pounds is about 90 kilograms. Each evening the baskets were carried to the building that housed the gin, and weighed:

> No matter how fatigued and weary he may be—no matter how much he longs for sleep and rest—a slave never approaches the gin-house with his basket of cotton but with fear. If it falls short in weight—if he has not performed the full task appointed him, he knows that he must suffer. And if he has exceeded it by ten or twenty pounds, in all probability his master will measure the next day's task accordingly.

Northup wrote from memory after his release, for readers in the North whom he hoped to turn against slavery. His account of the daily weighing is consistent with the plantation account books that survive, which record each picker's weight each day.

Those account books show that the amount of cotton a person picked in a day rose about fourfold between 1801 and 1862. Why is disputed, and the dispute has been heated. The economic historians Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode, working from the records of 142 plantations, credit mainly new varieties of cotton with larger bolls that were easier to pick. They note that picking rates rose very little where the older varieties were still grown. The historian Edward Baptist argues that the rise came from a system of steadily increased quotas enforced by the whip, which forced pickers to find faster ways of working. The two explanations do not exclude each other. Northup's last sentence above describes exactly how a gain in speed, whatever its source, was turned into a higher demand.

### Did ordinary people gain?

For the first two generations, the answer is that it is hard to show that they did. The economic historian Robert Allen calculates that output per worker in Britain rose by 46 percent between 1780 and 1840, while the real wage, meaning the wage measured by what it could buy, rose by 12 percent. Another estimate gives 38 and 18 percent. On either set of numbers, most of what the new machines added went to the owners of mills, mines and land. Allen calls the period "Engels' pause", after Friedrich Engels, who described the condition of Manchester's workers in a book of 1845. After about 1840 the pattern changed: between 1840 and 1900, by Allen's figures, output per worker rose by 90 percent and real wages by 123 percent.

Historians have long divided into pessimists, who hold that the working population gained little or nothing before mid-century, and optimists, who hold that wages rose earlier and faster than the pessimists allow. The evidence on health mostly favours the pessimists. A baby born in the central districts of Manchester or Liverpool around 1840 could expect to live about 25 to 28 years, a figure pulled down by the very high death rate of infants in crowded streets with fouled water. Whether city life expectancy actually fell in those decades or was simply low is itself disputed. Average adult heights, which reflect how well children were fed and how often they were ill, fell for those born from about the 1820s and 1830s and did not recover until after mid-century. Working time also seems to have grown: one study estimates that the hours worked in a year rose by about a fifth between the 1750s and the 1830s, mainly because customary days off disappeared.

## Why Britain?

Historians mostly agree on what happened. They disagree on why it happened in Britain first and not in France, the Netherlands, India or China. Five kinds of explanation dominate the argument.

### Dear labour and cheap coal

Robert Allen argues that the machines were invented in Britain because Britain was the place where it paid to invent them. His comparisons of wages and prices across cities show that eighteenth-century British workers were among the best paid in the world relative to the cost of living, and that energy, thanks to coal, was cheaper there than anywhere. A machine that uses more capital and more fuel in order to use less labour is worth developing only where labour is dear and fuel cheap. Allen calculates that a spinning jenny in about 1780 would have earned its owner a return of 38 percent a year in England, 2.5 percent in France and a loss in India. The Newcomen engine is the clearest case on the energy side: it made sense only beside a coal mine.

The weak point concerns whose wages were high. Jane Humphries and Benjamin Schneider collected evidence on what hand spinners, who were women and children, actually earned, and found that spinning was poorly paid work whose wages were not rising before the machines came. If the labour being replaced was cheap, the motive for replacing it has to be found elsewhere. Allen has replied that their data do not record how many hours the spinners worked and lean on charity schemes whose spinners were unusually slow. They have answered that a larger body of data confirms their findings, and the exchange is unresolved. Other critics note that the jenny also cost less to buy in England, which accounts for part of Allen's gap, and that only about a fifth of the patents of the period aimed at saving labour.

### A culture of useful knowledge

Joel Mokyr locates Britain's advantage in what people knew and how they shared it. He describes an "Industrial Enlightenment": a conviction, spread through printed books, public lectures and local societies, that nature could be investigated by experiment and the results put to practical use. In his account this was joined to institutions that rewarded improvement and, above all, to Britain's deep pool of skilled craftsmen, who could build what an inventor imagined and then refine it. That explains something the first account does not, which is the continuous stream of small improvements made by people who gained little from them.

The weak point is measurement. Mokyr himself grants that the links are hard to quantify. Allen examined 79 leading inventors and found connections to Enlightenment science to be common in steam and rare in textiles and metals, the industries that grew most. That fits this chapter's evidence, since Watt worked at a university and Hargreaves and Crompton did not. Gregory Clark objects that the Enlightenment ran right across Europe, including regions that grew poorer in these years, and that the Netherlands was the centre of the new science a century earlier without industrializing. Defenders answer that the crucial contributions were the anonymous ones of craftsmen, which no count of famous inventors can capture.

### Coal in the right place, and land overseas

Kenneth Pomeranz makes the comparison with China. He argues that the most advanced region of China, the Yangzi delta, and the most advanced regions of Europe were at broadly similar levels of living and development until about 1800, and that both were pressing against the limits of their land. In his account Britain escaped by two pieces of good fortune. Its coal lay close to its industry and to water transport, whereas China's main deposits were in the north and northwest, far from the delta. And Britain had the Americas. Pomeranz estimates how much British land would have been needed to produce substitutes for the cotton, sugar and timber imported from the New World around 1830, and the figures usually cited from his work are 10 to 12 million hectares (25 to 30 million acres), set against about 7 million hectares of arable land in England. The term used for them is "ghost acres". Wrigley's work on the organic economy supports the coal half of this argument, though not the overseas half, which is Pomeranz's own.

The weak points are three. Studies of wages by Allen and colleagues, and by Broadberry and Gupta, find that workers in northwestern Europe were already far better paid than those in China or India in the eighteenth century and perhaps earlier, which puts the divergence well before 1800. The ghost-acre figures assume that British farming could not have improved and that cotton could only have been replaced by wool; a study of the land actually embodied in Britain's trade finds much smaller numbers, and finds that coal mattered more than overseas land. And Gregory Clark and David Jacks argue that British coal output grew because demand for it grew, not because coal's presence called industry into being.

### Institutions

Douglass North and Barry Weingast argued in 1989 that the revolution of 1688, in which Parliament replaced one king with another and set limits on the crown, made the government's promises believable. A crown that could not seize property or default on its debts at will could borrow cheaply, and property in general became more secure. Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson built a wider theory on this: countries prosper when their institutions are "inclusive", protecting many people's property and letting new industries displace old ones, and Britain's settlement of 1688 was of that kind. This connects to the public borrowing of chapter 45.

The weak points are timing and evidence. Several historians find that interest rates did not drop sharply after 1688, and that incomes were already rising before it. A century separates the settlement from the cotton mills, a gap that the theory's authors acknowledge. And the Dutch Republic, richer than Britain and no less commercial, did not industrialize first, which is awkward for any account that rests on good institutions or active trade alone.

### Empire, slavery and the state

In 1944 the Trinidadian historian Eric Williams argued that profits from the slave trade and the sugar plantations of the West Indies supplied capital for British industry. For decades the standard reply was arithmetic. Stanley Engerman and David Eltis calculated that Caribbean sugar added less than 2.5 percent to British national income and that the slave trade's profits were perhaps half of one percent of it, and concluded that slavery was not indispensable to industrialization. The same estimates look different measured against investment instead of income: at their upper limit, slave-trade profits were about 8 percent of all British investment and 39 percent of investment in commerce and industry. Eltis and Engerman regard those as extreme upper bounds, since they assume every pound of profit was invested.

More recent work has widened the claim beyond profits. Joseph Inikori argues that Atlantic trade as a whole, with Africa and the Americas as markets as well as suppliers, drove the growth of British manufacturing. Maxine Berg and Pat Hudson, in a book of 2023, point to the navy that protected the trade, the financial methods developed to serve it, and the compensation paid to slave owners at abolition in the 1830s, which they trace into railways and other ventures. They say explicitly that they do not claim slavery caused the industrial revolution. Parthasarathi adds the state's commercial policy, arguing that the Calico Acts and later duties protected British cotton from Indian cloth while it learned to compete. This family of explanations accounts for something the others leave unexplained: the leading industry of the new economy processed a fibre that would not grow in Britain, bought from slave plantations, and sold much of its output abroad.

The weak points are that the sums, on most estimates, were modest beside the British economy as a whole; that the costs of the navy and the wars have to be set against the gains; and that the Calico Acts had many exemptions and, by some accounts, weak enforcement. Spain had more colonies than Britain and did not industrialize first.

### Weighing them

These explanations are not rivals in the sense that only one can be true. Most historians now combine several and disagree about the weights. Three of them are about material conditions: wages and fuel prices, the location of coal, the land and labour of the colonies. Two are about ideas and rules: a culture of practical inquiry, and a constitution that tied the government's hands. The evidence of this chapter does not let either group stand without the other. The atmospheric engine needed the science of the vacuum, and it also needed a coalfield where its waste did not matter. Watt's condenser came out of a university workshop, and it sold because Cornish mine owners were paying heavily for Welsh coal.

The comparison with Song China, left open in chapter 26, tests each explanation. Song China had coal and iron in quantity; one historian, Robert Hartwell, argued that its iron output in the eleventh century exceeded England's of six hundred years later, though the estimates vary by a factor of ten. It had printing, paper money and busy markets. It did not have an industrial revolution. The explanations offered are, first, that the iron and coal districts lay in the north, which the dynasty lost to invaders in 1127, and second, Mark Elvin's argument that Chinese methods were so efficient and labour so cheap that there was little to gain from machines, an argument made mainly about later centuries. Both are versions of explanations given for Britain: coal in the wrong place, and labour too cheap to replace. Neither side of the comparison proves the case, but it does weaken any account in which markets, technical skill or coal alone was enough.

## Consequences

Manchester had about 23,000 people in 1773, when a local physician counted them. The census of 1801 found about 75,000, and that of 1851 about 300,000, or more if the neighbouring town of Salford and the suburbs are included. It had been a market town and had become something for which there was no earlier model, a large city whose business was manufacturing with powered machines.

British coal output, roughly 3 million tons in 1700, was 10 to 15 million tons around 1800 and 40 to 50 million by 1850. Cotton goods became the country's largest export, between 40 and 50 percent of the value of everything Britain sold abroad from the first decade of the century through the 1840s.

The steam engine also went onto wheels. On 27 September 1825 the Stockton and Darlington Railway opened in northeastern England to carry coal from inland mines to the river Tees. It used steam locomotives for its coal trains, with horses still pulling the passenger coaches. On 15 September 1830 the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened between the cotton port and the cotton city, the first to rely on steam locomotives alone. Railways began as a way of moving coal and cotton. Chapter 50 follows what they did to the idea of a nation.

The longest consequence was not visible at the time. Britain had built an economy that grew by burning carbon that plants had taken out of the air over millions of years. For the first time, a society's supply of energy did not compete with its supply of food for the same land, and growth could continue for generations without meeting the old ceiling. The carbon went back into the air as carbon dioxide. Air trapped in Antarctic ice shows that the gas stood at about 275 to 284 parts per million for centuries beforehand and began to climb in the 1800s. Chapter 82 takes up what followed.

Much also stayed as it was. Waterwheels went on turning beside steam engines for decades. In 1851 farming was still the largest single occupation in Britain, and far more people worked in trades untouched by the new machines, as servants, labourers, builders, tailors and shoemakers, than worked in factories.

## The road not taken: water

The alternative to coal was the power source already in use. Water drove the first cotton mills and it still supplied most of Britain's industrial power in 1800. Its weaknesses were that it stopped in droughts and frosts and that the best sites on any river were soon occupied. Engineers had an answer to both: build reservoirs in the hills to store winter rain, and release the water through channels at a steady rate past a line of mills.

One such scheme was built. Robert Thom, a Scottish engineer who already ran a cotton mill on stored water on the island of Bute, designed a reservoir and a nine-kilometre channel above the town of Greenock, on the river Clyde. The Shaws Water Company was formed in 1825 and the works opened in April 1827. They supplied the town with drinking water and drove the wheels of a paper mill, woollen and cotton mills, a rope works, sugar refineries and an iron foundry. Watt himself had earlier judged such a scheme impractical. The channel stayed in service until 1971.

Larger schemes were drawn up for the rivers of Lancashire and were not built. Malm, who has studied them, argues that the reason was organization, not engineering or cost. A shared reservoir required mill owners to pay jointly for the works, to accept a common plan, and to take water by agreed rules, and competing manufacturers would not bind themselves to their rivals. A steam engine required agreement with no one. This is one historian's reading, and the 2024 study mentioned earlier offers a simpler one for Manchester, where the rivers were already fully used.

Staying with water would have required that kind of cooperation among owners, and it would have meant moving workers to the valleys instead of mills to the towns. What follows from that is speculation. Water might well have carried the cotton industry further than it did. It could not have driven a locomotive or a steamship, and the rain that falls on a country each year is limited in a way that the coal beneath it, for a few centuries, was not.

## What the chapter found

The working answer had two parts. The first was that early engines were wasteful but ran where coal was almost free. That held. A Newcomen engine turned less than a hundredth of its fuel into work, and it succeeded at coal mines, burning coal that could not be sold. The answer needs one revision. It explains the engine at the mine and does not, alone, explain the engine in the factory. Steam reached the mills only after Watt cut its fuel use by two-thirds or more, after it was made to turn a shaft, and after canals had made coal cheap in towns. Even then it took half a century to overtake water, and the reasons owners chose it included control over where and when work was done as well as cost.

The second part was that the reasons industrialization began in Britain are among the most argued questions in economic history. That held too. The chapter has set out five explanations, and the evidence does not let any one of them stand alone.

The scale of dependence had changed. A length of printed cotton sold in a British shop in 1850 was made from fibre picked under the whip in Mississippi, shipped on credit through Liverpool, spun and woven in Manchester by machines whose pace was set by an engine, with coal that a miner had cut and a canal boat or railway had carried. It was cheap enough to undersell a weaver in Bengal in his own former export markets. None of these people had met, and most did not know the others existed.

The machines made far more than hands had made. For two generations, most of the gain went to the people who owned them. That left a question the engine could not answer. Who should receive what the machines produced, and who should decide the price of a day's work? Chapter 49 turns to it.
