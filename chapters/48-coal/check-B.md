## 1. Factual flags

1. **“Sooner or later, prices of food and fuel rose, and the growth stalled. Rich civilisations, from Rome onwards, had … pressed up against this invisible roof.”**  
   Land and renewable-energy constraints limited preindustrial production, but this presents a historical interpretation as a universal explanation of economic stagnation. Rome’s economic trajectory, in particular, cannot confidently be attributed to reaching that ceiling; warfare, institutions, disease and political disruption also mattered.  
   **Confidence: medium. Severity: major.**

2. **“The vacuum still formed, and the air still drove the piston.”**  
   Watt’s developed separate-condenser engine had a closed cylinder with steam admitted above the piston, while condensation created low pressure below it. The pressure difference drove the piston; outside air no longer acted directly on its upper face as in Newcomen’s engine. The separate-condenser explanation is otherwise sound, but this sentence gives the wrong picture of Watt’s working engine.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

3. **“His engines kept leaking.… The steam whistled out through the gaps.”**  
   Poor cylinder accuracy did cause sealing problems, but Watt’s early engines operated with steam near atmospheric pressure and a vacuum on the condensing side. Air leaking inward, or steam leaking past the piston into the low-pressure space, undermined that vacuum; steam whistling outward suggests a pressurised engine of a different kind.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

4. **“In the 1780s there was not much in Britain that needed turning by a power source the size of an engine.”**  
   Britain already had substantial demand for rotary power in grain milling, ironworking, textile production and other industries, served by water, wind and animals. Cotton greatly expanded the market, but it did not create an otherwise largely absent need for powerful rotary machinery.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

5. **“A steam engine had none of these problems. It could be set down wherever coal could be brought.”**  
   The condensing engines discussed here also needed a dependable water supply, particularly for cooling the condenser. Steam greatly loosened dependence on suitable river sites, but access to coal alone did not make every location practicable, and drought could still cause difficulties.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

6. **“A waterwheel cannot be put on wheels. It cannot pull a train or drive a ship across an ocean, and it cannot smelt iron. Sooner or later the new industrial world would have needed what was in the coal.”**  
   This conflates steam power with coal fuel and mechanical power with furnace heat. Steam locomotives and vessels could—and did—burn wood, while charcoal could smelt iron. Coal’s abundance and economics were crucial to Britain’s actual industrial development, but these examples do not establish its technological inevitability.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

7. **“A hand-cranked machine that pulled the sticky seeds out of the kind of cotton that grew well across the inland South.”**  
   Whitney’s gin pulled cotton fibres through a narrow grating that the seeds could not pass through, leaving the seeds behind. It separated fibres from seeds rather than pulling seeds out through the mechanism.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

8. **“Britain’s empire brought … a flood of raw cotton.”**  
   This bundles imperial possession together with participation in the wider Atlantic slave economy. During the great expansion described here, Britain’s principal cotton supplier was the independent United States, as the chapter itself explains. British colonial cotton mattered, but the dominant American supply was not a product of British rule over its source.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

9. **“Without the tinkerers, the coal would have stayed in the ground, as it did in China.”**  
   China mined and burned coal extensively long before Britain’s Industrial Revolution, including for industrial purposes. The relevant difference is the development and spread of coal-powered machinery, not whether Chinese coal was extracted; the sentence also wrongly implies that China lacked inventive craftspeople.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

10. **“Some of it was carbon dioxide, which had been locked away underground since long before there were people.”**  
    The coal contained ancient carbon, not stored carbon dioxide subsequently released unchanged. Combustion combined that carbon with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

## 2. Clarity flags

- **“The spinning mule … combined ideas from the two earlier machines.”** Only Arkwright’s machine has been explained. The reader needs a brief introduction to Hargreaves’s spinning jenny before this reference makes sense.

- **“A German experimenter pumped the air out of two metal half-spheres, put them together…”** The sequence is backwards as written: the hemispheres were fitted together first, then air was pumped from the enclosed space. Otherwise there is no sealed chamber to evacuate.

- **“The rule for how much work a given amount of heat can possibly do…”** This suggests a fixed conversion ratio. The thermodynamic limit depends on the temperatures between which an engine operates; a short reference to those temperatures would make the explanation intelligible and accurate.

- **“The fourth is in India: a spinner … and a weaver…”** The promised fourth person becomes two people. Choose one viewpoint or introduce the structure as four positions along the trade rather than four individual lives.

- **“A slave never approaches the gin-house…”** “Cotton gin” has been introduced, but “gin-house” has not. Explain that this was the building where cotton was brought for processing and, in this account, weighing.

## 3. Overall

The chapter’s broad chronology and its account of coal, pumping and cotton manufacturing are largely accurate. Its most consequential errors concern existing demand for rotary power, the inevitability of coal, imperial cotton supply and Chinese coal use. The treatment of workers and slavery is generally fair, and the discussion of Crabtree’s testimony usefully explains how the evidence was selected. Readability is strong, but several confident transitions turn interpretations into certainties, and the missing spinning jenny leaves a conspicuous explanatory gap.
