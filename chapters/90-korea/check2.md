## Factual flags

1. **Major — newly introduced false choice.**

   > “Instead of fighting his way out of the pocket, he would land an army at Inchon, the port of Seoul, a hundred and fifty miles behind the northern lines.”

   The Inchon landing supplemented a coordinated breakout from Pusan; it did not replace one. The landing occurred on 15 September, and Eighth Army began its breakout on 16 September. The correct relationship is **landing behind the enemy while the southern army attacked out of the perimeter**. [U.S. Army official history, *Getting the Message Through*](https://history.army.mil/portals/143/Images/Publications/catalog/30-17.pdf)

2. **Minor — newly introduced false absolute.**

   > “Nor was the war called a war at home.”

   The absence of a congressional declaration and Truman’s adoption of “police action” do not establish this. Truman himself publicly described the fighting as a “limited war in Korea” in April 1951. The correct fact is already in the next two sentences: he did not seek a declaration and accepted the police-action description. [Truman’s address of 11 April 1951](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/april-11-1951-report-american-people-korea)

3. **Major — existing chronology error, sharpened by the transition.**

   > “MacArthur assured the president that the Chinese would not come, and that if they did there would be the greatest slaughter.”
   >
   > “In fact, they had already come.”

   This places the large Chinese deployment before MacArthur’s assurance. His remarks were made at Wake Island on **15 October 1950**; the main Chinese intervention force began crossing on **19 October**. The correct sequence is assurance, crossing, then combat. This is separate from the earlier check’s now-fixed omission of the October fighting. [Wake Island meeting record](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v07/d680), [U.S. Marine Corps history](https://www.marines.mil/Portals/1/Publications/U.S.%20Marines%20in%20the%20Korean%20War%20%20PCN%2010600000100_8.pdf)

4. **Minor — existing casualty-category error.**

   > “Thirty-six thousand Americans were killed.”

   Approximately 36,574 American service members **died in theatre**; that total includes 2,835 nonbattle deaths. Battle deaths numbered 33,739. “Died” preserves the intended rounded total without conflating categories. [Department of Veterans Affairs casualty statistics](https://department.va.gov/americas-wars/)

5. **Minor — earlier flag remains unfixed.**

   > “No post crossed it, and no telephone calls.”

   Replacing “letters” with “post” does not resolve the earlier finding. On **15 March 2001**, letters were exchanged between 600 separated families. Routine private communication was blocked, but exceptional exchanges occurred. The correct claim concerns the absence of **ordinary, freely available communication**, not all correspondence. [Congressional Research Service chronology](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20020109_RL30811_c21ab7db71693a8ec1b7a84c32a737d1e5d8e171.html)

## Clarity flags

- **Prohibited signposting:**  
  > “Their reasons were not hard to find.”  
  > “That marked something new.”  
  > “The difference between an armistice and a peace matters here.”

  These announce an explanation or distinction immediately supplied by the next sentence. They fit the style sheet’s prohibition on announcing that a point is coming.

- **Repeated explanation:**  
  > “In other words, the temporary line now had a government on either side of it, and each of those governments regarded the other as illegitimate.”

  This repeats the preceding account of two governments each claiming the whole peninsula. It adds little orientation.

- **Padding around an already concrete consequence:**  
  > “Those years left a mark that outlasted the war.”

  The next sentence identifies that lasting mark. This sentence merely announces it.

- **An unclear metaphor:**  
  > “The weather fought on both sides.”

  This can suggest that the weather helped both armies. The following passage means that the cold harmed both.

- **Elapsed time and geography become unnecessarily exact:**  
  > “A year of fighting up and down the length of the country had brought both sides back to the line two colonels had picked off a magazine map.”

  The preceding sentence places this in spring 1951, less than a year after June 1950, and correctly says “close to” the parallel. This recap turns both approximations into apparent exactness.

- **Unexplained historical reference:**  
  > “He wanted to bomb the bases and factories of China, blockade its coast, and bring Chiang Kai-shek's army over from Taiwan.”

  Chiang appears without identification. A brief identification as the defeated Chinese Nationalist leader would let a reader understand whose army this was.

- **Unspecified measurement:**  
  > “Around a frozen reservoir in the northern mountains, thirty thousand American and allied troops were surrounded by four times their number, at thirty-five degrees below zero.”

  Specify Celsius or Fahrenheit. This sentence also stacks three quantities despite the sheet’s preference for one number per point.

- **Absolutes broader than the intended point:**  
  > “The line had been drawn on paper, and on the ground it fitted nothing.”  
  > “The Americans, however, had not come to recognise anyone.”  
  > “The flag was shared, but the burden was not: nine tenths of the foreign soldiers were American.”

  “Nothing” overlooks the immediately preceding strategic purpose of retaining Seoul; “anyone” obscures which Korean authorities were excluded; and a nine-tenths American contribution describes an **unequally shared** burden.

- **A heading promises unexplained precision:**  
  > “Forty-eight telegrams”

  The body gives “dozens,” never explains forty-eight, and leaves the heading’s exact count unsupported within the narrative.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions, or cross-references.

## Overall

The revision generally improves continuity, but it is not wholly fact-neutral. The clearest new errors are the Inchon/Pusan false choice and the claim about American use of “war.” Correct those, the surviving chronology and casualty errors, and the unresolved correspondence absolute; trim the signposting.
