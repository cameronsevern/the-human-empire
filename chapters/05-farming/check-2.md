## Factual flags

1. **Minor — “She also has something that a hunter's wife does not, which is soft porridge of boiled grain that a baby can eat.”**

   This contradicts the chapter’s own distinction: these villagers are still hunters and gatherers who process wild grain. A hunter’s wife could therefore be precisely the woman making that porridge. The relevant contrast is between mobile and settled households, not hunters and farmers. **Source:** the preceding passages in [the chapter](chapters/05-farming/chapter.md), especially “They were still hunters and gatherers.”

2. **Major — “About eleven thousand seven hundred years ago, the cold ended for good, and it ended fast.” / “It held steady.”**

   The end of the Younger Dryas did not end subsequent abrupt cooling or major rainfall changes. Around 8,200 years ago, another climatic disruption affected early farmers—including those at Çatalhöyük, the chapter’s closing example. The correct distinction is greater climatic stability relative to the preceding ice age, not permanent stability or dependable annual rainfall. [Research on the 8,200-year event at Çatalhöyük](https://www.pnas.org/content/pnas/115/35/8705.full.pdf).

3. **Major — “It is what humans do when they are settled, crowded and blessed with steady weather.”**

   Applied to all eight origins, this gives them a sequence contradicted by archaeological evidence. Cultivation also developed among mobile groups; in parts of Africa, cultivation followed increasing aridity. Settlement before cultivation is one route, not the common prerequisite asserted here. This is a problem with the geographical extension of the explanation, rather than with choosing that explanation for the Middle East. [Fuller and colleagues’ comparative archaeological study](https://www.pnas.org/content/pnas/111/17/6147.full.pdf).

4. **Minor — “South America had one, the llama, together with its small cousin the alpaca.”**

   Adding the alpaca fixes its omission, but leaves the count inconsistent. Llamas and alpacas are two domesticated species, derived respectively from guanacos and vicuñas. If the fourteen-animal tally groups them together, the text needs to identify that grouping rather than call them one animal. [Camelid genomic study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7331169/).

5. **Minor — “That is why nobody rides zebras, which bite and do not let go.”**

   Individual zebras have been trained and ridden. A historical photograph records Dr Rosendo Ribeiro riding one in Nairobi. The defensible fact is that zebras did not become a routinely domesticated riding animal; inability to ride an individual is a different claim. [Historical photograph reproduced in *Conservation of Cultural and Natural Heritage in Kenya*, figure 8.1](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1519656/1/Conservation-of-Cultural-and-Natural-Heritage-in-Kenya.pdf).

6. **Major — “All of them died out soon after the first people arrived.” / “The descendants of those hunters, ten thousand years on, had fields of maize and potatoes and almost nothing to pull a plough, carry a pack or ride into battle.”**

   The paragraph combines incompatible American and Australian chronologies. Human presence in North America is documented around 23,000–21,000 years ago, allowing thousands of years of coexistence before the major late-Pleistocene extinctions. Australia was occupied tens of thousands of years earlier still; its hunters’ descendants did not acquire maize and potato fields ten thousand years later. Separate the continents and anchor the agricultural comparison to an actual period. [White Sands dating study](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70249447), [research on prolonged human–megafauna coexistence](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2795395/), [Australian occupation study](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22968).

7. **Major — “Those were places where no crop will grow, and so they were the places that farmers had left alone.”**

   This is false for the Kalahari. Ethnographic fieldwork documents cultivation of maize, sorghum, cowpeas and melons there, including cultivation by San communities. Difficult and unreliable farming conditions are not an inability to grow any crop, and cannot support the stated explanation that farmers simply left these regions alone. [Osaki’s field study of central Kalahari communities](https://jambo.africa.kyoto-u.ac.jp/kiroku/asm_suppl/abstracts/pdf/Sup.12/S.12-4%20Osaki.pdf).

## Clarity flags

1. **“Putting seed in the ground was only the beginning, because the first farmers did not have crops.”**  
   A deliberately cultivated wild plant can already be a crop. The intended distinction is between **cultivation** and **domesticated plants**. Without explaining it, the sentence makes “farmers without crops” unnecessarily puzzling.

2. **“When the ear is ripe, therefore, it shatters.” / “She cuts the stalks and carries the sheaves home.”**  
   “Ear,” “sheaves” and the earlier “threshing” are unexplained agricultural vocabulary. The style sheet requires unfamiliar terms to be explained on first use; these matter particularly because the reader must understand the selection mechanism.

3. **“The Middle East, and seven other places, from about 23,000 years ago to about 6,000 years ago.”**  
   The subtitle ends before the independent-origins survey’s “latest about four thousand.” The Roman population comparison is recognisably a later comparison, but the four-thousand-year date belongs to the advertised main story.

4. **“Then comes the step that matters.” / “Yet in one respect, farming beat hunting so thoroughly that nothing else counted.” / “That respect was numbers.”**  
   These announce an important point before stating it, contrary to the style sheet’s explicit prohibition on signpost sentences.

5. **“Besides, nobody remembered the other life.”**  
   Later, hunters live beside incoming farmers, who can see their way of life. Clarify whether this means loss of ancestral memory, loss of practical skills, or inability to return economically. Those are different claims.

6. **“When their descendants finally noticed what had happened, it was far too late to go back.” / “For most, however, the door had shut behind them.” / “So farming stayed.”**  
   The opening, the end of the plant-domestication section, and “No way back” repeatedly deliver the same population-trap conclusion. Some repetition provides structure, but the later section spends substantial space restating a conclusion already explained.

## Overall

The narrative is clear and engaging, and several earlier problems have been repaired. Seven factual issues remain worth correcting, chiefly the universal sequence into farming, the combined continental chronology, and the claim about crop-free refuges. The chapter’s confident interpretation does not itself need softening. No files were edited.
