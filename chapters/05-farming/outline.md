# Chapter 5 — Why did people start farming?

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Nobody decided to become a farmer. People knew how plants grow for at least ten thousand years before anyone farmed, and did not bother, because farming is harder work than gathering. It began when three things came together: a climate steady enough to make it possible, people who had already settled beside rich stands of wild grain, and more mouths than the wild could feed. Each step was small and sensible. The result was a worse life for the people living it, shorter, sicker and more laborious, and a far larger number of people living it. Farming won because farmers outbred hunters, and once a valley was full there was no way back. It also created, for the first time, a store of wealth that sat in one place and could be counted.

## The story

1. **They did not mean to.** Answer first. Then a camp on the Sea of Galilee, 23,000 years ago, where people were already gathering and grinding wild wheat and barley, twelve thousand years before anyone farmed. Knowledge was never the missing piece.

2. **Why bother?** Foragers understood plants better than any modern gardener. They did not farm because it was more work for a worse diet. The Kalahari answer: why plant, when there are so many nuts in the world?

3. **Staying put.** The first step was settling, not sowing. In the hills of the Levant, about 14,500 years ago, wild grain grew so thick that people built stone houses beside it. Bread before farming. The house mouse as proof that they stayed. Settled mothers have babies twice as often, so the villages grew.

4. **A steady sky.** The ice age climate swung too wildly for farming. A last cold snap squeezed the villages. Then, 11,700 years ago, the weather turned warm and steady and has stayed so. Within a few thousand years, farming began in at least seven places among people who had never met. It was a situation, not a stroke of genius.

5. **The plant that cannot let go.** How wheat changed without anyone intending it: the ear that does not shatter. It took two or three thousand years. Maize as the extreme case. The crop comes to depend on the farmer, and the farmer on the crop.

6. **Fourteen animals.** Goats, sheep, pigs and cattle. Why so few animals could be tamed, and why nearly all of them lived in Europe and Asia. The Americas had hunted their horses to extinction ten thousand years earlier.

7. **The bill.** What farming did to the people who did it: the worn toes and knees of the women at the grinding stones, shorter bodies, rotten teeth, hunger when the one crop failed. And yet many more children.

8. **No way back.** Why nobody gave it up, and how it spread: farmers moved into Europe at about a kilometre a year and swamped the hunters by numbers, as our kind had once swamped the Neanderthals.

9. **The town without streets.** Çatalhöyük, nine thousand years ago: thousands of people in mud houses packed wall to wall, entered through the roof, the dead under the floor, and no palace or temple. A new way of living, with no ruler yet. End on the storeroom: a year of work in one place, where it can be counted.
