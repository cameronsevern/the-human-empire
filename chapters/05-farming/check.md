## Factual flags

Related repetitions are grouped. “Major” means the problem affects the chapter’s explanation; “minor” means it affects a narrower claim or example.

1. **“Nobody ever stood at the edge of a forest and chose between the life of a hunter and the life of a farmer.”**  
   Gradual domestication does not establish that cultivation was unintentional, or that nobody consciously adopted farming. The opening confuses unintended evolutionary consequences with deliberate decisions about planting and subsistence, whose importance remains debated. [Domestication research review](https://www.pnas.org/content/pnas/112/11/3191.full.pdf). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

2. **“So people were harvesting grain and grinding it twelve thousand years before anyone farmed.”**  
   This is defensible for established farming economies, but too categorical about any cultivation. Researchers have interpreted the Ohalo II evidence itself as small-scale experimental cultivation; that interpretation is not settled, but it prevents this clean boundary from being stated without defining farming. [Ohalo II study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4511808/). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

3. **“They knew what wheat was and when it ripened, and they certainly knew that a seed dropped on wet ground comes up as a plant.” / “Knowledge was never the missing piece.”**  
   Grain processing demonstrates knowledge of harvesting and preparation, not directly knowledge of sowing. Familiarity with germination is plausible, but the broader claim that practical agricultural knowledge was never a constraint exceeds the evidence. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

4. **“People who live by gathering know plants better than any modern gardener does.”**  
   This is an untestable universal comparison between highly varied groups and kinds of expertise. Detailed local ecological knowledge does not establish superiority over every gardener in every relevant respect. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

5. **“They did not go further because farming is a bad deal.” / “The gatherer, meanwhile, has eaten forty different things and spent the afternoon in the shade.”**  
   Labour costs and returns vary with environment, technique and what counts as work; easier foraging is not an established universal explanation for delayed farming. A comparative study found no average difference in total work time between subsistence modes, although particular studies do find increased agricultural workloads; the forty-food day is unsupported illustrative precision. This also affects the later summary that farmers necessarily “worked harder than hunters.” [Comparative energetics study](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abf0130). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

6. **“People who wanted to live on it had to stop moving and live beside it.”**  
   Storing a seasonal harvest does not logically require permanent residence beside it: people can cache food and return, or combine seasonal settlements with mobility. The sentence turns one plausible route towards settlement into a necessity. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

7. **“As a result, her children came closer together, and she bore about two more of them in her lifetime.” / “Wherever people settled down, the share of children among the dead rises sharply, which is what happens when far more children are being born.”**  
   The preceding chain—one child carried, prolonged nursing, no carrying in villages, exclusive access to porridge—universalises particular observations and includes an unsupported distinction between farmers’ and foragers’ infant foods. The approximately two additional births estimate concerns the Neolithic demographic transition, not every instance of settlement, and cemetery age profiles require demographic and preservation assumptions. Recent comparative research also finds that the farming–fertility relationship depends strongly on context. [Fertility study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2318181121). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

8. **“Within a couple of thousand years, the good country was full of settled people who depended on wild grain, and there were far more of them than the land had ever held before.”**  
   Neither regional saturation nor general dependence on grain has been established at this precision. This presents population pressure—the mechanism needed for the chapter’s subsequent trap—as an observed fact rather than a disputed explanation. [Review of competing explanations](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ehr.13307). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

9. **“The woods shrank back, and the wild plants that the villagers lived on shrank with them.”**  
   Younger Dryas environmental effects differed across the region, so evidence of declining resources at particular sites cannot support this general statement about the villagers. Southern Levant evidence includes cooling without the drying assumed in older accounts of widespread resource contraction. [Gazelle-isotope study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27035951/). **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

10. **“About eleven thousand seven hundred years ago, the cold ended for good.” / “It held steady.” / “For most of the time our species has existed, nobody could count on that.”**  
    The Holocene was relatively stable, not uniformly steady: abrupt cooling and substantial rainfall changes continued, including the event around 8,200 years ago. Greenland’s temperature history also cannot establish the predictability of growing seasons everywhere humans lived throughout most of our species’ existence. [Holocene cooling study](https://publications.pik-potsdam.de/rest/items/item_12562_2/component/file_12563/content). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

11. **“It is what humans do when they are settled, crowded and blessed with steady weather.”**  
    This presents a disputed combination of causes as the established explanation for independent agricultural origins. The sequence was not universal: in parts of New Guinea and the Americas, inferred cultivation preceded clear evidence of settled village life by millennia. [Comparative domestication study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1308937110). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

12. **“The people in them had never met, did not know the others existed, and were working with entirely different plants.”**  
    Independent domestication does not imply complete isolation or ignorance of neighbouring populations. Northern and Yangtze-region China, for example, belonged to interconnected regional histories; separate agricultural origins cannot establish that their people never met. [Chinese agricultural origins review](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/659965). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

13. **“In the wild this is a death sentence, since a plant that cannot drop its seed leaves no offspring.” / “It could no longer sow itself at all, and without a farmer it would die out in a few seasons.”**  
    Failure to shatter reduces effective seed dispersal; it does not make seeds sterile or eliminate every route by which an ear reaches the ground and seeds germinate. The categorical reproductive impossibility and fixed extinction timetable overstate the genuine disadvantage of non-shattering wheat outside cultivation. [Wheat biology review](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article/60/6/1537/517393). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

14. **“Every plant with a tough stalk is therefore sure to end up in her basket.” / “The same blind sieve favoured plants with bigger grains, and plants whose seeds all sprouted at once.”**  
    Selection depends on harvesting time, technique and which seed is replanted; no plant is guaranteed collection. Larger grains and reduced dormancy also respond to different cultivation and selection pressures, so their evolution is not explained by the sickle mechanism alone. [Comparative selection study](https://www.pnas.org/content/pnas/111/17/6147.full.pdf). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

15. **“The DNA of modern cattle shows that all the humpless cattle in the world descend from a herd of about eighty wild females, tamed in the Middle East.”**  
    Eighty is a model-based estimate of the founding maternal population, not evidence of one identifiable captured herd. Ancient genomes also show substantial subsequent breeding with wild aurochs, making this a misleadingly closed account of taurine cattle ancestry. [Ancient aurochs genomes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08112-6). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

16. **“The herders killed the ones that were hard to handle and bred from the ones that were calm, and within a few hundred generations they had animals that were smaller, tamer and duller than their wild cousins.”**  
    This makes a plausible selection process sound like a documented, uniform breeding programme across species. “Duller” also asserts a general reduction in intelligence without defining or supporting it; domestication involves diverse behavioural changes, not a single scale of cleverness. [Domestication review](https://www.pnas.org/content/pnas/112/11/3191.full.pdf). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

17. **“There are about a hundred and fifty kinds of large plant-eating mammal in the world. People have turned fourteen of them into farm animals.” / “South America had one, the llama.”**  
    The familiar fourteen is a historical classification with particular size and domestication criteria, not a timeless count of farmable species. It grouped llamas and alpacas together, whereas genomic evidence supports separate domestication from guanacos and vicuñas; the chapter’s explicit claim of just one South American animal is therefore misleading. [Camelid genomic study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7331169/). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

18. **“Thirteen of them, including the sheep, the goat, the pig, the cow and the horse, lived in Europe and Asia.” / “North America, Australia and Africa south of the Sahara had none.”**  
    This geography loses Africa’s contribution, notably the African wild ass and donkey. Genomic research supports donkey domestication in Africa, with an eastern African origin, so the Europe–Asia allocation and categorical exclusion of sub-Saharan Africa are not defensible as written. [Donkey genomic study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36074859/). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

19. **“Fail one test, and the animal is useless. That is why nobody rides zebras, which bite and do not let go, and why nobody farms elephants, which can be tamed one at a time but will hardly breed in a pen.”**  
    These are tendencies affecting domestication, not mandatory tests whose failure makes an animal useless: working elephants themselves demonstrate that distinction. Individual zebras have been trained for riding or harness, and managed elephants reproduce, although maintaining captive populations presents substantial difficulties. [Library of Congress on zebras](https://www.loc.gov/everyday-mysteries/categories/zoology/item/can-zebras-be-domesticated/), [elephant population study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452069/). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

20. **“All of them died out soon after the first people arrived.” / “Their descendants, ten thousand years later, had fields of maize and potatoes and almost nothing to pull a plough, carry a pack or ride into battle.”**  
    The interval between human arrival and extinction varied greatly, and evidence for earlier settlement of the Americas undermines a uniform rapid sequence. The ten-thousand-year transition also conflates American and Australian chronologies, while treating the extinct animals’ potential domestication as knowable. [Research on American human–megafauna coexistence](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12487880/). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

21. **“She worked out what had done it.”**  
    The grain-grinding explanation for the Abu Hureyra skeletal changes is a biomechanical interpretation, not a uniquely demonstrated cause. The account also risks attributing the injuries specifically to adopting farming even though the chapter has already established that foragers ground grain. **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

22. **“In Europe, where the most skeletons have been measured, the first farmers were about four centimetres shorter than the hunters before them, and their descendants did not get that height back for thousands of years.”**  
    The approximately four-centimetre result in the cited research concerns height relative to genetic expectations, not simply a continent-wide measured difference between farmers and preceding hunters. The effect weakened after additional ancestry adjustment, so this sentence also overstates how directly the number measures farming’s nutritional cost. [Stature study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2106743119). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

23. **“Their teeth, which in hunters are mostly sound, filled with cavities from a diet of starch.”**  
    Caries commonly increased with agriculture, but the relevant distinction is diet and food preparation rather than a clean hunter–farmer divide. Starch-consuming foragers could have severe decay: at Taforalt, more than half the surviving teeth examined had cavities long before farming. [Taforalt dental study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3903197/). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

24. **“A farming village, by contrast, lived on two or three crops, and when drought or blight took them there was nothing else, and nowhere to go.”**  
    Early farmers commonly retained hunting, gathering and other food sources; dependence on cultivation did not automatically eliminate dietary alternatives or mobility. Archaeological evidence also records changes towards gathered foods and pastoralism during agricultural downturns, contradicting the absolute claim. [Study of changing subsistence and population](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5724262/). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

25. **“Measles descends from a plague of cattle, and influenza reached us from birds by way of the farmyard.”**  
    Measles and rinderpest share an evolutionary history, but their estimated divergence is thousands of years later than the first villages, making the placement chronologically misleading. Influenza A has avian origins, but a single established farmyard route into humans is not supported, and “influenza” is broader than influenza A. [Measles dating study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32554594/), [influenza evolution review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7328453/). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

26. **“A band of hunters needs a great deal of land, something like ten square kilometres for each person. The same land under wheat can feed fifty or a hundred times as many.”**  
    A rough cross-cultural forager density is not a measured carrying capacity for a particular tract of land. Combining it with an unspecified wheat yield creates an apparently controlled comparison that ignores suitability, fallow, seed reserves and other land requirements. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

27. **“Besides, nobody remembered the other life.”**  
    Farmers often lived alongside foragers, exchanged with them and continued some foraging themselves. A gradual transition does not establish universal loss of either cultural memory or practical knowledge of alternatives. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

28. **“From Turkey, in this way, farmers crossed into Greece about eight and a half thousand years ago.”**  
    “In this way” makes the preceding day’s-walk village expansion explain the Aegean crossing and subsequent European spread. Migration also involved maritime movement and discontinuous advances; the approximately one-kilometre annual rate is a broad average, not a description of that single mechanism. [European expansion study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/q-bio/0505013.pdf). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

29. **“There is no sign that it was done by killing.” / “The hunters were not defeated. They were swamped.”**  
    Ancient DNA can establish population replacement without establishing whether it resulted from fertility, migration, disease, violence or a combination. The chapter’s evidence therefore cannot support its categorical peaceful mechanism, even where replacement was substantial. [Danish population-turnover study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06862-3). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

30. **“It was done as our own kind had once displaced the Neanderthals, by numbers.”**  
    Neanderthal disappearance is not a settled example of simple numerical displacement. Demographic vulnerability is an important explanation, but it encompasses small-population effects and interacts with assimilation and other proposed causes. [Study of scholarly views](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-84410-7). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

31. **“The same thing happened when farmers spread through southern Africa and through south-east Asia, and it went on happening until quite recently.”**  
    This transfers the preceding claim of peaceful numerical replacement to regions with different migration and interaction histories. Southeast Asian ancient DNA specifically shows mixtures of incoming farmers and existing hunter-gatherers, followed by further migrations, rather than establishing the chapter’s single process. [Southeast Asian ancient-genome study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29976827/). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

32. **“That is why, when scholars wanted to learn about the hunting life, they had to go to the Kalahari, the Arctic and the Australian desert. Those were the places that no farmer wanted.”**  
    Anthropologists also studied foragers in tropical forests, coastal environments and other regions outside those examples. The explanation conflates suitability for cropping with desirability to outsiders and overlooks displacement, pastoralism and colonial land appropriation; even the general assumption that recent foragers occupy uniformly marginal habitats has been challenged. [Forager-habitat study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440306000781). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

33. **“The rooms were so smoky that the ribs of the dead are coated with soot from the inside.”**  
    Black deposits reported on ribs from three individuals were initially interpreted as evidence of inhaled soot. Later reassessment explicitly questions whether they accumulated during life or after burial, so the vivid causal statement presents a disputed interpretation as established. [Çatalhöyük air-pollution study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8863713/). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

34. **“In other words, people slept each night a hand's breadth above their grandparents.”**  
    Burial beneath platforms does not establish that the dead were the sleepers’ grandparents, or that every burial lay at that depth. Household burial groups included complex biological and social relationships, and some buildings contained more dead than their resident population would explain. [Study of Çatalhöyük house burials](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S235222672500008X). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

35. **“Hundreds of families were living side by side with no king and no priesthood, held together, as far as anyone can tell, by kinship, custom and the dead beneath the floor.”**  
    The absence of identified palaces or separate temples supports an interpretation of limited centralisation, but does not prove the absence of religious specialists or authority. “As far as anyone can tell” qualifies the proposed social bonds while leaving the stronger claim about priesthood categorical. [Excavation project’s site guide](https://www.catalhoyuk.com/sites/default/files/Catalhoyuk-Guidebook-ENGLISH.pdf?t=1). **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

36. **“A hunter's wealth was in his head, his hands and his friends, and none of it could be taken from him.”**  
    Foragers could also possess tools, stored food, boats, valued objects and rights over productive places. Documented foraging societies include inherited material wealth, hereditary elites and slavery, so this contrast removes precisely the counterexamples relevant to the argument about rule. [Hunter-gatherer wealth study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2999363/). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

37. **“It was a way of life that could not last, and the reason was standing in the corner of every house.” / “Farming had made the first thing in the human world that was worth ruling. Sooner or later, somebody was going to rule it.”**  
    Stored resources and institutionalised hierarchy existed among some foragers, while many farming societies lacked hereditary rulers. Agriculture therefore neither created the first appropriable wealth nor made rule inevitable, and no evidence presented here establishes grain storage as the reason Çatalhöyük’s social order ended. [Comparative study of forager hierarchy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8020663/). **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

## Clarity flags

1. **What counts as farming?** “They did not go further” follows descriptions of tending and replanting wild plants, while “The first farmers did not have crops” later separates cultivation from domestication without explaining the distinction. A novice cannot tell which activity crosses the chapter’s central boundary. **Severity: minor.**

2. **Conflicting time span.** The subtitle ends “about 6,000 years ago,” but the independent-origins survey reaches “about four thousand,” and the population comparison reaches the Roman Empire. The subtitle’s intended scope is unclear. **Severity: minor.**

3. **Counting the eight places.** Northern China and the Yangtze count separately, whereas all sub-Saharan Africa counts as one. The reader must reconstruct this grouping, which makes eight appear more geographically precise than the text explains. **Severity: minor.**

4. **Unexplained plant vocabulary.** “Ear,” “threshing” and “sheaves” are used before being explained. Because these terms carry the selection mechanism, unfamiliarity could prevent a reader from understanding how harvesting changes wheat. **Severity: minor.**

5. **An apparent contradiction about shattering.** A plant that cannot scatter seed supposedly “leaves no offspring,” yet such plants repeatedly occur in wild stands. The missing explanation is that new mutations can introduce a disadvantageous trait. **Severity: minor.**

6. **An ambiguous pronoun.** After “All of them died out,” “Their descendants, ten thousand years later” initially reads as referring to the extinct animals. The intended antecedent is the hunters. **Severity: minor.**

## Overall

The chapter has a clear narrative and effective concrete examples, but its central explanation is more certain and universal than the evidence supports. The largest problems are the single causal route into farming, categorical contrasts between farmers and foragers, peaceful replacement presented as established, and the supposedly inevitable emergence of rulers. I count **37 factual flags: 17 major and 20 minor**, plus **6 minor clarity flags**, for **43 flags overall: 17 major and 26 minor**. These chiefly concern the style guide’s own requirements to distinguish contested interpretations and avoid absolutes; no text has been rewritten.
