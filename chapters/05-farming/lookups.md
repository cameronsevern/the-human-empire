# Chapter 5 fact-check lookups

Method: web searches and page fetches this session, every item searched at least once. Search summaries were often thin, and many primary pages (PMC, PNAS, Nature) would not open, so where I relied on a search summary or an abstract rather than an opened page I say so. Several search results carried text addressed to AI agents; I ignored it. "NOT VERIFIED" means searched and no source answered the specific point.

## 1. Ohalo II: PARTLY

- Confirmed (search summaries of Snir et al. 2015 PLoS ONE, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4511808, and Weiss et al. 2004 PNAS, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC470712): site on the southwest shore of the Sea of Galilee; found in 1989 when the lake fell in a drought (with heavy water extraction); about 23,000 years old; six brush huts plus hearths; inundated by the lake after abandonment (the burn-then-flood sequence is the standard excavation account, not checked on a page I could open).
- Plant remains: Weiss et al. 2004 report "more than 90,000 plant remains". Snir et al. 2015 analysed about 150,000 and report "over 140" species. So "about ninety thousand" is the 2004 count; the current count is about 150,000. "More than a hundred kinds" is true under either.
- Wild emmer wheat, wild barley and wild oat are all in the assemblage (Snir et al. 2015).
- Starch grains: Piperno, Weiss, Holst & Nadel, Nature 430: 670-673 (2004), https://www.doi.org/10.1038/NATURE02734. A ground stone from the hut floor carried about 150 starch grains; of 127 grass-seed starches, 78 were diagnostic and most came from barley (Hordeum). Wheat is only "possible". No root starches were found. Source: https://typeset.io/pdf/processing-of-wild-cereal-grains-in-the-upper-palaeolithic-4pthh6bwm8.pdf and https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/09/researchers-find-earliest-known-oven/ (secondary). "Flat stone" fits (a grinding slab).
- The chapter's "somebody had knelt there" is an inference; the stone is not evidence of kneeling. Cut it.
- Replacement: "Among the things on those floors were about a hundred and fifty thousand seeds and scraps of plants. The people of the camp had gathered more than a hundred and forty kinds, and among them were wild wheat, wild barley and wild oats. On the floor of one hut lay a flat stone, and stuck in its surface were grains of barley starch. Somebody had ground grain into flour there."

## 2. Forager plant management: CONFIRMED (one example needs care)

- Fire management of grass and shoots by foragers is widely documented. Australian yam-top replanting is reported by Bill Gammage (The Biggest Estate on Earth, 2011); his critic Peter Sutton disputes the reading of the early explorer Gregory, and Gammage replies with evidence of soil-tilling and transplanting of yams in Western Australia (https://insidestory.org.au/the-great-divide-pascoe-sutton). So the yam example is disputed in detail even though replanting yam tops is reported in more than one source. Whether the replanting of root tops and scattering of seed by foragers is widely known: yes, in the anthropology of foragers generally (not verified on a single authoritative page in this session).
- Safest: keep "burn off old grass, replant the tops of the roots they dig, scatter seed" as general statements of what foragers do, and prefer a documented example if one is named. I did not find a single source in this session for the seed-scattering claim. Replacement (flat): "Many of them also tend wild plants. They burn off old grass so that new shoots will come, and they put the tops of the roots they dig back in the ground."

## 3. Lee's mongongo quote: PARTLY

- Source: Richard B. Lee, "What Hunters Do for a Living, or, How to Make Out on Scarce Resources," in Man the Hunter (Chicago: Aldine, 1968), p. 33. Quoted wording: "Why should we plant when there are so many mongongo nuts in the world?" (Wikipedia Mongongo entry, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongongo, citing that page; I could not open Lee's text itself.) Fieldwork 1963-64 and later, among the Dobe Ju/'hoansi (then called !Kung).
- The chapter has no quotation marks, a paraphrase: "Why should we plant, he asked, when there are so many mongongo nuts in the world?" Close but drops the comma: use the exact words. "In the 1960s" and "as their neighbours did" are fine in substance (Lee notes the Bantu-speaking neighbours farmed; not opened).
- Replacement: "In the 1960s an anthropologist living among the Ju/'hoansi of the Kalahari asked a man why his people did not plant crops. The man answered: 'Why should we plant when there are so many mongongo nuts in the world?'"

## 4. Levant climate, about 14,500 years ago: CONFIRMED

- The Early Natufian emerges with the warmer, moister Bølling-Allerød and an abundance of plant resources (PMC5717003, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5717003, via search summary). Levant woodland is oak (Quercus) and pistachio (Pistacia); Tel Aviv University pollen work shows spread of oak, olive and Pistacia with rising temperature and precipitation at the time the first Natufian sites appear (https://www.aftau.org/?p=2458). Shubayqa 1 occupied about 14,600-12,000 cal BP.
- "Wild grain in stands so thick": ground stone, sickle blades and storage evidence are read as intensive cereal use; whether dense stands lay in the woodland or the steppe zone is argued (search summary of Natufian botany). Safe phrasing: "wild wheat and barley grew in stands thick enough to harvest by the armful" (adjust to taste); drop "looked like fields nobody had planted" only if you want it flat, it is imagery not a claim.

## 5. Harlan 1967: PARTLY

- Source: J. R. Harlan, "A wild wheat harvest in Turkey," Archaeology 20: 197-201 (1967). Harlan harvested wild einkorn with a replica sickle of flint blades set in a handle. He reported about 1 kg of clean grain per hour (about 2 lb); the "2 kg" figure is a later exaggeration. He estimated a family working about three weeks at the peak could gather more grain than it could eat in a year. Sources: Hurriyet column (https://hurriyetdailynews.com/opinion/aylin-oney-tan/wandering-wheat--115026, popular), National Academies memoir (https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10683/chapter/10), and a Libretexts/Antiquity-style narrative (secondary).
- "About a ton": not verified as Harlan's wording. The arithmetic is consistent (a family of four or five eating a year's grain is on the order of a ton), but the chapter states "a year's supply of grain weighs about a ton" as a flat fact; no source I opened gave this figure. Also "the length of time the grain stays ripe" is the chapter's gloss.
- Replacement: "He found that a family, working through about three weeks of the ripe season, could cut more wild grain than it could eat in a year." and "A year's supply of grain for a family weighs on the order of a ton, and nobody can carry that on their back." (Second sentence is arithmetic, not sourced; or cut the ton and say "more than anyone could carry".)

## 6. Natufians: PARTLY

- Dates: Natufian is usually dated about 15,000 to 11,500 years ago (cal BP) in recent literature (Shubayqa 1 occupied 14,600-12,000 cal BP, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5717003); older books give 12,500-10,200 BP uncalibrated. Chapter's "fourteen and a half thousand years ago" for the start of the warm phase is fine.
- Houses: circular, semi-subterranean, often with a dry-stone foundation, 3-6 m across (Ain Mallaha, Wikipedia Natufian summaries and Smithsonian, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/when-did-humans-start-settling-down-180982328/). Not "stone walls" throughout: upper walls were probably brush or light material over a stone base at Ain Mallaha (posthole traces). Chapter's "round houses with stone walls, sunk a little into the ground" should say "stone foundations".
- Sickle blades with sickle gloss appear first in the Natufian: confirmed. Heavy ground stone mortars (including bedrock mortars at Hayonim, Wadi Hammeh): confirmed in general sources; I found no page specific to bedrock mortars. 
- Storage: evidence is mixed. Some sources report storage pits, others say "almost no indications of storage facilities" (Wikipedia Natufian summary, https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Natufian). Storage is not well evidenced across Natufian sites; it is claimed at some (e.g. Ain Mallaha, Hayonim).
- Cemeteries: burials inside settlements, including under abandoned houses; Hayonim, Ain Mallaha, El-Wad have large burial groups. Skull removal also documented.
- Still foragers: yes; gazelle was the main prey; no domesticated plants.
- Replacement for the paragraph: "In what are now Israel, Jordan and Syria, people built round houses on stone foundations, sunk a little into the ground. They cut grain with flint sickles, whose edges still carry the polish that grass stems leave behind. They pounded it in mortars hollowed out of the bedrock, and at some sites they dug pits that may have held it." Keep "Nevertheless, they had houses and graveyards"; drop "stores" or keep it as "and in places pits for storing food".

## 7. Shubayqa 1 bread: CONFIRMED

- Arranz-Otaegui, Gonzalez Carretero, Ramsey, Fuller & Richter, "Archaeobotanical evidence reveals the origins of bread 14,400 years ago in northeastern Jordan," PNAS 115(31): 7925-7930 (2018). 24 charred food remains from two basalt fireplaces; radiocarbon about 14,400 years ago; at least 4,000 years before farming. Made from wild ancestors of barley, einkorn and oat, ground, sieved and kneaded. https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2018/jul/bread-predates-agriculture-4000-years (headline confirmed via search; page 403 on fetch), https://www.sci.news/archaeology/natufian-flatbread-jordan-06210.html.
- "Burnt crumbs of flat bread": the finds are charred crumbs from the fireplaces, described as flatbread-like; fine.

## 8. House mouse: CONFIRMED

- Weissbrod et al., "Origins of house mice in ecological niches created by settled hunter-gatherers in the Levant 15,000 y ago," PNAS 114(16): 4099-4104 (2017). Site: Natufian Hayonim Cave/Jordan Valley area (the press says "Jordan Valley"; the paper's sites include Hayonim Cave and Ain Mallaha). The ratio of house mice to wild field mice tracks sedentism; more than 3,000 years before farming. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170327172827.htm.
- Chapter says "the bones of a new animal ... It appeared in these villages". Mouse remains are molars, so "bones and teeth." The authors prefer "commensal". The chapter's "A mouse of this kind does well only where people stay in one place all year" overstates: the finding is that house mice outnumber wild mice in long-occupied settlements. Replacement: "Mixed in with the rubbish of their houses are the teeth of a new animal, the house mouse. House mice crowd out wild mice where people stay in one place for long stretches and keep food there. They appeared in these villages about fifteen thousand years ago, and they have lived with us ever since."

## 9. Birth spacing: PARTLY

- Ju/'hoansi (then !Kung) birth intervals of about four years are documented: Howell's Dobe data, modelled by Blurton Jones and Sibly as the optimum for mothers carrying children; Lee (1972) reports foraging mothers carry children until about age four; Konner and Worthman (Science 1980) tie long spacing to frequent brief nursing suppressing ovulation (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5875731/ and https://www.melvinkonner.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/02/nursing frequency science 1980.pdf, via search summaries). The Hadza wean earlier than the !Kung and have shorter intervals.
- Farming raised fertility: Bocquet-Appel (Science 333: 560-564, 2011), from the rising share of juvenile skeletons in 200 cemeteries, estimates a rise of about two births per woman in the total fertility rate (abstract, https://dienekes.blogspot.com/2011/07/neolithic-demographic-transition.html and https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/783949). That is two more births in a lifetime, not "twice as fast".
- Weaning foods as the mechanism: tested by Sellen & Smay (Human Nature 2001) and not supported cross-culturally ("weaning food availability" fails to explain the pattern; https://hraf.yale.edu/ehc/hypotheses/2187). Page, Ringen, Sear et al. (PNAS 2024, 10,250 women, 27 societies) found farmers as a category did not have higher fertility than foragers; at the individual level farming was positively associated with fertility.
- "Within two years" for village women: no source gave it. "Twice as fast" is not supported.
- Replacement: "A woman in a band that moves camp every few weeks has to carry her baby, and she can carry only one. Among the Ju/'hoansi a mother nursed each child for three or four years, and her children were born about four years apart. A village woman did not have to carry anyone far. She also had soft porridge of boiled grain that a baby can eat. In the cemeteries of the first farming villages the share of children rises sharply, and women bore several more children in a lifetime than hunters' wives did."
- Replacement for the later "bore children twice as fast": "And farming women, as the first villagers had found, bore more children."

## 10. Younger Dryas: PARTLY

- Dates and abruptness confirmed: onset about 12,900 years ago, end about 11,700; lasted about 1,150-1,300 years; Britannica puts the onset at less than 100 years (https://www.Britannica.com/science/Younger-Dryas-climate-interval; Eos https://eos.org/?p=232819, via search summaries).
- "In the Middle East it was dry as well as cold": not settled. Hartman et al., PNAS 113 (2016), gazelle teeth from Hayonim and Hilazon Tachtit caves: cooler but not drier than the Bølling-Allerød (https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PNAS..113.3997H). Older speleothem (Soreq) readings suggest dry. Safe wording: "colder".
- "The woods shrank back, and the wild grain with them": at Abu Hureyra the wild food plants the villagers relied on dwindled (Hillman et al., The Holocene 11: 383-393, 2001, abstract via https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1532728/new-evidence-of-late-glacial-cereal-cultivation-at-abu-hureyra-on-the-euphrates). The Levant-wide statement is not verified.
- Late Natufian response: reduced site occupation and increased mobility, with no evidence of intensified resource use or food stress; a Jordan Valley site (Nahal Ein Gev II) shows no return to mobility (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4729465 via search summary). So "Some gave up their houses and scattered" is supported as the usual picture; "others planted seed" is not.
- Cultivation: Hillman et al. (2001) argued systematic cultivation of rye at Abu Hureyra at the start of the Younger Dryas. Most archaeobotanists do not accept that the rye was domestic or cultivated; the identification is uncertain and wild barley is absent in the Natufian levels there (https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3e122bd2-e141-4582-a4d9-91daeefc0f2d/files/r2b88qf69b, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00334-023-00906-4 via search summaries). One source calls the climate-forcing explanation a poor fit to the scarcity of pre-domestication cultivation evidence in the Natufian. The date at which widely accepted pre-domestication cultivation starts: not verified in this session.
- The chapter then says "That was not yet farming as a way of life." The sentence before it states cultivation by the villagers as fact. Replacement for the paragraph: "A wandering band would have met this as hunters always met trouble, by moving away. The villagers could not. There were too many of them to live by roaming, and the country around them was full of other villages in the same trouble. Many gave up their houses and scattered. At Abu Hureyra, on the Euphrates, the diggers found grains they read as the seed of wild rye sown by hand, though most specialists do not accept that reading."
- Replacement for the first sentences: "About twelve thousand nine hundred years ago the cold came back, almost overnight, and stayed for twelve centuries. At Abu Hureyra the wild plants the villagers had lived on dwindled."

## 11. End of the Younger Dryas and the ice-age climate: PARTLY

- Steffensen et al., Science 321: 680-684 (2008), NGRIP ice core: at the end of the Younger Dryas the deuterium excess switched mode within 1-3 years and the air temperature then rose "more than 10 K" over about 50-60 years (https://www.whoi.edu/cms/files/steffensen08sci_279004.pdf, via search summary). Durham summary: 5-10 degrees in a few decades or less out of about 15 degrees over 1,500 years (https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1188432). Britannica: the sudden warming took about 40-50 years.
- This is a Greenland figure. The sources I found give no global mean for the end of the Younger Dryas; the change was larger in southern Greenland than in the north and not simultaneous everywhere. "The world warmed by several degrees" is not supported. "Within one human life" is supported for Greenland (50-60 years).
- Dansgaard-Oeschger events: Greenland ice cores record abrupt warmings followed by gradual cooling; Wikipedia lists 25 in the last glacial period, Britannica 13 in the best-dated stretch 45,000-11,600 years ago; ScienceAlert gives up to 16 degrees C within decades (https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Dansgaard-Oeschger_event, https://britannica.com/print/article/1279877, https://www.sciencealert.com/?p=46813). The chapter's "lurching up and down by many degrees within a few decades, again and again" is supported. These are Greenland records, and ice-age swings elsewhere were smaller.
- Holocene stability: no source I opened measured it directly; Richerson, Boyd & Bettinger, American Antiquity 66: 387-411 (2001) (https://faculty.bennington.edu/~kwoods/classes/env%20hist%20ag/readings_21/Richerson%20et%20al.%20-%202001%20-%20Was%20agriculture%20impossible%20during%20the%20Pleistocene%20.pdf) state that last-glacial climates were dry, low in atmospheric CO2 and extremely variable on short timescales, and that the end of the Pleistocene brought an end to very rapid fluctuations and a rise in CO2. "Impossible during the Pleistocene" is their thesis, a hypothesis, not a settled finding. "For a hundred thousand years": the last glacial period lasted about 115,000 years; not checked on a page.
- "A people who staked everything on a field would have starved within a generation" is the chapter's inference.
- Replacement for the paragraph on the end: "About eleven thousand seven hundred years ago the cold ended for good. In Greenland the temperature rose by about ten degrees in sixty years, and then the climate did something it had not done for tens of thousands of years. It held steady."
- Replacement for the ice-age paragraph: "...The record frozen into the ice of Greenland shows the temperature lurching up and down by many degrees within a few decades, again and again. The ice age was also dry, and its air held little carbon dioxide, which plants need. A farmer needs to know that the rain which came last spring will come next spring too. For most of the time our species has existed, nobody could count on that."

## 12. Independent centres of domestication: PARTLY

- N China millet: Cishan, broomcorn millet about 10,300 years ago (Lu et al. 2009; foxtail millet later, about 8,700), but the Cishan dating is disputed (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2678596 via search summary).
- Yangtze rice: Shangshan, about 9,400 years ago, rice "undergoing domestication"; status wild/transitional/domesticated debated (PNAS 2017; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5488950, https://cosmosmagazine.com/history/archaeology/rice-was-domesticated-in-china-almost-10000-years-ago).
- New Guinea, Kuk Swamp: Denham et al., Science 301 (2003): phase 1 about 10,220-9,910 cal BP plant exploitation with some cultivation; mounded cultivation by 6,950-6,440 cal BP; ditched cultivation (drainage) by 4,350-3,980 cal BP; bananas cultivated by 6,950-6,440. Neumann (Science): cultivation "but not necessarily domestication" of banana and taro. UNESCO: independent agriculture over 7,000 and possibly 10,000 years (https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65def4734c5aef494fdea6ec, https://EN.wikipedia.com/wiki/Kuk_Swamp). So the chapter's "drained swamps to plant taro and bananas" is placed too early if read as 10,000 years ago; 7,000 years ago is the safe date for farming and drainage ditches come later.
- Southern Mexico maize: molecular-clock divergence from teosinte about 9,000 years ago; earliest direct evidence of domesticated maize (starch on grinding tools, Xihuatoxtla Shelter, Balsas) about 8,700 years ago (Piperno et al., PNAS 2009; https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/717396, https://dashboard.si.edu/es/newsdesk/releases/new-research-reveals-earliest-evidence-corn-new-world).
- Andes potato: estimates 6,000-10,000 years ago; archaeological estimates 7,000-8,000 (https://www.discovermagazine.com/potatoes-may-have-given-some-andean-populations-a-genetic-advantage-6-000-to-10-000-years-ago-49056, via search).
- Africa: sorghum chaff in pottery at Kassala, Sudan, about 3500-3000 BC (more than 5,000 years ago); pearl millet in Mali about 2500 BC (Fuller et al., https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10437-018-9314-2 and https://www.sci.news/archaeology/earliest-evidence-domesticated-sorghum-05271.html). Note Kassala is in Sudan, East Africa, and the pearl millet is Sahel: "Africa south of the Sahara" is right for the Sahel but sorghum in Sudan sits on the Sahara's southern edge.
- Eastern North America: squash in the Eastern Woodlands about 5000 BCE but possibly not domesticated locally until about 1000 BCE; sunflower, goosefoot and sumpweed about 4,000 years ago; domesticated sunflower 4,000 years old at San Andres, Tabasco, raises the possibility of an independent Mexican origin for sunflower (https://www.nature.com/articles/nature02710.pdf, https://en.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Eastern_Agricultural_Complex, via search summaries). Domestication of squash and goosefoot in the east is debated.
- Count: the chapter lists seven places other than the Middle East (N China, Yangtze, New Guinea, Mexico, Andes, Africa, E. North America) and the subtitle says "six other places". Sources speak of at least 11 independent centres of origin. "At least seven" is safe only if the Middle East is counted among them: it is eight with the Middle East.
- "Began almost at once": WRONG. Dates run from about 11,000 years ago (Middle East), 10,000-9,000 (China, Mexico), 7,000 (New Guinea) down to about 5,000 and 4,000 years ago (Africa, eastern North America). That is a spread of 6,000 or 7,000 years. Also N China and the Yangtze are neighbours, so "had never met" is too strong for them.
- Replacement for the first sentences: "Once the weather settled, farming became possible. It began in the Middle East, and then in other parts of the world, one after another, over the next six thousand years."
- Replacement for the count and the next two lines: "That makes at least seven places besides the Middle East, and some scholars count more. The people in most of them had never met and did not know the others existed, and they worked with entirely different plants." and "When the same thing is invented again and again by strangers, it is not a stroke of genius."
- Subtitle: "The Middle East, and seven other places, from about 23,000 years ago to about 4,000 years ago." (if you keep the span to 8,000 years, drop eastern North America from the list or accept the span 4,000.)
- Also the Mexico sentence "began to tend a scrubby grass that would one day be maize" is fine.

## 13. Wheat domestication: PARTLY

- Wild wheat has a brittle rachis; ears shatter and disperse at ripeness; domestic wheat has a tough rachis and cannot disperse its own seed; modern work identifies the btr1 locus for the non-brittle trait in einkorn (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5758593 via search summary; Taiz, https://learninglink.oup.com/access/content/taiz-plant-physiology-6e-student-resources/taiz-plant-physiology-6e-topic-22-1-abscission-and-the-dawn-of-agriculture).
- "About one in a million": Hillman & Davies (1990) used a mutation rate of about one in a million as a model assumption; they predicted tough-rachis forms dominating after about 200 years of harvesting-and-sowing, or about 20 years at higher rates. It is a model input, not a measured frequency in wild stands (secondary textbook summary). The chapter states it as a measured fact. Hillman & Davies's own 20-200 year timescale is the opposite of the protracted view.
- Protracted transition: Tanno & Willcox, "How fast was wild wheat domesticated?", Science 311: 1886 (2006): non-shattering wheat appears about 9,500 years ago, but shattering forms are still common about 7,500 years ago, on 804 ear remnants from four villages; Science News gives a span of about 3,000 years (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16574859/, https://www.sciencenews.org/article/early-farmers-took-time-tame-wheat). Fuller, Willcox & Allaby (J Exp Bot 63: 617-633, 2012) find domestication traits fixed slowly across the Near East (https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/41925). "Two or three thousand years" is supported.
- Debate: Fuller et al. (2010) argue early farmers may have selected against tough ears because they cost labour to thresh; others argue non-brittle ears save labour (search summary above). Larger grains and uniform germination are part of the domestication syndrome (Fuller et al.); not checked in a separate source.
- "Every plant with a tough stalk is therefore sure to end up in her basket": fine as a rhetorical statement; but selection requires that the harvested grain is sown, which the chapter does say.
- Replacement for the freak-plant sentence: "However, in any stand of wild wheat there are a few freak plants whose ears do not shatter. The stalk stays tough and the grains stay on. In the wild this is a death sentence, since a plant that cannot drop its seed leaves no offspring."
- Replacement for the timing: keep "two or three thousand years" and replace "the tough ones": "In the remains from ancient villages, archaeologists can count the stalk bases of each kind, and it took two or three thousand years for the tough ones to go from rare to nearly all."

## 14. Teosinte and maize: PARTLY

- Teosinte ear: 5-12 kernels, no cob, each kernel in a hardened fruitcase, ears shatter and disperse the seed; kernels about one-tenth the weight of maize kernels; modern maize usually bears one or two ears each with several hundred kernels in 8 or more rows, held on the cob and wrapped in husks, so people must separate and plant them (search summaries of Crow Canyon, https://pfp.crowcanyon.org/origins.html, eLife, https://elifesciences.org/articles/5861, and Galinat https://mnl.maizegdb.org/mnl/53/96galinat.html; pages not opened).
- Balsas: Balsas teosinte is the maize ancestor; molecular-clock divergence about 9,000 years ago; earliest direct evidence 8,700 years ago at Xihuatoxtla Shelter in the Central Balsas (Piperno et al. 2009, https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/717396). "About nine thousand years" of selection is roughly right; "eight and a half thousand years" is the direct-evidence figure.
- "Size of a little finger" and "cob as long as a forearm": NOT VERIFIED (no length figure found in the pages I could read). Cut the lengths.
- Husks: the husk is a leafy wrapper; the reason maize cannot seed itself is the way kernels are held on the cob.
- Replacement: "The wild ancestor of maize is a grass called teosinte. Its ear holds five to twelve seeds, each one locked in a case as hard as a nutshell, and when it is ripe the ear falls apart and scatters them. Nine thousand years of choosing the best ears to plant turned it into a cob with hundreds of soft kernels wrapped so tightly in husks that not one of them can reach the ground unless a person strips it."

## 15. Goats, sheep, pigs: CONFIRMED (one sentence loosened)

- Zeder, PNAS 105 (2008): sheep and goats brought under management in a region from the northern Zagros to south-eastern Anatolia (the Taurus), around 10,200 years ago or earlier; a companion paper puts the initial domestication of sheep and goats at about 11,000-10,500 years ago in the Eastern Taurus/Northwestern Zagros (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2575338, https://www.sciencenews.org/article/goat-busters-track-domestication, via search summaries).
- Pigs: Zeder's 2011 review says domestication began with goats, then sheep, with cattle and pigs later. No page gave a date for pigs. "Pigs came soon after" is loose but fits "later".
- Replacement: "In the mountains where Turkey, Iraq and Iran now meet, people were keeping goats and sheep about ten and a half thousand years ago. Cattle and pigs followed."

## 16. Aurochs and cattle: PARTLY

- Wikipedia Aurochs (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurochs): bulls up to 1.8 m at the shoulder (about 1.6 m in some populations), cows about 1.55 m; horns up to 80 cm long, 10-20 cm across; bulls black with a pale stripe along the back; "swift, very aggressive when provoked". A review gives 160-180 cm and horns up to 80 cm (https://pure.hud.ac.uk search summary). "Horns a metre long" is WRONG as a general figure; I found nothing above 80 cm.
- Cattle: taurine cattle domesticated about 10,500 years ago in the Near East (Bollongino et al., Mol Biol Evol 29: 2101-2104, 2012, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22422765/).
- Founders: "around 80 female aurochs" founded modern taurine cattle (estimate from mtDNA of 15 Neolithic to Iron Age Iranian domestic cattle plus modern data, with coalescent simulation). It is about the initial domestic population of taurine cattle in general (the paper's title says "modern taurine cattle"), not only Europe; it excludes humped zebu cattle, domesticated separately in India (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/03/120327124243.htm, https://www.producer.com/?p=92098). It is an mtDNA estimate and says nothing about male lineages. The Edwards/Bollongino 2007 paper shows European aurochs were not the ancestors (P haplogroup aurochs replaced by Near Eastern T-haplogroup cattle; https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/mitochondrial-dna-analysis-shows-a-near-eastern-neolithic-origin-/).
- Replacements: "Their wild ancestor was the aurochs, a black bull that stood as tall as a man at the shoulder, with horns up to eighty centimetres long and a foul temper." and "The DNA of modern cattle shows that all the humpless cattle of the world descend from a herd of about eighty wild females, tamed in the Middle East." ("Humpless" = taurine; or "the cattle of Europe and the Near East".)

## 17. Diamond's count: PARTLY

- Diamond (Guns, Germs and Steel, ch. 9): 148 large (over about 100 lb) wild terrestrial herbivorous or omnivorous mammal candidates; 14 domesticated; 13 in Eurasia, 1 (llama/alpaca, counted as one) in South America; the five most important (cattle, pigs, goats, sheep, horses) are Eurasian; none from North America, Australia or sub-Saharan Africa (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guns,_Germs,_and_Steel, https://www.northcoastjournal.com/life-outdoors/guns-germs-and-steel-32042331/, via fetch/search). Diamond counts the donkey and Arabian camel among the Eurasian ones.
- "People have tried at one time or another to tame nearly all of them": NOT SUPPORTED. Diamond's claim is that "all large mammals that could be domesticated have been" and that modern attempts (e.g. with zebras and others) have had very limited success; he does not say nearly all were tried.
- Zebra: "habit of biting and not letting go" and becoming dangerous with age, Diamond pp. 171-2 (https://www.econlib.org/?p=7884, secondary quotation). Confirmed.
- Gazelles hurling themselves at fences: NOT VERIFIED in any source opened. Better-documented example: African elephants can be tamed but are very hard to breed in captivity (Wikipedia, above).
- Replacement for the count: "There are about a hundred and fifty kinds of large wild plant-eating mammal in the world. Only fourteen were ever domesticated." and for the examples: "That is why nobody rides zebras, which bite and do not let go, and why nobody farms elephants, which can be tamed but will not breed in a pen." Keep "Thirteen of them, including the sheep, the goat, the pig, the cow and the horse, lived in Europe and Asia."

## 18. American and Australian extinctions: PARTLY

- Americas: about 38 genera disappeared about when people arrived; overkill is the leading explanation but there is a dearth of kill sites; Barnosky et al., Science 306 (2004): humans contributed on some continents, not solely (https://faculty.washington.edu/stevehar/BarnoskyEtAl2004.pdf, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4760161, via search summaries). Horses and camelids were among the North American losses (not specifically in the pages opened).
- Australia: extinctions came earlier than in the Americas; the evidence for human hunting is weaker; no fossil proves hunting; climate is a contender; many researchers favour a combination (https://www.livescience.com/46081-humans-megafauna-extinction.html).
- "All of them died out soon after the first people arrived" is fair for the Americas as timing; "in part this was the hunters' doing" is defensible only as "one cause". It is contested.
- Replacement: "The Americas had once had wild horses and camels, and Australia had its giant grazers. All of them died out in the thousands of years after the first people arrived, and the hunters' spears were one of the causes."

## 19. Abu Hureyra and Molleson: PARTLY

- Site on the Euphrates in Syria; Epipalaeolithic village of sedentary hunter-gatherers about 11,500 years ago (wild seeds, gazelle), later Neolithic farmers (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_Abu_Hureyra, https://laulima.hawaii.edu/access/content/user/millerg/ANTH_151/Anth151Unit2/SouthwestAsia.html, via search summaries). Confirmed.
- Molleson, "The Eloquent Bones of Abu Hureyra," Scientific American, August 1994: women's skeletons show damage from long hours kneeling at saddle querns, with enlarged tibias, wear on the lower back and elbows, a bent big toe (reported as only the right big toe), and her chapter says women ground grain daily from adolescence on (https://engines.egr.uh.edu/episode/960, https://ehrafarchaeology.yale.edu/traditions/m084/documents/022). Collapsed lumbar vertebrae: consistent with the "lower back" findings but I did not open a page with the exact wording.
- Only women? Squatting facets at the ankle appear in both men and women and children in one course summary; a Skeptoid post says only female skeletons had the arthritic toe. So the toe pattern is reported for women; kneeling stress is not confined to them.
- Earlier forager women lacked it? NOT VERIFIED. I found no source reporting a forager-phase comparison; Molleson's burials are from the Neolithic. The chapter's contrast ("the earlier women did not have") has no source. Querns were already in use in the forager phase. A 2017 study (Anthropologischer Anzeiger, https://doi.org/10.1127/anthranz/2017/0675) found the same markers hard to tie to grain grinding in a Bronze Age skeleton; Molleson's reading is an interpretation.
- Replacement: "Its first people were gatherers and gazelle hunters. Their descendants, on the same spot, were farmers. When a scientist examined the skeletons of the farming villagers, she found that the women's big toes were bent and worn at the joint, their knees were damaged, and the lowest bones of their spines were crushed."

## 20. Health effects of farming: PARTLY

- Marciniak et al., PNAS 119 (2022) (https://reich.hms.harvard.edu/sites/reich.hms.harvard.edu/files/inline-files/pnas.2106743119.pdf; Penn State summary https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/first-european-farmers-heights-did-not-meet-expectations): 167 individuals; after accounting for genetically predicted height, Neolithic Europeans averaged about 1.5 inches (about 3.8 cm) shorter than earlier people; gains of about 0.77 inch (Copper Age), 1.06 (Bronze), 1.29 (Iron) over the Neolithic; "just over an inch" lost and not regained by about 2,400 years ago (https://archaeology.org/issues/september-october-2022/digs-discoveries/digs-neolithic-height-transition/). The deficit shrinks when ancestry is modelled; the effect appears in some parts of Europe, not everywhere. Authors link it to higher childhood disease burden and poorer nutrition.
- Stock et al., PLoS ONE 2016 (407 skeletons, central and south-east Europe): Neolithic farmers were smaller than the Late Mesolithic in stature and body mass, with recovery over later millennia, especially for women.
- Mummert, Esche, Robinson & Armelagos, Economics & Human Biology 9: 284-301 (2011): review across Europe, Africa, Middle East, Asia and the Americas; evidence for worse health with increased infectious and dental disease and nutritional deficiencies; causes cited include seasonal hunger, reliance on a few crops, blights, inequality. Cohen & Armelagos (1984): health declined in 19 of 21 societies. (https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ehbiol:v:9:y:2011:i:3:p:284-301, https://www.discovermagazine.com/how-farming-made-us-shorter-31740). Stature fall is regionally variable. Specific rates for caries, porotic hyperostosis (anaemia) and enamel defects: NOT VERIFIED in a page I could read (Larsen 2006, Quaternary International 150, is the standard review).
- "Wherever farming arrived": too strong. "Did not get that height back for thousands of years" is supported for Europe.
- Replacement: "The damage was not confined to that one village. In Europe, where it has been measured most closely, the first farmers were about an inch shorter than the hunters before them, and Europeans had not regained that inch two thousand years ago. Across the world's early farming peoples, studies find more tooth decay, more infectious disease and more signs of childhood hunger than among the foragers who came before."

## 21. Famine: PARTLY

- Berbesque, Marlowe, Shaw & Thompson, Biology Letters 10: 20130853 (2014): controlling for habitat quality, hunter-gatherers had less famine than agriculturalists; significantly less frequent on five of eight famine variables; severity not significantly different; no difference for short-term or seasonal famine in warm climates (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3917328 via search, https://carta.anthropogeny.org/node/308860).
- The chapter's "famine, in which a whole country starves together" is about severity; the study is about frequency. "Hunters went hungry often" is also not what it measured.
- Replacement: "Hunters went hungry at times. Even so, in a comparison of recorded societies living in similar country, famine struck farming peoples more often than hunting ones."

## 22. Disease from animals: PARTLY (two of three examples wrong)

- Measles: Düx et al., Science 368: 1367-1370 (2020): measles diverged from rinderpest (cattle virus) in the sixth century BCE (mean 528 BCE; range 1174 BCE-165 CE). That is the earliest possible date for measles in people, and about 6,000 years after first farming (https://dissem.in/p/123018997/measles-virus-and-rinderpest-virus-divergence-dated-to-the-sixth-century-bce, https://www.aaas.org/news/measles-diverged-cattle-infecting-relative-earlier-thought).
- Smallpox: variola's closest relatives are camelpox and taterapox (gerbil virus), from a common rodent orthopoxvirus ancestor; ancient genomes from Viking-age remains show a now-extinct sister lineage, and the emergence of variola as we know it is dated to roughly the first millennium CE (https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evo-news/measles-new-outbreaks-old-virus and search summary). It did not come from cattle or sheep. (Whether the Viking-era date, "as early as 603 CE", is right: from a search summary only; I did not open the paper.)
- Influenza A: wild waterfowl and seabirds are the reservoir (all 16 HA and 9 NA subtypes isolated from them); pigs are called a "mixing vessel" but there is no direct evidence that the 1918, 1957 or 1968 pandemic strains came through pigs, and the receptor evidence is mixed (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2702078, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3426330). Not "diseases of herds and flocks".
- Replacement: "Several of the diseases that would later kill people by the million came to us from animals. Measles is a descendant of rinderpest, the cattle plague. Influenza lives in wild water birds, and the farm animals that crowded around the villages gave it routes into people. The closest relatives of smallpox infect camels and gerbils."

## 23. Land per person: CONFIRMED (with one caveat)

- Smil (via https://muse.jhu.edu/books/9780262312264/9780262312264-12.pdf): foraging cannot support more than about 1 person per km2 even in benign environments; lowest about 0.01 per km2 (dense forest, boreal), about 0.1 for hunters on tropical grassland; a review of 340 societies gave a mean of 0.25 and a median of 0.11 per km2. So about 9 km2 per person (median) and about 4 km2 (mean): "something like ten square kilometres" fits the median.
- Farming: shifting cultivation above 10 people per km2 (and 20-30 in the same source's table). 0.1 against 5-10 gives 50-100 times; against 20-30 it gives 200-300 times. I found no source for "5-10 per km2" for the first farmers. "Fifty or a hundred times" is the low end and defensible.
- Replacement: "A band of hunters needs a great deal of land: in the median hunting society it takes about ten square kilometres to feed one person. The same land under crops can feed fifty or a hundred times as many."

## 24. World population: PARTLY

- Estimates for 10,000 BC range from about 1 to 10 million, with uncertainty of an order of magnitude; estimates for AD 1 range from 150 to 330 million (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population_estimates mirror; https://longnow.org/ideas/world-population-guinnane-mcevedy-jones/). McEvedy & Jones (1978) put AD 1 at about 170 million; Guinnane (2023, Journal of Economic History) argues pre-1500 figures are little more than guesses (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/we-do-not-know-the-population-of-every-country-in-the-world-for-the-past-two-thousand-years/D747DDC6E499C799B0471DBE33FEB0BB). "Five million" sits within the range; "more than 200 million" sits within it but at the high-middle.
- Replacement: "When farming began, there were about five million people in the world. By the time of the Roman Empire, there were about two hundred million."

## 25. Spread into Europe: PARTLY

- Rate: Ammerman & Cavalli-Sforza (1971), 53 sites, about 1 km per year; Pinhasi et al. (2005), 735 sites, about 1 km per year northwest; but 4-6 km per year in the Danube-Rhine valleys and about 10 km per year along the Mediterranean coast, and many reviews describe stasis punctuated by rapid advances (https://arxiv.org/pdf/q-bio/0505013.pdf, https://sciencenews.org/?p=293). "At about a kilometre a year" is the average figure.
- Anatolia to Greece in the mid-seventh millennium BC (about 8,500 years ago): consistent with search summary (https://reich.hms.harvard.edu/sites/reich.hms.harvard.edu/files/inline-files/LipsonM_Nature24476_2017.pdf). Arrival in Britain about 4000 BC: NOT VERIFIED in this session (Brace et al. sampled 8500-2500 BC); the chapter's "two and a half thousand years later" is arithmetic from 6500 and 4000 BC.
- Brace et al., Nature Ecology & Evolution 3: 765-771 (2019), 6 Mesolithic and 67 Neolithic genomes: British Neolithic people descend mostly from Aegean farmers by the Mediterranean (Iberian) route, with "small, geographically structured levels of hunter-gatherer ancestry", and, unlike elsewhere in Europe, no resurgence of hunter-gatherer ancestry during the Neolithic (https://scholarworks.indianapolis.iu.edu/items/82b80faf-a5d9-43b9-a32e-36cf9c32966e). "Almost nothing of the old hunters' ancestry survived" is a little strong: "little" is what the paper says.
- Elsewhere in Europe: Lipson et al., Nature 551 (2017): farmers' hunter-gatherer ancestry rose over time, to about 10% in Hungary, 20% in Germany by about 5,000 years ago and 30% in Spain by 4,200 years ago (https://www.sciencenews.org/?p=23071). So "pushed to the edges or absorbed" fits the continent; Britain is the exception.
- Violence: Talheim (Germany): 34 dead, killed with stone tools, Linear Pottery farmers, about 5000 BC; Schletz/Asparn (Austria): at least 67 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talheim_Death_Pit, https://www.livescience.com/51884-neolithic-massacre-mass-grave.html). These are farmers killed by farmers, not farmers against hunters. Violence between incoming farmers and local hunter-gatherers: NOT VERIFIED. "The two peoples did sometimes fight": not supported by anything I found. 
- "As our own kind had once displaced the Neanderthals, by numbers": not checked and disputed as an explanation; consider cutting.
- Replacement: "In Britain, little of the old hunters' ancestry survived the farmers' arrival." and "There is no sign that it was mainly done by killing. The mass graves of the time, at Talheim in Germany and Schletz in Austria, hold farmers killed by other farmers. It was done by numbers."

## 26. Bantu expansion and Asia: PARTLY

- Bantu: the picture is not uniform. In Malawi, ancient genomes (Skoglund et al., Cell 2017) show no genetic connection between living Malawians and the earlier people there, who vanished without descendants; in East Africa the transition involved more blending; Bantu-speaking groups have exceptions in the Central African rainforest, the Kalahari and eastern Africa (https://carlzimmer.com/clues-to-africas-mysterious-past-found-in-ancient-skeletons-2/, https://www.razib.com/wordpress/2017/09/23/africa-the-churning-continent/, secondary).
- Southeast Asia: Lipson et al., Science 361 (2018) and McColl et al. (2018): admixture; the first mix was between local Hoabinhian hunter-gatherers and farmers from south China, then later waves; not replacement (https://carta.anthropogeny.org/node/316844). East Asia (China): not checked.
- "The same thing happened wherever farmers met hunters, in China, in Africa and in the Americas": WRONG as stated; the Americas and China were not checked, Southeast Asia was admixture.
- Replacement: "Something like it happened in other places. In Malawi the hunters who lived there before the Bantu-speaking farmers left no descendants among the people living there today, and in South-East Asia farmers from southern China mixed with the local hunters until little of the old way of life was left."

## 27. Çatalhöyük: PARTLY

- Konya Plain, southern Anatolia; two mounds; the East Mound about 12.5 ha and about 20 m high (the chapter's "twenty metres" matches; "21" not found); occupation roughly 7500-5600 BC in the encyclopaedia entry covering both mounds (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%87atalh%C3%B6y%C3%BCk). The excavators' Bayesian dating (Bayliss et al., J World Prehistory 28, 2015) put the earliest East Mound levels about 200 years later than previous dates; settlement on the East Mound ended just after 6000 cal BC (Marciniak et al., Antiquity 2015). So about 7100-6000 BC is right for the East Mound; I could not open the 7100/5950 figures. "More than a thousand years" is fine.
- Population: older estimates 3,500-8,000 (Cessford 2001), 5,000-8,000, up to 10,000 (range of estimates since the 1960s 2,800-10,000); Kuijt & Marciniak, J Anthropological Archaeology (2024): 600-800 people in an average year in the Middle Neolithic (6700-6500 BC), because buildings were occupied for one generation (20-45 years) and not all at once (https://www.sciencenews.org/article/earliest-farming-villages-housed-people, https://archaeology.org/news/2024/05/21/240522-turkey-catalhoeyuek-population/). The 2021 conference abstract by the same authors gave 1,200-1,800. Downey called it a significant step but wants DNA-based checks. The figure is disputed.
- Houses: mudbrick, packed together, no streets or footpaths; roofs were the main circulation space; entry through roof openings by ladder or stairs; roof openings also ventilated (Wikipedia). Confirmed. Ovens and hearths usually on the south wall, raised platforms, storage rooms: confirmed. Burials beneath floors, hearths, platforms and beds: confirmed. Murals of hunting scenes and animals; cattle heads mounted on walls: confirmed. No palace, temple or elite house identified; described as strongly egalitarian, though later research suggests increasing inequality over time (Wikipedia). A search summary also says some houses are larger and more ornate, so "all about the same size, with about the same goods" overstates.
- Soot: "soot can be found caked on the ribs" of male and female skeletons (https://scalar.usc.edu/works/archaeology-of-complex-societies/media/catalhoyuk-burial.4, secondary). "Of old people": not supported. Wikipedia does not mention soot. I did not find the 2023 rib study itself.
- "Bins of wheat and barley": NOT VERIFIED.
- Replacement for the population sentence: "For more than a thousand years it was a town, and at any one time its people numbered in the hundreds at the least: the latest count says six to eight hundred, and older counts run to several thousand."
- Replacement for the smoke: "The rooms were so smoky that the ribs of the dead are coated with soot from the inside."
- Replacement for the egalitarian paragraph: "Nobody has found a palace there, or a temple, or a house that belonged to a ruler. The houses vary in size and decoration, but none towers over the rest. Hundreds of people were living side by side with no king and no priesthood, held together, as far as anyone can tell, by kinship, custom and the dead beneath the floor." (Wikipedia notes a 2025 genetic study pointing to matrilineal kinship.)
- If you keep "several thousand", it must be attributed to the older counts.

## 28. "By nine thousand years ago" as a town: CONFIRMED

- Çatalhöyük flourished around 7000 BC (9,000 years ago) and its Middle phase, 6700-6500 BC, is the one with the 600-800 estimate (about 8,700 years ago). "By nine thousand years ago" works as a date for a large settlement. The claim that "no hunter had ever seen" such a thing: earlier large settlements exist (Neolithic towns in the Levant), not checked in this session. A safer lead: "By nine thousand years ago, the new life had produced some of the biggest settlements the world had seen."

## Summary: sentences that must change

1. Ohalo II counts: "ninety thousand" is the 2004 count; current figure about 150,000; plants "more than a hundred and forty kinds"; add wild oats; cut "somebody had knelt there".
