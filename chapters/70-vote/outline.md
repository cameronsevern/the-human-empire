# Chapter 70 — When did women get the vote?

**Line under the title:** New Zealand, Britain and the United States, from 1792 to 1920.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

New Zealand was first, in 1893, and most of Europe and North America followed between 1906 and 1920. The revolutions that declared all men equal had meant men. A married woman in the English-speaking world of 1800 had no legal existence apart from her husband: her property, her wages and her children were his. The campaign to change that grew out of the campaign against slavery, and took seventy years. It was won first in new societies on the edge of the settled world, where women were scarce and allied with the movement against drink, and last in the old centres of power. In Britain it took window-breaking, prison and hunger strikes, and then a war that needed women's work. The same war gave the vote to the two men in five who had never had it either.

## The story

1. **Opening: a roll of paper.** Answer first. Wellington, 1893: a petition a quarter of a kilometre long is unrolled down the floor of Parliament.
2. **Covered.** What the law said a wife was. A novelist whose husband took her children and her earnings.
3. **All men and women.** A book of 1792. A convention in London where the women were put behind a curtain, and a meeting in a small American town in 1848. The quarrel after the Civil War.
4. **The word "person".** A philosopher proposes one change of wording in 1867. The case against, including the queen's.
5. **The edge of the world.** Why New Zealand, Wyoming, Australia and Finland came first. The alliance with the campaign against drink.
6. **The typewriter and the bicycle.** What was changing underneath: schools, colleges, offices, and a machine that let a woman go where she liked.
7. **Deeds, not words.** Britain after 1903: the militants, the windows, prison, the feeding tube, and a racecourse in June 1913.
8. **Canaries.** The war of 1914 and the munitions factories. The law of 1918, and who else it let in.
9. **Ending: a letter from his mother.** Tennessee, August 1920. Who was still shut out. The last holdouts.
