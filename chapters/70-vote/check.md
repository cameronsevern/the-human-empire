1. **Factual error and contradiction with chapter 56 — high confidence.** “Women first won the vote in national elections in New Zealand, in 1893.” Also: “It was the first time that the women of any country had voted for a national parliament.”

   New Zealand was the first self-governing country to enfranchise women nationwide. Women had already voted in American congressional elections: surviving New Jersey poll lists document them in 1800. Chapter 56 explicitly establishes New Jersey women’s voting rights before 1807. The distinction is nationwide enfranchisement, not the first female participation in national elections. [Museum of the American Revolution](https://www.amrevmuseum.org/virtualexhibits/when-women-lost-the-vote-a-revolutionary-story/timeline); [New Zealand Ministry for Women](https://www.women.govt.nz/about-us/history-womens-suffrage-aotearoa-new-zealand).

2. **Misleading central premise — high confidence.** “Before any of that could happen, women had to win the right to own property, to keep their own wages, and to be treated by the law as persons at all.”

   This conflates women generally with married women under coverture, and turns overlapping reforms into a necessary sequence. Unmarried women and widows could already own property; some exercised voting rights while married women remained subject to coverture. Chapter 56’s property-owning New Jersey voters directly undermine this formulation. [Museum of the American Revolution](https://www.amrevmuseum.org/virtualexhibits/when-women-lost-the-vote-a-revolutionary-story/pages/how-did-the-vote-expand-new-jersey-s-revolutionary-decade).

3. **False generalisation underpinning the explanation — high confidence.** “The first places to give women the vote were all new societies, far from the old centres of power.” Also: “In such places there was no ancient aristocracy to defend the old order, and men had seen women work beside them to build a farm or a town from nothing.”

   Finland, included in the following list, was not a new settler society. Its legislature comprised four estates, including the nobility, until the 1906 reform. That reform followed upheaval in the Russian Empire and replaced the estate system. The frontier explanation needs to be confined to the cases it actually explains. Both sentences also breach the rule against exception-breaking absolutes. [Finnish Parliament](https://www.eduskunta.fi/en/parliament-and-its-activities/brief-history-of-parliament).

4. **Factual error — high confidence.** “It took until 1882 for a married woman in England to gain the right to own what she earned.”

   The Married Women’s Property Act **1870** protected a wife’s earnings from separate employment and literary, artistic or scientific work. The 1882 act extended separate property rights much further. [1870 act, section 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/33-34/93/pdfs/ukpga_18700093_en.pdf).

5. **Overstated legal claim — high confidence.** “Whatever a wife owned on her wedding day became her husband's.” Also: “So did anything that she later earned or inherited.”

   These erase significant distinctions between property types and between common law and equity. Trusts and marriage settlements could preserve property for a wife’s separate use; inherited land did not simply become her husband’s outright property in the same way as personal possessions. The oppression is real, but “whatever” and “anything” make the account legally false. [Research on married women’s separate property](https://academic.oup.com/manchester-scholarship-online/book/29265/chapter-abstract/243255923); [legal history of women’s landownership](https://www.nyulawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/NYULawReview-77-4-Crosswhite.pdf).

6. **Misleading account of legal permission — high confidence.** “He could lock her up, and the law allowed him to beat her, in moderation.”

   Blackstone describes moderate correction as an **older** doctrine whose legality had been questioned since Charles II. He also says wives could obtain legal protection against their husbands, and describes confinement as conditional on misconduct. This sentence presents a contested, qualified position as an unrestricted contemporary entitlement. [Blackstone, *Of Husband and Wife*](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/blackstone_bk1ch15.asp).

7. **Factual overstatement — high confidence.** “In Australia, the law that gave white women the vote in 1902 took it away from Aboriginal people of both sexes.”

   The federal act imposed a sweeping racial exclusion, but expressly preserved an exception under section 41 for people already entitled to vote in state elections. It also did not itself abolish state voting rights. Specify the federal exclusion and its exception rather than claiming universal removal. [Australian Electoral Commission](https://www.aec.gov.au/indigenous/milestones.htm).

8. **Doubtful blanket reassurance — high confidence that qualification is needed.** “They took care not to injure anyone.”

   Avoiding casualties was a stated principle of the property campaign, but this sentence treats it as a reliable description of every operation. Incendiary attacks injured postal workers: the Postmaster-General reported five injured in Dundee and two in York in February 1913. Distinguish the movement’s professed intention from the hazards and actual consequences. [Contemporary parliamentary report](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/1913-02-10/debates/0cbdfddd-d957-4932-98cc-c897f66f5db6/SuffragistOutrages).

9. **Oversimplified causation and a signpost — medium-high confidence.** “The question was settled by something else.”

   Followed by the war-work narrative, this makes the war appear to resolve the suffrage struggle independently of campaigning and political negotiation. The change of prime minister, the Speaker’s Conference and the urgent need to repair electoral registration were also central. Many young women doing war work remained excluded in 1918. The sentence additionally announces an explanation instead of giving it, contrary to the signpost rule. [UK Parliament’s assessment](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/electionsvoting/womenvote/case-studies-women-parliament/what-difference/did-the-war-make-a-difference/).

10. **Factual error in the opening object — high confidence.** “It was two hundred and seventy metres long, and it carried the signatures of nearly thirty-two thousand women, about a quarter of all the adult women in the colony.”

    The roughly 32,000 signatures belonged to **thirteen petitions combined**, not the single roll. The main petition was recorded as containing about 25,500 signatures. The contemporary quarter-population comparison concerned adult **European** women, not all adult women. [NZHistory’s petition documentation](https://nzhistory.govt.nz/page/about-suffrage-petition).

11. **Factual overstatement and contradiction with the chapter’s own chronology — high confidence.** “Of the hundred people who signed the declaration, one was still alive when American women finally voted, seventy-two years later.”

    The surviving signer is the familiar historical claim; “when American women finally voted” is the problem. Many American women voted before 1920, including in presidential elections. The chapter itself has already or subsequently establishes earlier Wyoming suffrage, while chapter 56 supplies New Jersey. The milestone is the nationwide constitutional prohibition on disfranchisement **because of sex**. [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2003/fall/poh-nineteenth-amendment).

12. **Factual error and internal contradiction — high confidence.** “The typewriter, sold from the 1870s, and the telephone exchange created the office job, and employers filled these posts with young women, who were cheaper than male clerks.”

    Office work long predated both technologies. Chapter 62 explicitly places Engels working in an office in the 1840s; this sentence itself refers to existing male clerks. These technologies expanded and changed clerical employment and opened particular occupations to women. They did not create office work.

13. **Exception-breaking absolute — high confidence.** “In Britain, forty years of polite petitioning had achieved nothing.”

    It had not secured the parliamentary vote, but some women gained municipal voting rights in 1869. “Nothing” erases a concrete electoral achievement and makes the transition to militancy misleading. [UK Parliament’s suffrage timeline](https://assets-learning.parliament.uk/uploads/2019/12/Votes-for-women-lesson.pdf).

14. **Overstated legal universality — high confidence.** “The act of 1918 gave the vote to every man over twenty-one.”

    It enfranchised virtually all men aged **21 and over**, and qualifying servicemen from **19**, while retaining disqualifications, including provisions against conscientious objectors. “Every” is precisely the kind of absolute prohibited by the style sheet; “over twenty-one” also technically excludes twenty-one-year-olds. [UK Parliament](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/electionsvoting/womenvote/case-studies-women-parliament/what-difference/the-vote-for-some/).

15. **Incomplete explanation of disenfranchisement — high confidence.** “Until 1918, two British men in every five had no vote either, because they did not own or rent property of sufficient value.”

    The proportion is supported, but the explanation omits residence and registration requirements, which excluded mobile workers and became especially consequential for soldiers. Property value alone does not explain the missing two fifths. This does **not** contradict chapter 62’s statement that most working men had gained the vote: a majority and a large excluded minority can coexist. [Oxford’s account of the 1918 reform](https://ohrh.law.ox.ac.uk/the-representation-of-the-people-act-1918-a-democratic-milestone-in-the-uk-and-ireland/).

16. **Chronological error — high confidence.** “In 1792, while the French were writing their constitution, an Englishwoman named Mary Wollstonecraft published a reply to those who wanted to leave women out of it.”

    The relevant constitution had been completed in **1791**. Wollstonecraft’s immediate target included Talleyrand’s 1791 proposals for unequal education. The sentence conflates the drafting of that constitution with the subsequent publication of her response. [Wollstonecraft’s dedicatory letter and historical introduction](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/wollstonecraft1792.pdf).

17. **Ambiguous identification and misleading implication — high confidence.** “Two of them, walking back to their lodgings that evening, agreed that something must be done about the condition of women as well.”

    “Them” points back to the excluded American women delegates, but the pair were Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton; Stanton attended on her honeymoon, not as a delegate. The reader also has no way to identify the pair before Stanton appears by name later. Identify their different roles here. [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/a-great-inheritance-abolitionist-practices-in-the-women-s-rights-movement.htm).

18. **Overstatement about clothing — medium-high confidence.** “A young woman who owned one could go where she pleased, without a chaperone or a carriage, and she could not ride it in a floor-length skirt and a tight corset.”

    Cycling encouraged clothing reform, but women also cycled in long skirts and corsets; manufacturers offered adaptations of both garments. Difficulty, discomfort and danger are defensible claims. Physical impossibility is not. [Smithsonian’s cycling garments and contemporary advertisements](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/object-project/online/bicycles/sport-corset).

19. **Reader-orientation gap.** “In her pocket were two flags in the movement's colours and the return half of her railway ticket.”

    The return ticket is presented as a loaded clue, but its significance is never explained. A general reader may not understand that it bears on the disputed question of whether Davison intended to die. Either explain that question briefly or omit the unexplained clue; the ticket alone should not be made to settle her intention.

20. **Unexplained historical term.** “One small mountain canton still decided its local affairs at an open-air meeting in the town square, to which each citizen carried his sword as proof of his right to take part.”

    “Canton” needs the short explanation required by the style sheet: a constituent state of Switzerland. Without it, the reader cannot clearly distinguish the national franchise granted in 1971 from the local exclusion overturned in 1990.

21. **Signpost sentence.** “To see what women were up against, one has to know what the law said a wife was.”

    This announces the explanation that the next paragraph already supplies. It describes the narrator’s teaching plan rather than advancing the story.

22. **Signpost sentence.** “The same law did something that is often forgotten.”

    This announces an interesting point before stating it. The following sentence supplies the actual information and can carry the transition itself.
