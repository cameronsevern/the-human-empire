## Factual flags

1. **Major — new false implication about Curie’s education.**

   > “The most famous of those scientists answered the doctors' warnings better than any pamphlet.”

   “Those scientists” refers back to graduates of the women’s colleges in the preceding paragraph. Curie studied at the Sorbonne, not a women’s college. The Nobel dates remain correct; the new bridge gives her the wrong educational background. [Nobel Prize biography and educational account](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/themes/marie-and-pierre-curie-and-the-discovery-of-polonium-and-radium/).

2. **Major — new internal contradiction about British voting rights.**

   > “In Britain, however, the change in women's lives had not yet reached the ballot box.”

   The next sentence says women had won votes for town councils. The missing distinction is **the parliamentary ballot box**. Moreover, the surviving phrase “town councils and nothing more” remains too narrow: qualifying women also voted for school boards from 1870, county councils, and other local bodies. This is a new contradiction plus an incomplete correction, rather than a repetition of the earlier complaint that campaigning had “achieved nothing.” [History of Parliament’s account of women’s electoral participation](https://historyofparliament.com/2018/08/29/women-and-politics-1868-1918/).

3. **Major — coverture is presented as complete legal nonexistence.**

   > “Because she had no legal self, she could not sign a contract, make a will or go to court in her own name.”

   Coverture severely restricted a married woman’s independent civil capacity; it did not eliminate her legal personality altogether. Blackstone explicitly distinguishes separate criminal responsibility and permits independent proceedings in ecclesiastical courts, alongside exceptions in common-law proceedings. The new causal clause turns a broad description of restrictions into an inaccurate universal rule. [Blackstone, *Of Husband and Wife*](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/blackstone_bk1ch15.asp).

4. **Minor — the revision partly reintroduces the corrected Finland problem.**

   > “Other new societies followed.”

   The paragraph now introduced this way includes Finland and Norway. Finland was not a new settler society: its 1906 reform replaced a legislature composed of four estates, including the nobility. The earlier correction limiting the frontier explanation to “the settler colonies” remains intact, but this new umbrella sentence blurs that distinction again. [Finnish Government’s history of the estates and the 1906 reform](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/history-and-buildings/house-of-the-estates).

5. **Minor — misleading chronology of hunger strikes.**

   > “About a thousand went to prison, and there the struggle took a new form.”

   Following the 1912 window-smashing and subsequent attacks, this introduces hunger strikes as the next development. The first suffragette hunger strike occurred in **1909**, and force-feeding began that year. The passage needs a clear indication that it is stepping back in time. [UK Parliament’s chronology](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/electionsvoting/womenvote/overview/deedsnotwords/); [National Archives’ contemporary force-feeding material](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/what-methods-did-the-suffragettes-use-to-gain-the-vote/).

6. **Minor — the unchanged description of the 1918 female franchise is inaccurate.**

   > “In 1918 Parliament gave the vote to women over thirty who were householders or the wives of householders, about eight and a half million of them.”

   The threshold was **30 and over**, consistent with the chapter’s later “until thirty,” rather than strictly over thirty. Householders and their wives were also not the complete set: qualifying occupation of other premises and university qualifications provided additional routes. [Representation of the People Act 1918, section 4](https://www.parliament.uk/globalassets/documents/upload/1918-rep-people-act.pdf).

## Clarity flags

- **Explicit signposts introduced by the revision:**

  > “What this meant in practice can be seen in one marriage.”

  > “The reasons these places led are not hard to find.”

  > “The problem with the soldiers points to a fact that is easily forgotten.”

  These announce an example, explanation, or noteworthy fact instead of stating it. The last sentence recreates the earlier check’s “often forgotten” problem in new wording.

- **An unresolved signpost:**

  > “The deadlock was broken by something else.”

  This still withholds the subject to announce an explanation. The expanded registration discussion addresses the earlier factual concern about war causation, but the style violation remains.

- **A misleading analogy:**

  > “Lawyers called her condition ‘coverture’: she was covered by him, much as a roof covers whatever is beneath it.”

  The roof explains the ordinary word “covered,” but not the legal relationship. It may suggest shelter or protection when the paragraph’s subject is restricted independent legal capacity.

- **Unclear scope:**

  > “It took them eight years to act on it.”

  “It” refers to doing something about women’s condition generally, although the dated event is specifically organizing the convention. Readers may infer eight years without any women’s-rights activity.

- **A gap between claim and example:**

  > “The most famous of those scientists answered the doctors' warnings better than any pamphlet.”

  Apart from the educational error above, the doctors’ stated warning concerns fertility. Nobel Prizes demonstrate scientific achievement; the chapter does not explain how they answer that particular warning.

- **Repetition rather than additional explanation:**

  > “While the argument went on, the lives of women in the industrial countries were changing in ways that made the old case harder to believe.”

  > “The claim that women belonged in the home was being undermined, year by year, by what women were actually doing.”

  Both sentences make substantially the same point before the examples begin.

- **Repeated age threshold:**

  > “Women, by contrast, had to wait until thirty.”

  > “The age limit for women was set at thirty for a frank reason.”

  The second immediately repeats the first instead of moving directly into the explanation.

- **Padding:**

  > “The case cuts both ways.”

  The surrounding sentences already establish both disagreement and the two opposing effects.

  > “The movement was not short of support.”

  The next sentence immediately supplies the membership and political support.

  > “What she intended is still a mystery.”

  The next sentence repeats that nobody knows whether Davison intended to die.

- **Unexplained historical vocabulary:**

  > “One of the women turned away was a Quaker preacher from Philadelphia.”

  “Quaker” receives no explanation.

  > “On one afternoon in 1912, well-dressed women walked down the main shopping streets of London, took out hammers that they had hidden in their muffs, and smashed hundreds of plate-glass windows.”

  Many general readers will not know that muffs are hand-warmers.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions, or prohibited chapter cross-references.

## Overall

The revision preserves the explicit dates, figures, and quotations, but it is not entirely fact-neutral: several bridges introduce misleading relationships or chronology. The main repairs are the Curie connection, the British ballot-box contradiction, and the absolute coverture explanation. The added signposts and repeated summaries also need trimming.
