1. **Factual error — the graduate lists do not measure turnover of the governing class.**  
   > “Half the governing class, in other words, was being replaced at every round by men from families that had never governed.”

   The lists describe **new examination graduates**, not the whole governing class. Absence of an official father, grandfather or great-grandfather also does not establish that a family had “never governed”: uncles, maternal relatives and earlier ancestors are excluded. The two lists date from 1148 and 1256, outside the chapter’s stated period. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [Peter Bol, “Sung Examinations and the Social Order”](https://www11.ihp.sinica.edu.tw/storage/w2_file/1600hJzbwBS.pdf).

2. **Factual error and exception-breaking absolute — officials’ sons had alternative routes into office.**  
   > “No aristocracy could form under those conditions, since an official's rank died with him, and his sons had to face the examiners like everybody else.”

   The Song retained **yin**, a privilege through which officials could secure entry into officialdom for relatives. Families also transmitted wealth, education and connections. The disappearance of the Tang aristocracy did not mean hereditary advantages disappeared. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [*The Cambridge History of China*, “Sung Government and Politics,” pp. 56–57](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B360D3EB6D72ADEE74E46FD8570548EC/9781139193061c1_p19-138_CBO.pdf/sung_government_and_politics.pdf).

3. **Factual error — the ending reverses part of the history of written examinations.**  
   > “Other countries followed, and universities and schools took up the written paper.”

   European universities used written examinations before Britain’s nineteenth-century civil-service reforms. Cambridge developed written mathematical examinations during the eighteenth century; Oxford introduced examination reform in 1800. Schools’ public examinations also developed through universities. Chinese influence on civil-service reform is defensible, but the simple China → governments → universities and schools sequence is not. This also affects the opening’s “schools copied the governments.” **Confidence: very high on chronology; moderate on the precise extent of Chinese influence.** Sources: [*Public Examinations Examined*, pp. 51–53](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/208231572358210940/pdf/Public-Examinations-Examined.pdf); [Cambridge’s examination history](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/about-us/our-history/).

4. **Factual error — landownership is confused with hereditary jurisdiction.**  
   > “The Song governed through officials who had no soldiers and no ancestral lands, who were moved to a new post every three years, and who were forbidden to serve in their home districts.”

   Song officials could belong to landed families and hold or establish family estates. What they generally lacked was an independently inherited territorial command. Rotation and avoidance rules did not make them landless men without family resources. **Confidence: high.** Source: [“The State, the Gentry, and Local Institutions,” especially its discussion of Song lineage estates](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/state-the-gentry-and-local-institutions-the-song-dynasty-and-longterm-trends-from-tang-to-qing/98FF05D23838EA992380D13B122A4598).

5. **Factual error and internal inconsistency — the opening identifies the wrong examination stage.**  
   > “Several hundred candidates had come through the lower rounds to this final examination, and the men who passed it would be the next generation of the empire's officials.”

   Ouyang’s famous assessment of Su Shi belongs to the **Ministry of Rites examination**, not the final palace examination. Later, the chapter correctly describes the palace examination as a separate third stage. Calling Ouyang’s sitting “final” makes those accounts conflict. **Confidence: high.** Source: [*Shantang Sikao*, volume 83, recording the Ministry of Rites anecdote](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=230086&if=en).

6. **Factual error — Britain’s implementation is compressed into the wrong decade.**  
   > “It began with its administration in India and then turned to the home country, bringing in recruitment by open written examination, anonymously marked.”

   Open competition began for the Indian Civil Service in 1855. The home commission established that year initially retained nomination and largely qualifying examinations; **1870** was the decisive extension of open competition. The preceding “In the 1850s” makes this read as one completed reform. **Confidence: very high.** Sources: [House of Commons Library, “The Civil Service”](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10819/); [the 1858 parliamentary discussion explicitly describing continued nomination](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Lords/1858-06-08/debates/2f4d6823-b33f-492b-b6bd-cb7464d8f936/LordsChamber).

7. **Factual overstatement — military governors did not control China uniformly for two centuries.**  
   > “The Tang survived it only by handing power to other generals, and for the next two centuries China was in the hands of military governors who obeyed the court when it suited them.”

   This suppresses substantial recovery of Tang central authority. By about 820 the court had regained control over much of the empire, although important Hebei commands remained autonomous. Regional military power mattered enormously, but its reach and independence varied over time and place. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Institute of Historical Research review of Nicolas Tackett’s *The Destruction of the Medieval Chinese Aristocracy*](https://reviews.history.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/pdf/Reviews_in_History_-_The_Destruction_of_the_Medieval_Chinese_Aristocracy_-_2015_07_30.pdf).

8. **Factual error — a Tang degree was not an entitlement to appointment.**  
   > “A man who passed the hardest version received the title of ‘presented scholar’, and with it the right to an appointment.”

   Under the Tang, obtaining the *jinshi* qualification normally left further selection by the Ministry of Personnel before appointment. Eligibility and an assured job are different things. **Confidence: high.** Source: [*Xu Tongdian*, volume 17, describing the separate examination and appointment stages](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=237734&if=gb).

9. **Factual error — the three-year cycle is projected backwards across the Song.**  
   > “Under the Song, the examinations were held every three years in three stages, with the first in the candidate's home prefecture, the second in the capital, and the last in the palace, where the emperor himself presided, so that every successful man was in a sense his personal pupil.”

   The regular triennial system dates from the later 1060s; earlier Song examinations followed different schedules, commonly biennial. That distinction matters because the opening occurs in 1057. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [Charles Hucker, *A Dictionary of Official Titles in Imperial China*](https://www.academia.edu/127122615/A_DICTIONARY_OF_OFFICIAL_TITLES_IN_IMPERIAL_CHINA); [*Cambridge History*, “Sung Government and Politics,” p. 57](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B360D3EB6D72ADEE74E46FD8570548EC/9781139193061c1_p19-138_CBO.pdf/sung_government_and_politics.pdf).

10. **Factual error and exception-breaking absolute — the Song remained vulnerable to military coups.**  
    > “A dynasty that had tamed its generals and filled its government with essayists was safe from its own army.”

    In 1129 imperial-guard commanders forced Emperor Gaozong to abdicate temporarily. Civilian control reduced the danger; it did not eliminate it. Although that incident falls beyond the chapter’s main period, the sentence makes an unrestricted claim about the dynasty. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [*Consolidation of Southern Sung China under the Emperor Kao-tsung*, p. 48](https://eprints.soas.ac.uk/33540/1/11010290.pdf).

11. **Factual error — the five dynasties did not all begin through the same kind of usurpation.**  
    > “In the next fifty-three years, five dynasties followed one another in the north, each of them founded by a general who overthrew his master.”

    The Later Han is a clear exception: Liu Zhiyuan established it after the Khitan destruction of the Later Jin and their withdrawal from Kaifeng. The repeated military succession is real; “each … overthrew his master” is not. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Naomi Standen’s doctoral student Lance Dugdale’s study of the Liao and their neighbours](https://etheses.bham.ac.uk/id/eprint/9526/1/Dugdale2019PhD_vol1.pdf).

12. **Doubtful factual claim — early printing had a broader market.**  
    > “The first uses were scriptures and examination texts, and the second soon outran the first.”

    Commercial almanacs were already important in the ninth century; an example dated 877 survives, and attempts to suppress their printing show an established market. The assertion that examination texts “soon outran” scriptures needs evidence specifying when and by what measure. **Confidence: high about the omitted almanac market; the claimed crossover remains unverified.** Source: [British Library’s International Dunhuang Programme, “The Diamond Sutra and Early Printing”](https://idp.bl.uk/blog/exhibition-the-diamond-sutra-and-early-printing/).

13. **Reader continuity — an important change since chapter 21 is unexplained.**  
    > “Their sons married one another's daughters and entered office on the strength of their fathers' rank.”

    Chapter 21 established that Qin officials were appointed, paid and unable to pass their jobs to their sons, and that the Han retained appointed officials. The reader needs a brief explanation of how powerful officeholding families subsequently entrenched themselves—and how inherited access differed from inheriting a particular office. **This is a missing bridge, not a direct contradiction.** See [chapter 21](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md).

14. **Reader orientation — an administrative term arrives unexplained.**  
    > “Of every hundred who entered the prefectural examination, one or two went on to the capital.”

    “Prefecture” first appears in the preceding description of the stages without explanation. A general reader needs to know that it was an administrative district containing several counties. Otherwise the relationship between the home examination, counties, provinces and capital remains hazy. This also breaks the style guide’s requirement to explain historical terms at first use.

15. **Style — an announcement substitutes for the point itself.**  
    > “There was a further difficulty, which the first Song emperor had created on purpose.”

    This announces a forthcoming difficulty before explaining it: a straightforward instance of the prohibited signpost sentence. The following discussion already supplies the substance.
