# 31. Why do we take exams?

*China, from 589 to about 1100*

Because the emperors of China wanted officials who owed their careers to the throne and not to their families. A written test, marked by someone who did not know whose paper it was, gave them such men. Many centuries later, other governments copied the method.

In the spring of 1057, the chief examiner of the Chinese empire sat reading essays in the capital. His name was Ouyang Xiu, and he was the most admired writer of his day. Several thousand candidates had come through the lower rounds to the great examination in the capital, and the few hundred who passed it would be the next generation of the empire's officials.

One essay stood out from the rest. It was plain, forceful and free of the showy style that Ouyang had been campaigning against for years. He wanted to rank it first. Then a suspicion stopped him. The essay was so much to his taste that he feared it had been written by one of his own pupils, who was among the candidates. If he put his pupil at the top of the list, people would talk. He ranked it second.

He could not check, because the paper had no name on it. For the past fifty years, the name on every examination paper had been pasted over before marking. For the past forty, the candidates' papers had not even reached the examiners in the candidates' own hands. A staff of clerks copied each one out afresh, so that no examiner could recognise a friend's handwriting, and it was the copy that was marked.

When the names were uncovered, the essay turned out to belong to a twenty-year-old from the far south-west whom Ouyang had never heard of. He was Su Shi, who went on to become one of the greatest poets in the Chinese language.

Nothing in that scene would surprise anyone who has sat a public examination, which is a measure of how thoroughly the Chinese invention has conquered the world. The set texts, the anonymous script and the nervous wait for a list of results are all in it. So is the principle, which was a strange one in 1057, that a governor of a province should be chosen for what he can write under supervision on a given morning, and that his father's name is irrelevant.

## A ditch for rice

The examination grew up inside an empire that had been put back together.

After the fall of the Han, China had been divided for three and a half centuries, with dynasties of mounted invaders in the north and Chinese courts in the south. In 589 a northern general who had seized his master's throne conquered the south and reunited the country. His dynasty, the Sui, lasted less than forty years, and in that time it carried out a project on the scale of the First Emperor's.

During the centuries of division, the balance of the country had shifted. Millions of people had fled south from the wars, cleared the forests of the Yangzi valley, and planted rice. The south was now the richer half. The armies and the capital, however, had to be in the north, facing the grasslands. The rivers of China run from west to east, and none of them connected the two.

The Sui therefore dug a waterway from the Yangzi to the Yellow River and beyond, joining older canals and natural streams into a single route well over a thousand kilometres long. A barge loaded with rice near the southern coast could now be poled all the way to the capital. The court historians say that more than a million men and women were conscripted for one section alone, and that a great many of them died. The dynasty that built the Grand Canal was overthrown by the people who had dug it. The canal remained, and for the next thirteen hundred years it tied the two halves of China together.

The dynasty that took over, the Tang, ruled from 618 for almost three centuries, and its early years were among the most brilliant in the country's history. Its armies pushed out along the old Han road to the west until they held the oasis towns as far as the high mountains of Central Asia. Its capital, laid out as a grid of walled districts covering more than eighty square kilometres, held about a million people. Among them were Persian and Central Asian merchants, Turkish soldiers, Korean and Japanese students, and Buddhist monks from India. The pottery figures buried in Tang tombs show bearded foreigners in peaked caps leading camels.

## Born to office

Under the Tang, the examination already existed, and it mattered very little.

The real power lay with a small number of great clans, who had run the governments of the north and south through all the centuries of division. Under the Qin and the Han, officials had been appointed and moved about by the court, and a post was not a family's property. That principle had worn away. Weak courts had needed the backing of powerful families, and had paid for it by ranking candidates for office according to their birth. Each of the great families could trace its descent back for centuries, and compilers published ranked lists of them so that everyone would know which family outranked which. Their sons married one another's daughters and entered office on the strength of their fathers' rank. The Tang emperors' own family was not near the top of the list, and they knew it.

For any ruler, an aristocracy of this sort is a constraint. An official who holds his position by birth owes the emperor nothing. He has his own lands, his own followers and a network of cousins in every ministry. If the emperor displeases his family, he can be awkward, and if the dynasty falls his family will serve the next one as it served the last.

What a ruler wants is a servant with nothing behind him, whose whole career depends on royal favour. The Chinese found them by setting an examination.

The idea was old. Since Han times, officials had been asked to recommend able men, who were then questioned at court. The Sui and the Tang turned the questioning into a regular written test on the old books of Confucius and his followers, and on the composing of poetry. A man who passed the hardest version received the title of "presented scholar", and with it the chance of an appointment.

The ruler who saw the use of this most clearly was a woman. The empress Wu had entered the palace as a junior concubine at about fourteen, became the consort of one emperor, ruled through her sons after his death, and in 690 set them aside and took the throne herself. She is the one woman in Chinese history to have reigned as emperor in her own name. The great families despised her. She needed officials who would be loyal to her against them, and she found them by enlarging the examinations and promoting men from obscure families who passed. The court historians, who came from the class she had offended, gave her a dreadful reputation.

Even so, under the Tang only twenty or thirty men a year won the highest degree, far too few to staff an empire. Most posts still went to the well born, and the examiners knew the candidates by name. Du Fu, whom many Chinese consider their greatest poet, sat the examination and failed.

## A cup of wine

The old families were brought down by a rebellion.

In 755 a frontier general named An Lushan, the son of a Central Asian father and a Turkish mother, turned his army on the capital. He commanded more than a hundred and fifty thousand men on the north-eastern border, and the emperor had trusted him completely. The rebellion lasted eight years and wrecked the north. The Tang survived it only by handing power to other generals, and for much of the next two centuries large parts of China were in the hands of military governors who obeyed the court when it suited them.

In the last decades of the dynasty, rebel armies took the capital and hunted down the old families in their mansions. One rebel leader, who had himself failed the examinations, held the city for more than two years, and a poem of the time says that people walked the avenues over the bones of ministers. When the fighting ended, the genealogies no longer mattered, because the people listed in them were dead.

The Tang fell in 907. In the next fifty-three years, five dynasties followed one another in the north, each of them founded by a general. In 960 it happened a sixth time. The army of the last of them, on the march against an invasion, woke its commander one morning, draped him in a yellow robe, the colour reserved for the emperor, and hailed him as the founder of a new dynasty, the Song.

The new emperor understood how he had come to the throne, and that the same thing would be done to him. A year later, the story goes, he invited his senior generals to dinner. When the wine had gone round, he told them that he could not sleep at night. Any one of them, he said, might wake up some morning to find a yellow robe thrown over his own shoulders, whether he wished it or not. The generals asked, in alarm, what he wanted them to do. He suggested that they retire to large estates in the provinces, with handsome pensions, and marry their children into his family. The next day, all of them reported sick and resigned.

In place of the generals, the emperor put civilians. The Song governed through officials who commanded no soldiers and held no territory of their own, who were moved to a new post every three years, and who were forbidden to serve in their home districts. They were recruited through the examination, which now became the main road to office.

## Four hundred thousand characters

Under the Song, the examinations were held every two or three years, in three stages, with the first in the candidate's home prefecture, a district of several counties, the second in the capital, and the last in the palace, where the emperor himself presided, so that every successful man was in a sense his personal pupil. The numbers were of a different order from the Tang's. Each round now produced several hundred "presented scholars", and each of them went into government.

A boy who was to attempt it began at about five. Before he could write an essay he had to know the Confucian books by heart, and they run to more than four hundred thousand characters, roughly the length of four fat modern novels. He learned them at the rate of a couple of hundred characters a day, chanting them aloud, and took six or seven years over it. Then came the commentaries, the histories, and the art of composing verse and argument in the approved forms. If all went well, he was ready for his first attempt at about twenty. Most men who eventually passed did so in their thirties, after failing several times.

The odds were terrible, and they grew worse. By about 1100, some eighty thousand men were sitting the first stage at each round, and at the end of the Song period the figure was close to four hundred thousand. Of every hundred who entered the prefectural examination, one or two went on to the capital. A man could spend his whole life as a candidate, and many did.

In law, almost any male subject could enter. The sons of merchants, who had been shut out under the Tang, were now admitted. Women could not enter at all. In practice, the candidate needed a family that could do without his labour for twenty years and pay a teacher, which ruled out most farmers.

Nevertheless, the examination did what the emperors wanted. Two complete lists of successful candidates survive from the Song, each giving the careers of the man's father, grandfather and great-grandfather. In both, more than half of the new graduates had no official among those three generations. At every round, in other words, about half of the new entrants to the governing class came from families with no recent history of office. A high official could still ease a son or a nephew into a junior post, and money and a house full of books were advantages that passed down. Rank itself, however, could not be inherited, and a family that failed the examinations for two or three generations sank back into the crowd.

The effect on Chinese society ran deeper than the government. In every county of the empire, the leading families now wanted the same thing, a degree for a son, and went about getting it in the same way. A rich landowner hired a tutor and bought books. A group of relatives pooled money for a school and for the travelling costs of the cleverest boy among them. Whether they lived on the northern frontier or in the far south, a week's sail from one another, their sons were memorising identical sentences and learning to admire the same writers. The men who failed, who were the great majority, became the schoolmasters, the doctors, the letter writers and the managers of local affairs. They went on respecting the system that had rejected them.

The Chinese empire had found a way to give the leading families of every district a stake in the state and to make them compete for its favour, at their own expense.

## For my two parents

All those candidates needed books, and a new technique supplied them.

In 1907, the archaeologist who had found the Han watchtowers near Dunhuang was shown a walled-up chamber in a cave temple outside the town. It was packed from floor to ceiling with manuscripts, which had been sealed in about nine hundred years earlier. Among them was a paper scroll five metres long. It was a copy of a short Buddhist scripture, in the Chinese translation made by the captive monk Kumarajiva, with a picture of the Buddha preaching at the front. Nobody had written it out by hand. Every character and every line of the picture had been printed.

At the end is a note: reverently made for free distribution to all by Wang Jie, on behalf of his two parents, with a date that works out as the eleventh of May 868. It is the oldest dated printed book in the world.

The note explains why printing began. In Buddhist teaching, to copy a scripture is to earn merit, and merit can be passed to one's parents. A man who paid a scribe to copy a text once earned a little. A man who could turn out a thousand copies earned a great deal. The earliest printers were in the business of mass-producing merit.

The method was simple, and it suited Chinese writing. A scribe wrote the text in ink on a sheet of thin paper. The sheet was pasted face down on a smooth block of pear wood, so that the writing showed through backwards. A carver then cut away the wood around every stroke, leaving the characters standing up from the surface, in reverse. After that, a printer brushed the block with ink, laid a sheet of paper on it, and rubbed the back with a dry brush. One man could pull more than a thousand sheets in a day. The blocks were stacked in a storeroom and could be brought out to print another batch twenty years later.

In the tenth century, during the chaos between the Tang and the Song, a chief minister had the entire set of Confucian books carved and printed for the government. The job took twenty-one years. Under the Song, government offices, monasteries and commercial workshops all printed, and the price of a book fell to a fraction of what a handwritten copy had cost.

The first things printed were scriptures, charms and calendars. Examination texts soon joined them. Printers in the south made a good living from the classics, from dictionaries, and from collections of model answers by previous top candidates, which the government from time to time tried to ban. Cheap books explain a good part of the swelling crowd at the examination halls. A family of modest means could now own the syllabus.

About 1040, a craftsman named Bi Sheng went a step further. He made a separate little block of baked clay for each character, set them in a frame with wax, printed, and then melted the wax and used the characters again. Movable type would one day transform Europe, whose alphabets need only a few dozen different letters. Chinese needs thousands of different characters, and a printer had to keep them all sorted and to hand. For most jobs it was quicker and cheaper to carve the whole page, and Chinese printers went on carving for another eight hundred years.

## Essays against cavalry

The examination state was a remarkable machine for holding a large country together. The Song had some hundred million subjects by 1100, nearly twice as many as all of Europe, and governed them with twenty or thirty thousand ranked officials, who had read the same books and written in the same style. The neighbouring kingdoms saw the advantage. Korea set up examinations on the Chinese pattern in 958, and Vietnam followed.

The Chinese themselves saw the weakness. The examination tested memory, literary polish and command of moral philosophy. It did not test whether a man could survey a canal, audit a granary or defend a town. A reforming chief minister of the 1070s complained that candidates spent their youth shut up composing verse, and were then sent out to govern without knowing anything of the world. He abolished the poetry paper and replaced it with essays on the classics and on policy. After he fell from power, the poetry came back.

A dynasty that had tamed its generals and filled its government with essayists had little to fear from its own army. Safety from foreign armies was a different matter, and the grasslands to the north had not ceased to produce horsemen.

The method itself outlasted the Song by many centuries. The last imperial examination in China was held in 1904. By then the idea had travelled. Europeans who visited China from the 1500s onwards wrote home in astonishment about a country where office was earned by study, and reformers took note. In the 1850s the British government, which had always filled its posts through family connections and the favour of the powerful, was looking for a way to stop. It began with its administration in India, bringing in recruitment by open written examination, and by 1870 it had done the same at home. In the debates, its friends and its enemies both pointed to China. Other countries followed. The written paper, which European universities had begun to use for their own purposes, became the ordinary gate to a career.

On the day the results came out, a list of names was pasted on a wall outside the examination compound, in order of merit, and the candidates pushed through the crowd to find out whether twenty years of work had been wasted. Su Shi wrote, many years after his own name had gone up near the top of such a list, and after his career had been wrecked by politics, that he hoped only that his son would grow up ignorant and stupid, and so reach high office without any trouble.
