# Chapter 31 — Why do we take exams?

**Line under the title:** China, from 589 to about 1100.
**Length:** 4,300 to 4,900 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

The written examination was invented to solve a ruler's problem. Chinese emperors were hemmed in by great families who held office by birth and by generals who made and unmade dynasties. A test on set books, marked without the marker knowing whose paper it was, produced officials who owed their careers to the throne. After the old families were destroyed in the wars of the ninth century, the Song made the examination the one road to power and honour. Every ambitious family in every county then spent its money on the same books, which bound the educated class of a vast country to the state and to one another. Printing, which began as a way of mass-producing Buddhist merit, made those books cheap and multiplied the candidates. The price was a ruling class of men trained to write essays. Twelve centuries later, European governments looking for a way to end jobs-for-friends copied the Chinese method, and schools copied the governments.

## The story

1. **Opening: the paper with no name.** Answer first. The examination of 1057: the chief examiner marks down the best essay because he suspects his own pupil wrote it. Covered names and recopied papers.
2. **A ditch for rice.** China reunited after three and a half centuries. The canal that joined the rice of the south to the capitals of the north, and what it cost. The Tang and their capital of a million.
3. **Born to office.** The great families and their genealogies. Why an emperor wants officials with no family behind them. The empress who used the examination against the old houses. A great poet fails.
4. **A cup of wine.** The frontier general's rebellion and the century of warlords. The old families wiped out. A general becomes emperor and retires his generals over dinner.
5. **Four hundred thousand characters.** What a candidate had to do and for how long. The odds. Who could sit and who really did. New men: more than half. What families did in response.
6. **For my two parents.** The oldest dated printed book and the reason it was printed. How a woodblock is cut. The state prints the classics. Cheap books, more candidates. Movable type and why it did not win.
7. **Essays against cavalry.** What the system built: one educated class from the Great Wall to Canton, and neighbours who copied it. What it did not test.
8. **Ending.** The British borrow it in the 1850s. The list on the wall.
