# 46. How did Russia get so big?

*Siberia, China and Central Asia, from 1552 to 1771*

By following the rivers of Siberia eastward in search of fur. Russian trappers and soldiers went from the Ural Mountains to the Pacific in about sixty years, because the peoples in between were few and had no guns. They halted only when they met another growing empire, China, and between them the two brought the long age of the horse peoples to an end.

The animal that drew Russia across a continent is about the size of a house cat. The sable is a kind of marten that lives in the cold forests of northern Asia, where it hunts squirrels and mice among the fallen trees. Its winter coat is dark brown or black, and so dense and fine that it feels the same whichever way it is stroked.

Rich people in Europe, Turkey, Persia and China all wanted it, and there was never enough. In Moscow in the 1600s, the very best sable skins sold for more than a craftsman earned in a year. Russians called fur "soft gold". Their country had no gold or silver mines at that time, and little else that foreigners wished to buy. Fur was what the tsar used to pay for foreign cannon, foreign officers and foreign silver, and at times it supplied a tenth of all his income.

The difficulty with an animal like the sable is that it breeds slowly and is easily hunted out. The forests west of the Urals had been stripped of their best fur by the middle of the 1500s. East of the mountains lay a forest larger than the whole of Europe, and almost nobody in Russia knew what was in it.

## Eight hundred men

For three hundred years the Russians had been in no position to take it. Their princes had been subjects of the Mongols, and then of the Mongols' heirs, the Tatar khans who ruled the grasslands along the river Volga and blocked the way east. In the 1550s the first Russian ruler to be crowned tsar, Ivan the Terrible, brought cannon down the Volga and took the khans' two capitals. The road to the Urals was open.

The Urals themselves are not much of a barrier. They are old, worn hills, and in many places a person can cross them without noticing. What lay beyond them was a small Tatar kingdom called Sibir, which took fur as tribute from the forest peoples around it. The tsar granted the borderlands on the Russian side to a family of salt merchants, the Stroganovs, with permission to defend them however they liked.

The Stroganovs hired Cossacks. These were free men of the southern frontier, many of them runaway serfs, who lived by fishing, raiding and serving whoever paid. Their leader was a river pirate named Yermak, who was wanted by the tsar for robbing royal boats. In 1582 he crossed the hills with about eight hundred and forty men, dragging his boats over the watershed, and floated down into Sibir.

The khan of Sibir had many times that number of warriors, armed with bows. Yermak's men had muskets and a few light cannon, and they fought from their boats and from behind barricades. They took the khan's capital in the autumn. Yermak sent the tsar a present of several thousand sable skins, and was forgiven everything.

He did not live to enjoy it. Three years later the Tatars caught his camp asleep on a river bank, and Yermak drowned trying to swim to a boat, pulled under by the weight of his armour. By then, however, the tsar's own soldiers were coming over the hills behind him, and what they found suited them perfectly.

Siberia is drained by three enormous rivers, the Ob, the Yenisei and the Lena, each of which flows north to the Arctic Ocean. Between them the land is flat, and the branches of one river system reach almost to the branches of the next. A party in a flat-bottomed boat could row up a branch, drag the boat a few kilometres over a low ridge, and float down into the next great valley. At each of these crossings the Russians built a fort of upright logs with a watchtower, a storehouse and a church, and left a few dozen men in it.

In this way they moved east at a speed that has few equals in the history of exploration. They were on the Yenisei by 1619 and the Lena by 1632. In 1639 a party of about thirty Cossacks came down a mountain stream to a grey sea and built a hut on the shore. It was the Pacific Ocean, and it lay six thousand kilometres from Moscow. From Yermak's crossing it had taken fifty-seven years.

## A skin from every man

Russian trappers worked the forests themselves, in small bands. The tsar's share, however, came mostly from the people who already lived there.

Beyond the Tatar kingdom, Siberia was thinly settled. Perhaps a quarter of a million people lived in all of it, in dozens of small nations of reindeer herders, hunters and fishers. Most of them had no towns and no rulers above the level of a clan chief, and their weapons were bows and spears. When a Russian party arrived at a new river, it demanded that every grown man of the district deliver a fixed number of sable skins each year to the fort, as tribute to the tsar. The number varied from place to place and might be five, ten or more.

To make sure that the skins arrived, the Russians took hostages. They seized the chief men of each clan, or their sons, and kept them in a locked hut inside the fort. Once a year the hunters brought their furs to the gate, and the hostages were led out and shown to them, to prove that they were alive. Then the furs were handed over and the hostages went back in. If a clan failed to pay, the Cossacks went out and burned its camp.

For a hunter of the forest peoples, this was a heavy and bewildering demand. Reindeer and elk fed and clothed his family, and sable had been a sideline. He now had to spend much of each winter chasing a small animal for strangers. As the sable grew scarce, the tribute stayed the same, and men fell into debt to the fort or fled deeper into the forest. The Russians also brought smallpox. In the 1630s and again later in the century it ran through peoples who had never met it, and some of the northern nations lost more than half their number.

The Cossack at the fort did not have an easy life either. He was a thousand kilometres from the next Russian settlement, in cold that froze bread solid, and his pay from Moscow came years late or not at all. He made his living from what he could squeeze out of the district beyond what he owed the tsar, and when the sable in his valley ran out, he had every reason to move on to the next. The exhaustion of the fur drove the Russians eastward as surely as the wind drives a fire.

One thing the northern forest could not give them was bread. Wheat does not ripen there, and flour had to be hauled for months from the farms of western Siberia and Russia. When the Cossacks heard that far to the south-east there was a great river, the Amur, whose valley was warm and full of grain, they went to take it. There they ran into people who had cannon of their own.

## Keep your hair or keep your head

While the Russians were crossing Siberia, something just as large had happened in China, and it had started in the forests north of Korea.

The people who lived there were hunters, farmers and horsemen, the descendants of the forest tribes that had once ruled northern China and been driven out by the Mongols. Around 1600 a chief named Nurhaci united them. He sorted all his followers, with their families, into military units called banners, each under its own coloured flag, and the banners replaced the old clans. His son gave the people a new name, the Manchus, and declared a dynasty, the Qing.

The Manchus numbered about a million. The Ming empire of China, on the other side of the Great Wall, had about a hundred and fifty times as many. The Ming, however, were falling apart. The court had spent its treasury on the war against the Japanese in Korea. A run of cold, dry years brought famine to the north, and starving farmers and unpaid soldiers gathered into rebel armies.

In April 1644 one of those rebel armies took Beijing. The last Ming emperor walked up the hill behind his palace and hanged himself from a tree. The strongest Ming army was then stationed two hundred and fifty kilometres to the east, at the fortress where the Great Wall meets the sea, facing the Manchus. Its general found himself with rebels behind him and Manchus in front. He chose the Manchus. He opened the gate and invited them in to help him put down the rebellion.

The Manchus came through the wall, beat the rebels, entered Beijing and did not leave. Within a year they issued an order that showed what kind of rulers they meant to be. Every Chinese man was to shave the front of his head and wear the rest of his hair in a long plait down his back, as Manchu men did. It was a sign of submission that could be seen from across a street. People summed up the choice in a rhyme: keep your hair and lose your head, or keep your head and lose your hair. Several cities resisted, and their people were massacred.

Having made that point, the Manchus ruled China in the Chinese way. They kept the examinations, the scholar-officials and the tax system. Their emperors studied the Confucian classics and wrote Chinese poetry. They kept their own people apart, living in walled quarters in the big cities and forbidden to marry Chinese, as a permanent army of occupation. The second of them to reign in Beijing, the Kangxi emperor, came to the throne as a child in 1661 and ruled for sixty-one years, and he was among the most capable rulers that China ever had.

The Manchus were not simply a steppe people or a farming one. They understood horses and they understood cannon, and they now had the wealth of China behind them.

## A treaty in Latin

The Amur valley lay in the Manchus' own homeland, and the Cossacks who appeared there in the 1650s behaved as they had everywhere else, taking hostages and demanding furs and grain. The local people appealed to Beijing. For thirty years there were skirmishes. In 1685 the Kangxi emperor sent an army with cannon to the main Russian fort on the river. It took the fort and burned it. The Russians came back and rebuilt it, and the Qing army besieged it again.

Neither government wanted this war. Moscow was eight months' travel from the Amur, and what the tsar really wanted from China was trade, since the Chinese would pay well for Siberian fur. The emperor, for his part, had a more dangerous enemy rising in the west and did not want the Russians to make friends with him.

In the summer of 1689 the two sides sent ambassadors to a Russian outpost called Nerchinsk. They had an immediate practical problem, which was that nobody on either side spoke the other's language. The Qing delegation had brought two Jesuit priests, a Frenchman and a Portuguese, who served at the emperor's court as astronomers. The Russian party included a Pole who knew Latin. The negotiations were therefore conducted in Latin, with the Jesuits walking back and forth between the tents.

The treaty they wrote drew a border along a range of mountains north of the Amur. Russia gave up the valley and pulled down its fort. In return, Russian merchants received the right to bring caravans to Beijing. The official text was the Latin one. It was the first treaty that China had signed with a European state, and it treated the two rulers as equals, which was something the Chinese court of that age granted to nobody else.

Its deeper meaning concerned the people who were not at the table. Between Russia and China lay the whole belt of grassland from Manchuria to the Caspian Sea, and the herding peoples who lived on it. For two thousand years, when a Chinese army marched against such people, they had simply moved away across the steppe. Now, in the east at least, there was a line, and a government on the far side of it that had agreed to send fugitives back.

## Grain across the desert

The enemy that worried the emperor was a people called the Zunghars. They were western Mongols, and their pastures lay in the high basins north of the mountains of Central Asia, in what is now the far north-west of China. Like most Mongols by that time they were Buddhists, who followed the teachers of Tibet.

Their ruler, Galdan, had been raised as a monk in Tibet and had come home to take power when his brother was murdered. He was building a real state. He had farmers growing grain in the oases, craftsmen making muskets and powder, and Muslim merchants running his trade. In 1688 he attacked the eastern Mongols, who lived in what is now Mongolia. They fled south in their tens of thousands and asked the emperor for protection, and he took them under his rule. Galdan followed them to within a few days' ride of Beijing. It looked as though the days of Genghis Khan might be coming back.

Every Chinese dynasty had faced this situation, and the difficulty was always the same. Horsemen could retreat into the grassland faster than a farming army could follow, and a farming army that did follow had to carry every mouthful with it, because the herders drove their animals away and the steppe grows nothing else that soldiers can eat. Chinese armies had crossed the desert and won before, at ruinous cost. More often the horsemen had only to wait.

The Kangxi emperor decided to solve the problem by brute organisation. In 1696 he led an army in person across the Gobi desert. Tens of thousands of soldiers marched in three columns, with heavy cannon carried in pieces on camels. Behind them came thousands of carts loaded with grain, and wells were dug ahead of them along the route. The emperor wrote cheerful letters home about the water and the weather. After eighty days, one of his columns caught Galdan's army on the far side of the desert, with its animals worn out and nowhere left to run, and destroyed it with cannon and muskets. Galdan died the next year, a fugitive.

The horse and the bow had not become worse weapons. A mounted archer could still shoot faster than a musketeer. What had changed was that a rich state with a paid army could now carry its food and its guns deep into the grassland. It was a huge effort, and the emperor's own column nearly ran out of grain. It could be done, however, and the horsemen could no longer count on being left alone.

Russia was doing the same thing on the western half of the steppe, with a different tool. For centuries the Tatars of the Crimea had ridden north every summer to seize Russian villagers and sell them in the slave markets of the Ottoman empire, and they had carried off people by the hundred thousand. In the 1600s the tsars built a wall against them. It was a line of earth ramparts, felled trees and wooden forts eight hundred kilometres long, manned by soldiers who were paid in land. When the first line was secure, farmers ploughed up the grassland behind it, and a new line was built farther south. Step by step, the open range of the horse peoples was turned into wheat fields.

## The new frontier

The Zunghars survived Galdan's defeat and held their mountain basins for another sixty years. In the 1750s they fell out among themselves over who should be khan, and one of the rivals went to Beijing for help. The emperor of the day, Kangxi's grandson, saw his chance and sent in the armies.

When the Zunghars rose against the occupation, he decided to make an end of them. His orders to his generals survive, and they are explicit. He told them to show no mercy, and to kill the men who were fit to fight. The soldiers obeyed, and smallpox, to which steppe peoples had little resistance, did even more of the killing than they did. A Chinese scholar who studied the records two generations later reckoned that of about six hundred thousand Zunghars, three in ten were killed by the army and four in ten died of disease. Most of the rest fled to the Russians or were given away as servants. Some historians call this a genocide, and the word fits the evidence. The Zunghars ceased to exist as a people.

The emperor settled their empty pastures with farmers, exiles and soldiers from other parts of his empire, and named the region Xinjiang, which means "the new frontier". Tibet, which the Zunghars had invaded a generation earlier, had been occupied by a Qing army in reply, and a Qing official and garrison had sat in Lhasa ever since. Mongolia was divided into banners under Manchu supervision. China under the Qing was twice the size it had been under the Ming.

The last steppe empire able to threaten a great state was gone. Two farming empires, each with guns, forts, clerks and granaries, now pressed in on the grassland from both sides, and the part that was still free, the Kazakh steppe in the middle, was taken by Russia in the century that followed. The frontier itself moved in the 1850s, when Russia took back the Amur. There has been a border between Russia and China across the middle of Asia ever since.

One more people was to test the new order. A branch of the western Mongols, the Torghuts, who were cousins of the Zunghars, had migrated west long before, in the 1600s, and settled on the Volga under Russian rule. By 1771 they were tired of the tsar's officials and of Russian farmers crowding their pastures, and they had heard that the old Zunghar grasslands stood empty. That winter about a hundred and seventy thousand of them set out for home, with their tents and herds, on a journey of three thousand kilometres. They were attacked the whole way by other herding peoples and pursued by Cossacks. Fewer than half arrived.

The Qing officials at the frontier met the survivors with grain, tea and tents. They then counted them, divided them into banners, and assigned each banner a stretch of pasture with fixed boundaries, which it was forbidden to leave.
