1. **Factual error; overbroad absolute — high confidence.** “Within six months he had abolished every institution that might have removed him.”

   Hindenburg remained president until August 1934, and the army retained independent power. Even the Enabling Act expressly preserved the president’s rights. The chapter’s later account of Hitler placating the generals and absorbing the presidency contradicts this opening. Say that he destroyed parliamentary democracy and organised political opposition within six months. [Enabling Act, full text](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-enabling-act)

2. **Doubtful causal claim — high confidence that the wording overstates what can be established.** “Its opponents could have stopped it at any point by combining.”

   This makes an uncertain counterfactual sound mechanically guaranteed. In November 1932 the Social Democrats and Communists together held 221 of 584 seats: more than the Nazis, but far short of a majority. Cooperation could have strengthened resistance; it would not automatically have prevented a presidential appointment or overcome the conservative parties’ hostility. “At any point” also extends the claim beyond the opportunities the chapter actually establishes. [Election results](https://www.kas.de/de/web/geschichte-der-cdu/statische-inhalte-detail/-/content/wahlen-zum-reichstag-in-der-weimarer-republik)

3. **Contradiction with chapter 77 — high confidence.** “The unemployed factory workers mostly did not, since they went to the Communists.”

   Chapter 77 ends: “In the country that the slump had hit hardest, six million unemployed had made their choice in the summer of 1932, at the ballot box.” In context, that means choosing Hitler. These accounts point in opposite directions. Chapter 79 is closer to the evidence: unemployed workers disproportionately supported the Communists. The earlier chapter needs to distinguish the Depression’s contribution to Nazi support from the voting behaviour of unemployed people. [Research on austerity and Nazi voting](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/gem/nazi_austerity.pdf)

4. **Misleading restriction; style absolute — high confidence.** “For one group of Germans there was no contentment to be had.”

   Introducing Jews this way implies that they were the sole excluded or persecuted group. Political prisoners have already appeared in this chapter; Roma and disabled people also faced persecution before the war. The July 1933 sterilisation law is a concrete counterexample. Introduce the Jews’ particular situation without implying exclusivity. [USHMM: forced sterilisation](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/forced-sterilization-a-form-of-nazi-persecution)

5. **Factual errors — high confidence.** “By 1939 seven households in ten had one, the highest share in the world.”

   “One” refers to a *people’s receiver*, but household radio-ownership figures cover all makes. The German Historical Museum gives approximately 65% ownership in **1941**; the German Historical Institute places approximately 70% in **1942**. Germany was not the world leader: American household ownership was already substantially higher. Correct the date, distinguish radios generally from this model, and remove the ranking. [German Historical Museum](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/kapitel/ns-regime/alltagsleben/volksempfaenger), [German Historical Institute](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/nazi-germany-1933-1945/radio-everywhere-advertisement-1935), [US Census](https://www2.census.gov/prod2/decennial/documents/36911485v2p1ch1.pdf)

6. **Incorrect comparison and doubtful technical claim — high confidence on price; moderate-to-high on the reception objection.** “One of the first acts of the regime was to commission a cheap radio set, the ‘people’s receiver’, which cost a week’s wages for a skilled man and could barely pick up foreign stations.”

   The original standard model cost 76 marks, roughly **two weeks’ wages for a well-paid worker**, not one. Foreign reception varied with location and conditions; the sets could receive foreign broadcasts, and research specifically challenges the familiar story of their deliberately restricted reception. “Could barely” needs more support than the chapter supplies. [University of Kassel historical study](https://kobra.uni-kassel.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/c46810c9-7209-4b45-895b-09ace7c81e3e/content), [Wolfgang König’s technical-history study](https://www.nomos-elibrary.de/10.5771/0040-117X-2003-2-73.pdf)

7. **Factual error; style absolute — high confidence.** “The army stood on French and Belgian soil, and no enemy had set foot in Germany.”

   Russian armies invaded East Prussia in 1914–15. The useful point is that Germany’s defeat in 1918 occurred without the enemy occupying its heartland, not that German territory had remained untouched throughout the war. As written, the narrator endorses part of the misleading picture being explained. [Alexander Watson’s research on the invasions](https://research.gold.ac.uk/11072/1/HIS_Watson2014.pdf)

8. **Factual overstatement — high confidence.** “The Decree for the Protection of People and State suspended, ‘until further notice’, every civil right in the constitution: freedom of speech, of the press and of assembly, the privacy of letters and telephone calls, and protection against arrest without charge.”

   It suspended seven specified constitutional articles, not every constitutional right. “Fundamental civil liberties” would carry the point accurately. [Text of the decree](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/reichstag-fire-decree)

9. **Reader loses the mechanism of the takeover.** “It passed by four hundred and forty-four votes to ninety-four.”

   The preceding election gave the Nazis only 44%, so a general reader needs to understand where this overwhelming legislative majority came from. Explain that the Enabling Act required a two-thirds majority and that the Catholic Centre and other non-Nazi parties supported it under intimidation and after assurances from Hitler. Otherwise, the decisive transition from minority electoral support to dictatorship happens inside an unexplained vote count. [Bundestag account](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/189778/d0f948962723d454c536d24d43965f87/enabling_act-data.pdf)

10. **Factual error — high confidence.** “At the start of 1929 about a million and a quarter Germans were unemployed.”

    Contemporary figures put unemployment in January 1929 at roughly **2.85 million**, with some series giving approximately three million at month’s end. The annual average was around 1.9 million. The sentence appears to attach a lower seasonal figure to the wrong time of year. [Contemporary US Bureau of Labor Statistics report](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/bls_mlr/bls_mlr_193201.pdf), [German Historical Museum](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/bestand/objekt/arbeitslose-in-deutschland-1921-1932)

11. **Doubtful attribution — high confidence that certainty is unwarranted.** “The most influential forgery of modern times, a pamphlet purporting to be the minutes of a secret meeting of Jewish leaders planning to take over the world, had been concocted by the tsar’s secret police around 1900.”

    The *Protocols of the Elders of Zion* appeared in Russia in 1903, but its authorship and the secret police’s alleged role remain disputed. Its fraudulent character is established; this particular origin story is not. Identify the pamphlet and give its first publication rather than asserting a settled author. [USHMM account of the document’s origins](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion)

12. **Misleading account of refugee policy — high confidence.** “One after another they expressed their sympathy and explained that their own countries, regrettably, could take no more.”

    The conference largely failed to expand admission sufficiently; it did not produce a universal refusal to admit further refugees. The United States announced that it would fill its existing German–Austrian quota of 27,370 annually. Distinguish refusing substantial additional places from accepting nobody else. [US National Archives: the American statement at Évian](https://docsteach.org/document/us-immigration-laws-submitted-to-evian-conference/)

13. **Factual error; misleading description — high confidence.** “On 30 June 1934 Hitler had the leader of the brownshirts and about a hundred others shot without trial, among them two generals and the widow of one.”

    The purge began on 30 June, but Ernst Röhm was killed on **1 July**. Elisabeth von Schleicher was attacked alongside her husband; describing her simply as a general’s “widow” misleadingly suggests a separate, subsequent killing. Spread the action across the purge’s several days and call her Schleicher’s wife. [USHMM: Röhm purge](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/roehm-purge)

14. **Factual overstatement; style absolute — high confidence.** “On 2 May, the morning after a national holiday in honour of labour, the brownshirts occupied the offices of every trade union in the country and confiscated their funds.”

    The 2 May operation targeted the socialist-oriented **Free Trade Unions**. Christian and liberal unions were brought into the Nazi labour organisation subsequently, by late June. “Every trade union” collapses separate stages into one day. [German Historical Museum](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/kapitel/ns-regime/etablierung-der-ns-herrschaft/zerschlagung-der-gewerkschaften)

15. **Factual error; unsafe “first” — high confidence.** “The first concentration camp, for political prisoners, had opened the day before in a disused factory at Dachau, near Munich.”

    Dachau opened on 22 March, but other Nazi camps preceded it, including Nohra earlier that month. Dachau became the enduring organisational model; it was not simply the first. USHMM’s specialist camp encyclopedia explicitly identifies Nohra as earlier. [USHMM, *Encyclopedia of Camps and Ghettos*, volume I](https://www.ushmm.org/online/camps-ghettos-download/EncyclopediaVol-I_PartA.pdf)

16. **Doubtful biographical certainty — moderate confidence.** “Hitler read them and believed them.”

    In context, this fixes Hitler’s adoption of racial antisemitism in his Vienna years. His exposure there is clear, but the timing and development of his radical antisemitism remain debated; his 1919 antisemitic letter supplies firmer evidence. State the exposure without presenting his later account of conversion as established fact. [USHMM: Hitler’s early years](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/adolf-hitler-early-years-1889-1921)

17. **Overcompressed persecution timeline — high confidence.** “In April Jews were dismissed from the civil service and the universities.”

    Dismissals began then, but the wording suggests a completed blanket expulsion. The April law initially exempted qualifying war veterans, certain longstanding officials, and people whose fathers or sons had died in the war. “The government began dismissing Jews…” would preserve the pace without erasing those exceptions. [USHMM: 1933 chronology](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/1933-key-dates)

18. **Factual error — very high confidence.** “The bill was four paragraphs long.”

    The Enabling Act contained **five articles**. [Bundestag account](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/189778/d0f948962723d454c536d24d43965f87/enabling_act-data.pdf)

19. **Reader loses an important institutional distinction.** “He called an election for 5 March, and now he had the police.”

    Becoming chancellor did not automatically give Hitler personal command of every German police force. The missing bridge is Göring’s control of the Prussian police and the recruitment of Nazi paramilitaries as auxiliary police. One sentence explaining that would make the subsequent arrests intelligible. [USHMM: Nazi rise to power](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-nazi-rise-to-power)

20. **Reader orientation.** “He burst into a Munich beer hall where the leaders of the state government were holding a meeting, fired a pistol at the ceiling, and announced a national revolution.”

    “State government” has not been distinguished from Germany’s national government. Identify Bavaria as the German state containing Munich. That also helps explain why capturing local leaders was intended as the beginning of a national seizure of power.

21. **Direct style violation: signposting.** “It is worth being exact about who voted for it.”

    This announces the forthcoming explanation instead of giving it. The next sentence already starts the explanation and can carry the transition.
