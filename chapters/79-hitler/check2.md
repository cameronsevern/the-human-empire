## Factual flags

1. **Minor — new erroneous “first.”**  
   > “The first attempt to use his following ended in gunfire.”

   Hitler had used his followers for organised political intimidation before November 1923. In September 1921, Nazis disrupted a meeting led by Otto Ballerstedt; Hitler subsequently served a prison sentence for his involvement. The putsch was his first attempt to seize state power, not his first use of his following. [Institute of Contemporary History, critical edition of *Mein Kampf*](https://www.mein-kampf-edition.de/Pref-Book/Pref-Book_Sec10.html)

2. **Major — wrong explanation of the parliamentary step.**  
   > “They still lacked a majority of their own, and so the next step had to be taken through parliament itself.”

   Lacking a Nazi-only majority did not make parliamentary action necessary: even a Nazi absolute majority would not have supplied the constitutional supermajority required for the Enabling Act. Moreover, Hitler’s coalition already possessed an ordinary majority—340 of 647 seats. The distinction is between governing with a parliamentary majority and obtaining extraordinary legislative powers through a two-thirds vote. [Contemporary US diplomatic report](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v02/d151), [German Bundestag](https://www.bundestag.de/dokumente/textarchiv/1933-03-23-ermaechtigungsgesetz-938540)

3. **Major — new claim contradicts the law itself.**  
   > “After that it went quickly, because nothing in the law stood in the way any longer.”

   The Enabling Act granted sweeping powers but expressly protected the president’s rights and the institutions of the Reichstag and Reichsrat. The regime subsequently violated or dismantled those remaining protections. “Nothing in the law” erases that distinction and also sits awkwardly with the later account of Hitler absorbing the presidency. [Enabling Act, Article 2](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-enabling-act)

4. **Minor — new sequencing contradicts earlier events in the chapter.**  
   > “Then the regime turned on the organised workers.”  
   > “Ideas came next.”

   These sentences make repression sound like successive campaigns beginning in May. Trade unionists were already being arrested before the March election, while the chapter itself has already described banned newspapers and the suspension of speech and press freedoms in February. May brought the destruction of independent unions and public book burnings; it did not begin repression of workers or ideas. [German Bundestag’s account of the takeover](https://www.bundestag.de/en/parliament/history/parliamentarism/third_reich/third_reich-200358)

5. **Major — new false division between legislation and violence.**  
   > “The persecution came in steps, each dressed up as law.”  
   > “Three years later the pretence of law was dropped.”

   Anti-Jewish persecution combined legislation, intimidation, and physical violence from 1933 onward. Conversely, discriminatory legislation continued after Kristallnacht, including further exclusions from education and public facilities. November 1938 marked a major escalation of violence, not a change from exclusively legal persecution to abandonment of legal forms. The chapter’s subsequent account of the government-imposed fine itself undermines the contrast. [USHMM: methods of persecution](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/question/how-did-the-nazis-and-their-collaborators-implement-the-holocaust), [USHMM: antisemitic legislation](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/antisemitic-legislation-1933-1939)

6. **Minor — existing error in the auxiliary police’s composition.**  
   > “One of the three Nazi ministers took charge of the police of Prussia, the state that covered three fifths of Germany, and enrolled fifty thousand brownshirts as auxiliary constables.”

   The approximately 50,000 auxiliaries included SA, SS, and Stahlhelm members, not 50,000 SA brownshirts. This is a separate problem from the earlier check’s request to explain control of the Prussian police, which the chapter has addressed. [German Historical Museum: Hermann Göring](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/biografie/hermann-goering)

## Clarity flags

- **Signposting:**  
  > “The votes did not come from where one might expect, however.”

  This announces a surprise instead of stating it. The next sentence supplies the substantive contrast. It breaches the prohibition on announcing an approaching point.

- **Unclear arithmetic:**  
  > “Meanwhile, its opponents never combined against it, although the numbers were there.”

  “The numbers” could mean more votes than the Nazis, a parliamentary majority, or enough strength to prevent Hitler’s appointment. The next sentence establishes only the first. This new wording makes the earlier correction less clear.

- **Overbroad recovery claim:**  
  > “All of that recovery rested on borrowed money.”

  “All” makes financing carry the entire explanation, including the preceding cultural revival in Berlin. The useful connection is Germany’s dependence on foreign credit.

- **Unnecessary absolutes:**  
  > “Nothing they had been told had prepared them for defeat.”  
  > “For a man who had failed at everything else, that mattered.”  
  > “The secret police, which everyone believed to be everywhere, was in fact small.”  
  > “By then every Jew in Germany who could get out wanted to.”

  “Nothing,” “everything else,” “everyone,” and “every Jew” claim universal knowledge or experience that the narrative does not need. These are precisely the exception-sensitive absolutes prohibited by the style sheet.

- **Repeated explanation:**  
  > “Instead, the court gave him a stage.”

  This immediately precedes the judges letting him “turn the dock into a platform.” The two theatrical metaphors make the same point.

  > “Even that he did not serve in full.”

  The next sentence—“He served nine months”—already makes this clear after the five-year sentence.

  > “The bill was five short paragraphs long, and its meaning was simple.”

  “Its meaning was simple” adds little before the clear explanation of its powers.

- **Abstract, awkward agency:**  
  > “Fear did the rest, and it needed fewer people than Germans imagined.”

  Fear does not literally need personnel. The next sentence reveals that the intended subject is the secret police, but the transition briefly obscures it.

- **Ambiguous reference:**  
  > “One of the writers burned was a German poet of the previous century…”

  This initially sounds as though the writer was burned. The objects burned were his books.

- **Missing identification:**  
  > “The bill was five short paragraphs long…”

  The chapter never names the Enabling Act. Giving this central measure its name would help readers distinguish it from the preceding emergency decree without introducing an incidental historical term.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions, or explicit chapter cross-references. The “work” and “pride” transitions state their points directly and do not require flagging merely because they organise the explanation.

## Overall

The revision improves paragraphing and orientation, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. Its main problems are new causal links and artificial sequences, especially around the Enabling Act and persecution. Keep the confident interpretation; correct those specific claims and trim the redundant setup sentences. No files were edited.
