1. **Wrong account of Malthus — high confidence.**  
   > “Then, in one country after another, something happened that Malthus had thought impossible. People chose to have fewer children.”

   Malthus explicitly recognised voluntary restraints on population, including postponing marriage because of the cost of raising children. This appears in the **1798 edition**, not merely his later revisions. His error was not believing that people could never limit births. [Malthus, *Essay*, chapter IV](https://dev.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4239/pg4239-images.html).

2. **Contradiction with chapter 67 — high confidence.**  
   > “In the middle of the nineteenth century, the cities of Europe and America learned that diseases were carried by germs in dirty water, and built sewers and waterworks.”

   This reverses an important part of the earlier story. Chapter 67 says that sanitary reformers acted under the theory of bad air, that Snow’s evidence was rejected, and that “London got its sewers a few years later for the wrong reason.” Distinguish sanitation improvements from the later acceptance of their microbial explanation. [Chapter 67](chapters/67-germs/chapter.md).

3. **Misleading comparison in the central claim — high confidence.**  
   > “Over the last two hundred years, and above all since 1950, clean water, vaccines and cheap medicines cut the deaths of children from nearly half to fewer than one in twenty-five.”

   The chapter subsequently identifies “nearly half” as deaths **before fifteen**, but the modern figure as deaths **before five**. Those are different measures. Use the same age boundary at both ends, or state the distinction. The historical synthesis and modern UN figures explicitly use these different ages. [Historical mortality](https://ourworldindata.org/child-mortality-in-the-past); [UNICEF mortality estimates](https://www.unicef.org/esa/press-releases/global-child-deaths-reach-historic-low-2022-un-report).

4. **Two seed shipments conflated — high confidence.**  
   > “In 1966 India had imported eighteen thousand tons of Borlaug's seed, the largest purchase of seed in history, part of which arrived late because of riots at the American port and a war at the Indian end.”

   The Watts-riot and war difficulties concerned the **1965 shipment**, not the much larger 1966 purchase. Borlaug’s own account dates the troubled shipment to 1965; his Nobel lecture distinguishes the two imports. [Borlaug’s 1968 account](https://www.bicga.org.uk/docs/borlaug1968_wheat_breeding.pdf); [Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/1970/borlaug/lecture/).

5. **False “first” — high confidence; also a style violation.**  
   > “For the first time, a woman could decide when to have children without asking a man to cooperate.”

   Diaphragms and other methods controlled by women preceded the pill. The pill greatly improved convenience and reliability, but did not inaugurate contraception independent of male cooperation. This is precisely an absolute broken by an exception. [PBS’s contraceptive timeline](https://cgi.pbs.org/wgbh/amex/pill/timeline/index.html).

6. **Incomplete list presented as exhaustive — high confidence; tension with chapter 72.**  
   > “Until the twentieth century the only sources of usable nitrogen were animal dung, certain crops such as beans, whose roots harbour bacteria that capture it, and deposits of bird droppings shipped from islands off Peru.”

   This omits **mineral sodium nitrate from Chile**, a major nineteenth-century agricultural fertiliser. Chapter 72 has already introduced Chilean nitrate in the explosives story, so attentive readers may notice its disappearance. Replace the exhaustive claim with an accurate account of the principal sources. [Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/dirty-business/); [chapter 72](chapters/72-trenches/chapter.md).

7. **Oral-rehydration story overstates the disappearance of intravenous treatment — high confidence.**  
   > “A young Indian doctor in charge of one of them ran out of drip bottles.”

   The original report describes **limited supplies**, with severe cases initially receiving intravenous fluids and oral treatment conserving those supplies. The subsequent 3.6% mortality figure covers this combined treatment programme, not an entirely oral replacement after drips ran out. Preserve that distinction in the story. [Mahalanabis and colleagues’ original report](https://pure.johnshopkins.edu/en/publications/oral-fluid-therapy-of-cholera-among-bangladesh-refugees-5/).

8. **Unsupported ranking of causes — moderate-to-high confidence.**  
   > “The strongest single influence is the schooling of girls.”

   Girls’ education matters, but research does not establish it as the universally strongest cause of fertility decline. Its effects vary with contraceptive access, social circumstances and the outcome being measured. A systematic review found causal effects less consistent than simple correlations suggest. The problem is the superlative, not the chapter’s support for education. [Systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6582211/).

9. **False absolute about family policy — high confidence.**  
   > “Governments have offered cash, housing and long paid leave to parents, and none has found a way of bringing the birth rate back up.”

   Raising fertility is different from restoring it permanently to replacement level. The OECD finds that Hungary’s support package likely contributed to an increase in fertility, although it remained low. The defensible claim concerns the difficulty of securing a sustained return to replacement, not the impossibility of any increase. [OECD, *Society at a Glance 2024*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/society-at-a-glance-2024_918d8db3-en/full-report/fertility-trends-across-the-oecd-underlying-drivers-and-the-role-for-policy_770679b8.html).

10. **Replacement fertility is misdefined — high confidence.**  
    > “Two thirds of humanity now live in countries where women have fewer than the two children needed to keep the population level.”

    The cited demographic threshold is **about 2.1 children per woman**, not two, and describes long-run replacement without migration. Population can continue growing below that threshold because many young people are entering their childbearing years. This distinction matters to the chapter’s explanation of continuing growth. [UN demographic policy brief](https://desapublications.un.org/policy-briefs/un-desa-policy-brief-no-172-new-landscape-fertility-and-family-planning-30-years).

11. **Misleading chronology around dwarf wheat — high confidence.**  
    > “Mexico, which had imported half its wheat, was feeding itself by 1956.”

    The date is sound **for wheat self-sufficiency**, but its position immediately after the completed dwarf-wheat breeding story implies that those varieties produced the 1956 achievement. Borlaug’s earlier improved varieties helped achieve self-sufficiency; the first Mexican semidwarf varieties were released in **1962**. Also, “feeding itself” broadens wheat self-sufficiency into food self-sufficiency. [Borlaug’s Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/1970/borlaug/lecture/); [CIMMYT’s breeding history](https://repository.cimmyt.org/bitstreams/1468b2fd-df13-4970-a7d6-495c8d9b3222/download).

12. **Historical average turned into a universal — high confidence; also a style violation.**  
    > “In every society for which there are records before 1800, in Roman Egypt, imperial China, medieval England, about a quarter of all babies died before their first birthday, and close to half before they were fifteen.”

    The evidence supports very high mortality across many societies, not these rates in **every recorded society**. The synthesis itself describes incomplete historical snapshots and gives differing estimates—for example, about 40% dying before fifteen in eighteenth-century Sweden. “Across many societies” would retain the force without claiming exhaustive uniformity. [Historical evidence and its limitations](https://ourworldindata.org/child-mortality-in-the-past).

13. **Hunger figure outdated for the stated 2025 endpoint — high confidence.**  
    > “More than seven hundred million people still do not get enough to eat, in a world that grows more than enough, because they are too poor to buy it or because someone is fighting a war around them.”

    The report available in 2025 estimated **673 million** undernourished people in 2024, with an uncertainty range of 638–720 million. “More than seven hundred million” should not be presented as the central estimate. Date the figure. [FAO, *The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2025*](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/cfs/CFS53/Plenary_documents/Inf.15_SOFI_Report/EN_compiled.pdf).

14. **Water measures change without explanation — high confidence; reader-comprehension problem.**  
    > “A kilogram of beef takes about fifteen thousand litres of water to produce.”

    The figure is a global **water footprint**, predominantly rainwater used in growing feed, with an additional pollution-related component. It is not fifteen thousand litres withdrawn from rivers and wells—the measure introduced earlier in the paragraph. Explain the change so readers do not mistake it for irrigation demand. [Water Footprint Network](https://www.waterfootprint.org/water-footprint-2/what-is-a-water-footprint/).

15. **The ending misstates what was measured — high confidence.**  
    > “In 2018 three scientists added up the weight of every kind of living thing on Earth.”

    They compiled **estimates of biomass expressed as carbon mass**, not ordinary body weight. The mammal percentages are recognisable rounded results from that study, but readers need the measurement explained. Also, its bird comparison concerns **domesticated poultry**, not exclusively chickens in sheds. [Original biomass study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1711842115).

16. **Bangladesh’s transition is compressed too far — moderate-to-high confidence.**  
    > “The average number of children there fell from seven to two in a generation.”

    UNICEF documents a fall from **6.3 in 1975 to 2.3 in 2019**. Different series vary somewhat, but this is roughly four decades, not an ordinary generation. “Over four decades” would preserve the achievement without accelerating it. [UNICEF demographic account](https://www.unicef.org/bangladesh/en/media/5086/file/brochure_A4_Demographic_web_final.pdf.pdf.pdf).

17. **Missing explanation of continuing growth — reader-comprehension problem.**  
    > “The United Nations expects the human total to reach about ten billion three hundred million in the 2080s, and then to begin to fall.”

    The forecast is consistent with the UN’s 2024 projection, but readers have just learned that most people live in below-replacement countries and may not understand another sixty years of growth. Explain that large generations already born will produce many births even when each woman has fewer children. The earlier “gap” explains the initial acceleration but does not clearly explain this continuing momentum. [UN population projections](https://population.un.org/wpp/assets/Files/WPP2024_Summary-of-Results.pdf).

18. **An unexplained mechanism — reader-comprehension and plain-language problem.**  
    > “Fertiliser washing off the fields has left dead zones at the mouths of the world's great rivers.”

    “Dead zones” is introduced without explaining what dies or why. One short causal chain is needed: excess nutrients encourage blooms; decomposition consumes oxygen; fish and other animals cannot survive in the depleted water. That would satisfy the style guide’s instruction to explain a mechanism in the order it happens.
