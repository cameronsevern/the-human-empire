# 110. How did there get to be eight billion of us?

*The world, from 1798 to 2025*

There are eight billion of us because people stopped dying young. Over the last two hundred years, and above all since 1950, clean water, vaccines and cheap medicines cut the share of children who die from nearly half to about one in twenty-five. Parents went on having many children for a generation or two after that, and the population multiplied in the gap. Then births fell as well, and the growth is now coming to an end.

At twenty-nine minutes past one on the morning of 15 November 2022, a girl was born in a public hospital in Manila, in the Philippines. Officials of the country's population commission photographed her in her mother's arms, and announced that she had been chosen to stand for the eight billionth human being alive. The United Nations had picked that day from its estimates. The real eight billionth child was born somewhere unknown, at about that time, and nobody could have said which one it was.

The ceremony was a piece of theatre, but the number behind it was real, and so was the speed at which it had been reached. It had taken the whole of human existence, until about 1804, for the number of people on Earth to reach one billion. The second billion took a hundred and twenty-three years. The eighth took eleven. The wait between one billion and the next had shrunk from almost the whole of history to a little over a decade.

## Half the children

For most of history, the number of people hardly changed from one century to the next.

That was not because few were born. A woman who lived through her childbearing years typically had six or seven children, enough to replace herself and her husband several times over. The numbers stayed flat for a grimmer reason. The children died.

In the societies for which there are records before 1800, from Roman Egypt to imperial China and medieval England, about a quarter of all babies died before their first birthday, and close to half before they were fifteen. They died of diarrhoea, measles, smallpox, pneumonia and fevers that nobody could name. A family buried as many children as it raised.

So many early deaths make the old figures for lifespan easy to misread. The average length of a life, counting all those infants, was about thirty years. That figure does not mean that grown men and women usually died at thirty. It is pulled down by the great number of children who never grew up at all.

Births and deaths therefore ran level. A run of good harvests might let numbers rise for a while. Sooner or later, however, a famine or a plague brought them down again.

Over the very long run, the total grew only slowly. The world of 1700 held about six hundred million people, perhaps three times as many as the world of the Roman emperors seventeen centuries before. That sounds like a great deal of growth until it is spread across seventeen centuries.

## A parson's arithmetic

To the people living inside this pattern, it seemed simply the way the world worked. In 1798 an English clergyman named Thomas Malthus set this out as a law.

People, he observed, multiply. Two parents can have four children, who can have eight, and so on, doubling every generation. Food cannot be made to grow like that. A farmer may add a field, and then another, but the land is limited, and each new field adds only so much. Sooner or later, then, the mouths must outrun the harvest.

Population would therefore always press against the supply of food, and be held down by hunger, disease and war. Worse still, any improvement in the lot of the poor would only produce more of them, until they were as poor as before.

It was a fair description of the previous five thousand years. Again and again, numbers had risen in good times and been cut back by hunger and plague. It was published at almost the exact moment when it stopped being true.

## A pinch of salt

The pattern broke in two places. Death began to retreat, and food began to multiply.

The retreat of death began with the simplest things. In the middle of the nineteenth century, the cities of Europe and America built sewers and waterworks. They did the right thing for the wrong reason. At first they believed that foul smells caused disease, and they learned only afterwards that the cause was germs in dirty water.

Clean pipes were a start, yet the water running through them could still carry germs. In 1908 a town in New Jersey became the first in America to add a little chlorine to its drinking water. Chlorine kills the germs outright. The method was simple, and within ten years most large cities had copied it. As a result, deaths from typhoid and from the diarrhoea that killed infants fell away.

![Black and white Punch cartoon: a woman representing London recoils as a gaunt, dripping figure of the River Thames presents three thin children labelled diphtheria, scrofula and cholera, with factory chimneys behind](images/thames.jpg)
*"Father Thames introducing his offspring to the fair city of London", a cartoon in the magazine Punch in 1858, the summer of the Great Stink. The river, thick with sewage, presents diphtheria, scrofula and cholera. Image: John Leech, public domain.*

Then came the needle. A vaccine teaches the body to recognise a disease before meeting it, so that when the real infection arrives, the body is ready for it. There had been one, for smallpox, since the 1790s, and one for rabies since the 1880s. For most of the diseases that killed children, however, there was still no vaccine.

That changed in the twentieth century, when vaccines were made for diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus, polio and measles. After 1974 a worldwide programme set out to give them to every child, in poor countries as well as rich ones. By one careful estimate those vaccines have saved a hundred and fifty-four million lives in the fifty years since, most of them babies.

![A woman in a patterned dress holds a baby and puts drops of vaccine into its mouth, while a crowd of children and an older man look on](images/polio.jpg)
*A health worker gives oral polio vaccine drops to a baby in a farming community in Adamawa, Nigeria, in 2014. Photo: CDC Global, CC BY 2.0.*

After 1945 there were also antibiotics, which cured the infections that vaccines had not prevented. Meanwhile, insecticides cleared the mosquitoes carrying malaria from whole countries.

One of the greatest of these advances cost almost nothing and is hardly known. Cholera and other diarrhoeas kill by draining the body of water and salt, sometimes within hours. The patient, in effect, dries out. For a century the only treatment was to drip salt solution into a vein, which needs sterile equipment and a trained nurse. Wherever those were scarce, the treatment was scarce too.

In the 1960s researchers in Dhaka and Calcutta found a way round the problem. If sugar is dissolved in the water with the salt, the gut will absorb the mixture even while the disease is raging. The patient, therefore, can simply drink it, with no needle and no nurse.

The idea was soon put to a hard test. In 1971 a war drove ten million refugees across the border from East Pakistan into India, and cholera broke out in the camps. A young Indian doctor in charge of one of them had drip bottles enough only for the worst cases. To stretch them, he had his staff mix salt and sugar in drums of water, and told the families to keep giving it by the cupful.

The difference showed in the death counts. In the neighbouring camps nearly a third of the cholera patients were dying. In his, the rate fell below one in twenty-five.

Since then the drink has become one of the humblest tools in medicine. A sachet of the mixture now costs a few cents, and a mother can give it at home. It is reckoned to have saved more than fifty million lives.

Each of these advances saved lives on its own. Taken together, the effect has no parallel. In 1950 about one child in five in the world died before the age of five. Today it is fewer than one in twenty-five. In 2022, for the first time since records began, the number of small children who died in a year fell below five million. Meanwhile the average human life has lengthened from about thirty-two years in 1900 to seventy-three.

## Bread from air

More living children meant more mouths, and Malthus had said that the land could not feed them. He was wrong because of a discovery that he could not have foreseen.

Plants need nitrogen to grow. Four fifths of the air is nitrogen, but in a form that plants cannot use. Every field, in effect, sat beneath an ocean of the stuff and went hungry for it.

Until the twentieth century, farmers had only a few sources of usable nitrogen. The main ones were animal dung, and certain crops such as beans, whose roots harbour bacteria that capture it. Beyond those, there were bird droppings and mineral salts shipped from Peru and Chile. The yield of the world's fields was limited by nitrogen.

In 1909 a German chemist found a way to combine the nitrogen of the air with hydrogen, under great pressure and heat, to make ammonia. Fertiliser is easily made from ammonia, so in effect he had learned to pull plant food out of the sky. A German chemical company built the first factory four years later.

The invention had a dark side from the start. The process was soon used for explosives, and its inventor went on to organise poison gas. Even so, it also removed the oldest limit on the growing of food.

The world's farms now receive more nitrogen from factories than from all natural sources together. About half the people alive today are fed by crops that could not have been grown without it. In fact, about half the nitrogen in the body of anyone reading this came out of such a factory.

## A short stalk

Fertiliser by itself was not enough. The old varieties of wheat and rice were tall and thin-stemmed. Given plenty of nitrogen, they grew heavy heads of grain and promptly fell over and rotted. The extra food ended up in the mud. What the farmer needed was a plant built to carry the weight.

Norman Borlaug was a farm boy from Iowa who had seen the dust storms of the Depression and trained as a plant scientist. In 1944 he went to Mexico, on a programme paid for by an American foundation, to work on wheat.

The work was slow and fiddly. For twenty years he crossed thousands of varieties by hand, transferring pollen with tweezers. To go faster, he grew two generations a year, by planting in the hot lowlands in winter and the cool highlands in summer, which the textbooks said could not be done. Every year, as a result, did the work of two.

He ended with a wheat that was short and thick-stemmed. It could carry a heavy head without falling, it resisted the main diseases, and it yielded two or three times as much. Even before that, his earlier varieties had already made Mexico, which had imported half its wheat, able to grow all that it needed by 1956.

*Norman Borlaug in 2004, at the age of ninety, sixty years after he began breeding wheat in Mexico. Photo: Ben Zinner, USAID, public domain.*

The real test came on the other side of the world. In the mid-1960s India and Pakistan were living from one shipload of American grain to the next. Then the monsoon failed two years running, and experts in the West wrote the subcontinent off. A book published in America in 1968 opened with the statement that the battle to feed all of humanity was over, and that in the 1970s hundreds of millions of people would starve to death whatever was done.

By the time that book appeared, the answer was already in the ground. In 1965 a first shipment of Borlaug's seed reached India late. It had been held up by riots at the American port and a war at the Indian end, and it was sown anyway. In 1966 India went much further, and imported eighteen thousand tons more, the largest purchase of seed in history.

The harvests that followed were unlike anything before them. Two years later the harvest was so large that schools in Punjab were closed to store the grain. India's wheat crop went from twelve million tons to twenty million in five years, and stands at more than a hundred million now. Meanwhile a dwarf rice bred in the Philippines on the same principle did as much for the rest of Asia.

The mass famines did not come. Borlaug was given the Nobel peace prize in 1970, and is often said to have saved a billion lives.

It was not free. The new seeds needed fertiliser, pesticides and above all water. Much of that water came from wells, and in the wheat lands of northern India the level of the groundwater has been sinking by up to a metre a year.

The fertiliser does harm further away. Washing off the fields, it feeds great blooms of algae at the mouths of the world's rivers. When the algae rot they use up the oxygen in the water, so that fish cannot live there. Closer to home, small farmers who could not afford the outlay fell into debt.

Nor did hunger end. Nearly seven hundred million people still do not get enough to eat, in a world that grows more than enough, because they are too poor to buy it or because someone is fighting a war around them. The difficulty is no longer how much food the world can grow, but who can reach it.

## The gap

The population explosion of the twentieth century was a matter of timing.

Deaths had fallen, and in time births would fall too. Much depended on how long the second fall lagged behind the first, and on how wide the gap between them grew.

When the death rate falls in a society, the birth rate does not fall with it at once. For a generation or two, families go on having the six or seven children that their grandparents had, and now five or six of them live. Each of those then has children of their own, and so the numbers multiply.

How fast this happened depended on how fast death retreated. In Europe this gap opened slowly in the nineteenth century, and the surplus emigrated to the Americas. Elsewhere it came all at once. In Asia, Africa and Latin America, the vaccines and the antibiotics arrived all together after 1945. As a result, death rates there fell in twenty years by as much as they had in two centuries in Europe.

The world's population grew faster in the 1960s than at any time before or since, by two per cent a year. That sounds modest. At that rate, however, a population doubles in thirty-five years.

## Choosing

Then, in one country after another, something happened that Malthus had not expected of the poor. People chose to have far fewer children.

It follows, with a delay, from the fall in deaths. When parents can be confident that their children will live, they do not need spares. In a world where nearly half of all children died, a large family was a way to improve the odds of raising a few. Once that danger passed, one reason for the large family went with it.

Money pointed the same way. When children go to school and do not earn their keep in the fields, each one becomes a cost rather than a help.

One of the strongest influences is the schooling of girls. A woman who has been to secondary school marries later, knows more about her own body, and has more say in her household. She also has other things that she might do with her life.

Wanting fewer children, however, is not enough on its own. She also needs the means. Until the middle of the twentieth century these were crude and unreliable, and in many countries illegal.

In 1916 an American nurse named Margaret Sanger, who had watched her own mother worn out by eighteen pregnancies, opened a clinic in a poor district of New York to give advice on preventing them. The police closed it after ten days and she went to jail.

Her next great step came decades later. Thirty-five years later, at a dinner party, she asked a biologist whether a pill could be made that a woman could swallow. A widow who had inherited a farm-machinery fortune paid for nearly all of the research out of her own pocket.

The pill uses man-made versions of the body's own hormones to prevent the release of an egg, so that there is nothing to fertilise. Its testing was a scandal of its own: it was tried out on poor women in Puerto Rico who were not told that it was an experiment. It went on sale in America in 1960. For the first time, a woman had a reliable and private way to decide when to have children. Within five years six million American women were taking it.

In the 1960s the average woman in the world had five children. Today she has a little over two, which is close to the number at which a population only replaces itself.

Some governments tried to hurry this by force. China limited most couples to one child. In India, during a period of emergency rule in 1976, officials were given quotas. To meet them, more than six million men were sterilised in a year, many of them rounded up from villages and slums. In the end the policy brought down the government.

The countries that did best used persuasion instead. Bangladesh looked an unlikely place to succeed: it was one of the poorest places on Earth and a conservative Muslim society. It trained tens of thousands of village women to go from door to door. They talked to wives in their own homes, and left a supply of pills. The average number of children there fell from more than six to little more than two in forty years. Iran moved faster still. There it fell from six and a half to two in fifteen years.

## Below two

The result has surprised the people who study it. Two thirds of humanity now live in countries where women have fewer than the two children, or slightly more, needed to keep the population level in the long run. That includes all of Europe, China, Brazil, Iran, and India. Most of humanity, in other words, is already having too few children to replace itself.

In some the fall has gone a long way further. The average woman in South Korea now has fewer than one child. At that rate each generation is less than half the size of the one before it. Japan's population has been shrinking since 2008, and it sells more nappies for the old than for babies. China's began to shrink in 2022.

In such countries there are villages with no children, schools turned into nursing homes, and too few workers to support the retired. Governments have offered cash, housing and long paid leave to parents. None has found a way of bringing the birth rate back up to the level at which a population replaces itself.

The growth that remains is mostly in Africa south of the Sahara. There the average is still more than four children, and the gap between deaths and births is at its widest. As a result, Nigeria is on course to pass the United States. By the middle of the century one person in four will be African.

The United Nations expects the human total to reach about ten billion three hundred million in the 2080s, and then to begin to fall. It may seem odd that the total keeps rising when most families are already small. It goes on rising until then because the large generations already born have yet to have their own, smaller, families. A child born today will probably live to see the peak.

## A plate

Keeping eight billion people fed has reshaped the surface of the planet.

Half of all the land on Earth that is not ice or desert is now used for farming, most of it as pasture or to grow feed for animals.

Farming takes seven tenths of all the fresh water that human beings draw from rivers and wells. It also runs on fossil fuel: for the fertiliser, the tractors, the pumps, the lorries and the refrigeration. In a rich country, something like ten units of energy from oil and gas go into each unit of food energy that reaches a plate.

Raising animals adds a further step, because the animal has to be fed before it can feed anyone. A kilogram of beef takes about fifteen thousand litres of water to produce, most of it the rain that fell on the grass and grain that the animal ate.

![Aerial view of a flat landscape divided into squares and many dark green and tan circles, each a field watered by a rotating sprinkler arm](images/pivot.jpg)
*Fields watered by centre-pivot irrigation, seen from the air in 2009. Each circle is the reach of one sprinkler arm turning around a well. Photo: John A. Kelley, USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service, public domain.*

In 2018 three scientists estimated the weight of every kind of living thing on Earth, measured by the carbon in it. Among the mammals, they found, human beings make up about a third of the total. The cattle, pigs, sheep and other animals that we raise make up about three fifths. All the wild mammals in the world together, every elephant, whale, deer, bat and mouse, come to four per cent. Among birds, farmed poultry outweigh all the wild birds by more than two to one.
