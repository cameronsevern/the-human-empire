# 110. How did there get to be eight billion of us?

*The world, from 1798 to 2025*

There are eight billion of us because people stopped dying young. Over the last two hundred years, and above all since 1950, clean water, vaccines and cheap medicines cut the deaths of children from nearly half to fewer than one in twenty-five. Parents went on having many children for a generation or two after that, and the population multiplied in the gap. Then births fell as well, and the growth is now coming to an end.

At twenty-nine minutes past one on the morning of 15 November 2022, a girl was born in a public hospital in Manila, in the Philippines. The nurses had a small cake ready. Officials of the country's population commission photographed her in her mother's arms, and announced that she had been chosen to stand for the eight billionth human being alive. The United Nations had picked that day from its estimates. The real eight billionth child was born somewhere unknown, at about that time, and nobody could have said which one it was.

It had taken the whole of human existence, until about 1804, for the number of people on Earth to reach one billion. The second billion took a hundred and twenty-three years. The eighth took eleven.

## Half the children

For most of history, the number of people hardly changed from one century to the next.

That was not because few were born. A woman who lived through her childbearing years typically had six or seven children. It was because they died. In every society for which there are records before 1800, in Roman Egypt, imperial China, medieval England, about a quarter of all babies died before their first birthday, and close to half before they were fifteen. They died of diarrhoea, measles, smallpox, pneumonia and fevers that nobody could name. A family buried as many children as it raised. The average length of a life, counting all those infants, was about thirty years.

Births and deaths therefore ran level, and when a run of good harvests let numbers rise, a famine or a plague brought them down again. The world of 1700 held about six hundred million people, perhaps three times as many as the world of the Roman emperors seventeen centuries before.

## A parson's arithmetic

In 1798 an English clergyman named Thomas Malthus set this out as a law. People, he observed, multiply. Two parents can have four children, who can have eight, and so on, doubling every generation. Food cannot be made to grow like that. A farmer may add a field, and then another, but the land is limited. Population would therefore always press against the supply of food, and be held down by hunger, disease and war. Any improvement in the lot of the poor would only produce more of them, until they were as poor as before.

It was a fair description of the previous five thousand years. It was published at almost the exact moment when it stopped being true.

## A pinch of salt

The retreat of death began with the simplest things. In the middle of the nineteenth century, the cities of Europe and America learned that diseases were carried by germs in dirty water, and built sewers and waterworks. In 1908 a town in New Jersey became the first in America to add a little chlorine to its drinking water, which kills the germs outright, and within ten years most large cities had copied it. Deaths from typhoid and from the diarrhoea that killed infants fell away.

Then came the needle. A vaccine teaches the body to recognise a disease before meeting it. There had been one, for smallpox, since the 1790s, and one for rabies since the 1880s. In the twentieth century they were made for diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus, polio and measles, and after 1974 a worldwide programme set out to give them to every child. By one careful estimate those vaccines have saved a hundred and fifty-four million lives in the fifty years since, most of them babies.

After 1945 there were also antibiotics, which cured the infections that vaccines had not prevented, and insecticides that cleared the mosquitoes carrying malaria from whole countries.

One of the greatest of these advances cost almost nothing and is hardly known. Cholera and other diarrhoeas kill by draining the body of water and salt, sometimes within hours. For a century the only treatment was to drip salt solution into a vein, which needs sterile equipment and a trained nurse. In the 1960s researchers in Dhaka and Calcutta found that if sugar is dissolved in the water with the salt, the gut will absorb the mixture even while the disease is raging, and the patient can simply drink it.

In 1971 a war drove ten million refugees across the border from East Pakistan into India, and cholera broke out in the camps. A young Indian doctor in charge of one of them ran out of drip bottles. He had his staff mix salt and sugar in drums of water and told the families to keep giving it by the cupful. In the neighbouring camps a third of the cholera patients were dying. In his, the rate fell below one in twenty-five. A sachet of the mixture now costs a few cents, a mother can give it at home, and it is reckoned to have saved more than fifty million children.

Taken together, the effect has no parallel. In 1950 about one child in five in the world died before the age of five. Today it is fewer than one in twenty-five. In 2022, for the first time since records began, the number of small children who died in a year fell below five million. The average human life has lengthened from about thirty-two years in 1900 to seventy-three.

## Bread from air

More living children meant more mouths, and Malthus had said that the land could not feed them. He was wrong because of a discovery that he could not have foreseen.

Plants need nitrogen to grow. Four fifths of the air is nitrogen, but in a form that plants cannot use. Until the twentieth century the only sources of usable nitrogen were animal dung, certain crops such as beans, whose roots harbour bacteria that capture it, and deposits of bird droppings shipped from islands off Peru. The yield of every field in the world was limited by it.

In 1909 a German chemist found a way to combine the nitrogen of the air with hydrogen, under great pressure and heat, to make ammonia, from which fertiliser is easily made. A German chemical company built the first factory four years later. The process was soon used for explosives, and its inventor went on to organise poison gas. It also removed the oldest limit on the growing of food. The world's farms now receive more nitrogen from factories than from all natural sources together. About half the people alive today are fed by crops that could not have been grown without it, and about half the nitrogen in the body of anyone reading this came out of such a factory.

## A short stalk

Fertiliser by itself was not enough. The old varieties of wheat and rice were tall and thin-stemmed. Given plenty of nitrogen, they grew heavy heads of grain and promptly fell over and rotted.

Norman Borlaug was a farm boy from Iowa who had seen the dust storms of the Depression and trained as a plant scientist. In 1944 he went to Mexico, on a programme paid for by an American foundation, to work on wheat. For twenty years he crossed thousands of varieties by hand, transferring pollen with tweezers. He grew two generations a year by planting in the hot lowlands in winter and the cool highlands in summer, which the textbooks said could not be done. He ended with a wheat that was short and thick-stemmed, could carry a heavy head without falling, resisted the main diseases, and yielded three times as much. Mexico, which had imported half its wheat, was feeding itself by 1956.

In the mid-1960s India and Pakistan were living from one shipload of American grain to the next. The monsoon failed two years running, and experts in the West wrote the subcontinent off. A book published in America in 1968 opened with the statement that the battle to feed all of humanity was over, and that in the 1970s hundreds of millions of people would starve to death whatever was done.

In 1966 India had imported eighteen thousand tons of Borlaug's seed, the largest purchase of seed in history, part of which arrived late because of riots at the American port and a war at the Indian end. It was sown anyway. Two years later the harvest was so large that schools in Punjab were closed to store the grain. India's wheat crop went from twelve million tons to twenty million in five years, and stands at more than a hundred million now. A dwarf rice bred in the Philippines on the same principle did as much for the rest of Asia. The mass famines did not come. Borlaug was given the Nobel peace prize in 1970, and is often said to have saved a billion lives.

It was not free. The new seeds needed fertiliser, pesticides and above all water, and in the wheat lands of northern India the level of the groundwater has been sinking by a metre a year. Fertiliser washing off the fields has left dead zones at the mouths of the world's great rivers. Small farmers who could not afford the outlay fell into debt. And hunger did not end. More than seven hundred million people still do not get enough to eat, in a world that grows more than enough, because they are too poor to buy it or because someone is fighting a war around them.

## The gap

The population explosion of the twentieth century was a matter of timing.

When the death rate falls in a society, the birth rate does not fall with it at once. For a generation or two, families go on having the six or seven children that their grandparents had, and now five or six of them live. Each of those then has children of their own. In Europe this gap opened slowly in the nineteenth century, and the surplus emigrated to the Americas. In Asia, Africa and Latin America, where the vaccines and the antibiotics arrived all together after 1945, death rates fell in twenty years by as much as they had in two centuries in Europe. The world's population grew faster in the 1960s than at any time before or since, by two per cent a year, which doubles it in thirty-five years.

## Choosing

Then, in one country after another, something happened that Malthus had thought impossible. People chose to have fewer children.

It follows, with a delay, from the fall in deaths. When parents can be confident that their children will live, they do not need spares. When children go to school and do not earn their keep in the fields, each one becomes a cost. The strongest single influence is the schooling of girls. A woman who has been to secondary school marries later, knows more about her own body, has more say in her household, and has other things that she might do with her life.

She also needs the means. Until the middle of the twentieth century these were crude and unreliable, and in many countries illegal. In 1916 an American nurse named Margaret Sanger, who had watched her own mother worn out by eighteen pregnancies, opened a clinic in a poor district of New York to give advice on preventing them. The police closed it after ten days and she went to jail. Thirty-five years later, at a dinner party, she asked a biologist whether a pill could be made that a woman could swallow. A widow who had inherited a farm-machinery fortune paid for nearly all of the research out of her own pocket. The pill, which uses the body's own hormones to prevent the release of an egg, was tested on poor women in Puerto Rico who were not told that it was an experiment, and it went on sale in America in 1960. For the first time, a woman could decide when to have children without asking a man to cooperate. Within five years six million American women were taking it.

In the 1960s the average woman in the world had five children. Today she has a little over two, which is close to the number at which a population only replaces itself.

Some governments tried to hurry this by force. China limited most couples to one child. In India, during a period of emergency rule in 1976, officials were given quotas, and more than six million men were sterilised in a year, many of them rounded up from villages and slums. The policy brought down the government.

The countries that did best used persuasion. Bangladesh, one of the poorest places on Earth and a conservative Muslim society, trained tens of thousands of village women to go from door to door, talk to wives in their own homes, and leave a supply of pills. The average number of children there fell from seven to two in a generation. In Iran it fell from six and a half to two in fifteen years.

## Below two

The result has surprised the people who study it. Two thirds of humanity now live in countries where women have fewer than the two children needed to keep the population level. That includes all of Europe, China, Brazil, Iran, and India.

In some the fall has gone a long way further. The average woman in South Korea now has fewer than one child. Japan's population has been shrinking since 2008, and it sells more nappies for the old than for babies. China's began to shrink in 2022. In such countries there are villages with no children, schools turned into nursing homes, and too few workers to support the retired. Governments have offered cash, housing and long paid leave to parents, and none has found a way of bringing the birth rate back up.

The growth that remains is mostly in Africa south of the Sahara, where the average is still more than four children and the gap between deaths and births is at its widest. Nigeria is on course to pass the United States. By the middle of the century one person in four will be African.

The United Nations expects the human total to reach about ten billion three hundred million in the 2080s, and then to begin to fall. A child born today will probably live to see the peak.

## A plate

Keeping eight billion people fed has reshaped the surface of the planet.

Half of all the land on Earth that is not ice or desert is now used for farming, most of it as pasture or to grow feed for animals. Farming takes seven tenths of all the fresh water that human beings draw from rivers and wells. It runs on fossil fuel: for the fertiliser, the tractors, the pumps, the lorries and the refrigeration. In a rich country, something like ten units of energy from oil and gas go into each unit of food energy that reaches a plate. A kilogram of beef takes about fifteen thousand litres of water to produce.

In 2018 three scientists added up the weight of every kind of living thing on Earth. Among the mammals, they found, human beings make up about a third of the total. The cattle, pigs, sheep and other animals that we raise make up nearly two thirds. All the wild mammals in the world together, every elephant, whale, deer, bat and mouse, come to four per cent. Among birds, the chickens in farm sheds outweigh all the wild birds by more than two to one.
