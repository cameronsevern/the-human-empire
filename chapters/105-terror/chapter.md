# 105. What did 9/11 change?

*The United States and the Middle East, from 2001 to 2025*

Nineteen men armed with knives killed three thousand people in a single morning, and the most powerful country in the world answered with twenty years of war. It overthrew two governments, one that had sheltered the attackers and one that had not, and in neither place could it build the country that it had promised. Each war left behind something that fed the next.

The morning of Tuesday, 11 September 2001, was cloudless along the whole east coast of the United States. At fourteen minutes to nine an airliner flying far too low came down the length of Manhattan and struck the north tower of the World Trade Center between the ninety-third and ninety-ninth floors. People in the streets assumed a terrible accident. Television cameras were trained on the smoking tower when, seventeen minutes later, a second airliner banked across the harbour and flew into the south tower, live, in front of a large part of the human race.

A third aircraft hit the headquarters of the armed forces outside Washington. A fourth was over Pennsylvania, heading for the capital, when its passengers, who had learned by telephone what had happened to the others, tried to break into the cockpit. It crashed in a field.

The towers were a hundred and ten storeys high. The south tower burned for less than an hour and the north tower for an hour and three quarters, with most of those on their upper floors cut off. Many of the people trapped above the fire jumped or fell. Then, one after the other, the buildings came straight down. The New York fire department lost three hundred and forty-three of its members, most of whom had climbed the stairs to reach the trapped. In all, 2,977 people were killed, from more than ninety countries.

The president of the United States was in a classroom in Florida, listening to seven-year-olds read aloud, when an aide bent to his ear and told him that America was under attack.

## The Base

The nineteen hijackers had taken over the four aircraft with small blades of the kind used for opening cartons. Fifteen were from Saudi Arabia. Several had trained at American flying schools, where they had shown little interest in learning to land. The whole operation had cost about half a million dollars.

They had been sent by al-Qaeda, the Base, the organisation that the Saudi millionaire Osama bin Laden had founded among the foreign volunteers of the Afghan war. When that war ended, he had looked for the next enemy. In 1990 Iraq invaded Kuwait, and the Saudi royal family invited half a million American soldiers into the kingdom to protect it. To bin Laden the presence of unbelieving troops in the land of Mecca and Medina was a desecration. He decided that the corrupt rulers of the Muslim world could not be overthrown while the United States stood behind them. If America was struck hard enough at home, it would withdraw, as it had from Lebanon and Somalia.

In 1998 he issued a declaration that it was the duty of every Muslim to kill Americans, civilian and military, wherever they could be found. That August his followers drove truck bombs into the American embassies in Kenya and Tanzania and killed two hundred and twenty-four people, nearly all of them Africans.

He was by then living in Afghanistan. After the Soviet withdrawal the country had fallen into civil war among its own commanders, and in 1996 Kabul was taken by a new movement that had grown up in the religious schools of the refugee camps in Pakistan. They called themselves the Taliban, the students. They brought order, and a rule of extreme severity. Girls were forbidden to go to school and women to work or to leave the house without a male relative. Music, television, kite-flying and the shaving of beards were banned. They gave bin Laden a home.

## Sixty words

Three days after the attacks, Congress passed a resolution whose working part was a single sentence. It authorised the president to use all necessary and appropriate force against those nations, organisations or persons that he determined had planned, authorised, committed or aided the attacks, or harboured those who had. It named no enemy and set no limit of place or time. The vote was ninety-eight to nothing in the Senate and four hundred and twenty to one in the House.

The Taliban were told to hand over bin Laden and refused. On 7 October the bombing began. The Americans sent only a few hundred men at first, intelligence officers and special forces, some of whom rode into battle on horseback beside the Afghan militias that had been fighting the Taliban for years. They brought suitcases of dollars and radios with which to call in aircraft. Kabul fell in five weeks.

In December bin Laden was cornered in a complex of caves in the mountains on the Pakistani border. The job of blocking his escape was left to local Afghan fighters, who took money from both sides, and he walked out into Pakistan. The Taliban leaders crossed the same border and waited.

Afghanistan was given a new government, and for a few years it seemed a success. Millions of girls went back to school. By then the attention of Washington had moved elsewhere.

## A vial

Iraq had nothing to do with the attacks. Its dictator, Saddam Hussein, was a secular tyrant whom bin Laden despised. He had been beaten in the war over Kuwait ten years before, and since then his country had lived under sanctions, with foreign inspectors hunting for the poison gas and germ weapons that he had once made.

A group of officials around the American president had wanted to finish him for years. After 11 September they argued that the risk could no longer be run. Saddam, they said, still had stocks of chemical and biological weapons, was trying to build a nuclear one, and might hand them to terrorists. Remove him, and a democratic Iraq would become a model for the whole Arab world. In February 2003 the American secretary of state, a former general whom the world trusted, sat before the United Nations Security Council and held up a small glass vial of white powder, a stage prop, to show how little anthrax, a germ that can be made into a weapon, would be needed to kill thousands. Every statement that he was making, he said, was backed by solid sources.

It was not. The inspectors on the ground had found nothing, and asked for more time. On 15 February, in six hundred cities around the world, between six and ten million people marched against the coming war. It was the largest day of protest that had ever been held.

The United States and Britain invaded on 20 March without the approval of the Security Council. The Iraqi army collapsed in three weeks. In Baghdad an American armoured vehicle helped a crowd to pull down a bronze statue of the dictator, and on 1 May the president stood on the deck of an aircraft carrier, under a banner reading "Mission Accomplished", and announced that major combat was over.

Twelve hundred specialists then searched Iraq for eighteen months. There were no such weapons, apart from some rusting shells left over from the 1980s. The programmes had been shut down in the 1990s, and Saddam had kept up the pretence in order to frighten Iran.

## Order number two

Winning the war had needed a hundred and fifty thousand soldiers. Governing a country of twenty-five million needed far more, and a plan, and there was neither. As the regime fell, crowds stripped every public building in Baghdad down to the wiring, including the hospitals and the national museum, while American troops, who had no orders to interfere, looked on.

In May an American official was sent to rule Iraq as a viceroy. His first decree dismissed everyone who had held a senior rank in the old ruling party, which removed most of the people who knew how to run the ministries, schools and power stations. His second dissolved the Iraqi army. Four hundred thousand trained men were sent home without work, and they took their weapons with them.

By the summer there was an insurgency, of former soldiers, tribesmen and foreign volunteers. In 2004 photographs came out of a prison near Baghdad, where American guards had been tormenting Iraqi prisoners: naked men piled in heaps, a hooded figure balanced on a box with wires attached to his hands, a prisoner cowering from a dog. They were the best recruiting poster that al-Qaeda could have wished for.

Iraq's people are divided between the two main branches of Islam. The Shia are the majority, and Saddam and the men who had ruled with him were Sunni. Elections now handed power to the Shia parties. In February 2006 Sunni extremists blew up the golden dome of one of the holiest Shia shrines. After that it was a civil war of neighbours. Death squads in police uniform took men away at night, and bodies were found each morning with holes drilled in them. In the worst months three thousand civilians were killed. Baghdad, which had been a city of mixed districts, was sorted into Sunni and Shia quarters behind concrete walls.

American forces left at the end of 2011. Nearly four and a half thousand of them had died, and at least two hundred thousand Iraqis. The chief gainer was Iran, whose old enemy had been removed and replaced by a friendly government.

## In the dark

The vice-president had said, five days after the attacks, that the country would have to work the dark side. It did, and some of what it built then is still in use.

In January 2002 the first prisoners in orange overalls, shackled and blindfolded, were flown to an American naval base on the coast of Cuba. It had been chosen because the government's lawyers believed that it lay outside the reach of American courts. Seven hundred and eighty men were held there over the years. Most were never charged with anything, and a few were still there at the end of 2025. Others disappeared into secret prisons run by American intelligence in Poland, Romania, Thailand and elsewhere. There they were kept awake for a week at a time, slammed against walls, and strapped to a board while water was poured over a cloth on their faces to produce the sensation of drowning. This was done to the man who had planned the attacks for bin Laden, a Pakistani engineer captured in 2003, a hundred and eighty-three times. A Senate inquiry later concluded that it had produced no information that could not have been had by other means.

A new weapon came into its own. A pilotless aircraft could circle over a village in Pakistan or Yemen for a day, watched on a screen by an operator at an air base in the American desert, and then fire a missile at a man whose telephone had been traced. Under three presidents such strikes killed several thousand people in countries with which the United States was not at war, among them hundreds of civilians.

At home, the agency that intercepts foreign signals was turned, in secret, on Americans. In 2013 a young contractor fled to Hong Kong with a cache of its documents, which showed that it was collecting the records of telephone calls made in the United States by the billion and tapping the cables that carry the world's internet traffic.

## A fruit seller

On 17 December 2010, in a dusty town in the middle of Tunisia, a policewoman confiscated the scales of a young man who sold fruit from a barrow without a permit. He went to the governor's office to complain and was turned away. An hour later he stood in the road outside, poured paint thinner over himself, and lit it.

His name was Mohamed Bouazizi, and his death set the Arab world alight. Its countries were ruled by ageing dictators who had held power for twenty, thirty and forty years. Most of their people were under thirty, and many had degrees and no work. Protests spread across Tunisia, organised on mobile telephones, and within a month the president had fled. In Egypt, crowds occupied the central square of Cairo for eighteen days, until the army told the man who had ruled for thirty years to go. Risings followed in Libya, Yemen, Bahrain and Syria.

Almost everywhere it ended badly. In Libya, Western aircraft helped rebels to overthrow and kill the dictator, and the country broke up among rival militias. Egypt held a free election, which the Muslim Brotherhood won. A year later the army took power back, and when the Brotherhood's supporters camped in protest, it killed at least eight hundred of them in a day. Only Tunisia came out with a democracy, and that was dismantled ten years later.

The worst was Syria. In March 2011 some schoolboys in a southern town sprayed a slogan on a wall addressed to the president, who had trained as an eye doctor: your turn, doctor. They were arrested and tortured. When their families marched, the army opened fire. Peaceful protest turned into an armed revolt, and that into a war of everyone against everyone, in which the government dropped barrels packed with explosive and scrap metal onto apartment blocks from helicopters and, in 2013, used nerve gas on a suburb of its own capital. Iran and Russia sent men and bombers to keep the president, Bashar al-Assad, in power. More than half a million people were killed. Half of the country's twenty-two million people were driven from their homes.

In 2015 a million refugees and migrants, half of them Syrian, crossed the sea to Europe, many in rubber boats. That September a photograph of a three-year-old boy in a red shirt, drowned and washed up face down on a Turkish beach, was on the front page of newspapers around the world. Assad lasted until December 2024. By then Russia's army was tied down in Ukraine and Iran's allies had been battered by Israel, and when the rebels of the north-west attacked, his own army melted away in eleven days.

## The caliphate

In the wreck of Iraq and Syria something appeared that even al-Qaeda considered too extreme.

The Iraqi branch of al-Qaeda had been nearly destroyed. Its survivors crossed into Syria, recruited among the Sunnis whom both governments had persecuted, and took on as commanders the unemployed officers of Saddam's army. In June 2014 about fifteen hundred of their fighters drove into Mosul, the second city of Iraq. The Iraqi garrison of thirty thousand, trained and equipped by the Americans at vast cost, threw away its uniforms and fled. The attackers captured hundreds of armoured vehicles.

Their movement proclaimed a caliphate, and a few days later its leader stood in the pulpit of the city's great mosque as caliph, the successor of the Prophet and ruler of all Muslims. The Islamic State, as it called itself, soon held a territory half the size of Britain with eight million people. It collected taxes and ran courts. It filmed the beheading of its captives and posted the films on the internet. In the mountains of northern Iraq it set out to exterminate the Yazidis, a small people of an ancient religion, killing the men and selling several thousand women and girls as slaves. Forty thousand volunteers travelled to join it from more than a hundred countries, and its followers carried out massacres in Paris, Brussels and elsewhere.

It took a coalition of more than seventy countries, Kurdish and Iraqi soldiers on the ground, and nearly five years to take its last village. The recapture of Mosul alone lasted nine months and left the old city a field of rubble.

## Eleven days

Bin Laden was found in 2011. He had been living for five years in a walled house in a quiet Pakistani garrison town, a short walk from the country's military academy. On the night of 2 May American commandos flew in by helicopter from Afghanistan, shot him, and buried his body at sea.

The war that had been started to catch him went on for ten more years. The Taliban had come back from their sanctuaries across the border, and no number of foreign troops, which reached a hundred and thirty thousand, could hold the countryside for a government that its own people regarded as corrupt. In 2020 the United States signed an agreement with the Taliban to leave. In the summer of 2021, as the last troops packed, the Afghan army and police, three hundred thousand strong on paper, dissolved in nine days. On 15 August the Taliban walked into Kabul without a fight.

At the airport, thousands of Afghans who had worked for the foreigners ran alongside a departing American transport plane, and some clung to its wheels and fell. The last aircraft left on 30 August. It had been the longest war in American history. Girls over twelve were once again forbidden to go to school.

## 7 October

The conflict between Israelis and Palestinians had seemed, to many governments, to have been put aside. On the morning of 7 October 2023 it returned.

Several thousand fighters of Hamas broke through the fence around Gaza at dawn. They overran army posts, farming villages and an all-night music festival, and killed about twelve hundred people, most of them civilians, in their homes, in cars and in the fields. They took two hundred and fifty-one hostages back into Gaza, among them small children and the very old. For Israelis it was the deadliest day in the history of their state, and the worst for Jews anywhere since the Holocaust.

Israel went to war to destroy Hamas. It bombed and then invaded a strip of land forty kilometres long in which more than two million people were shut, with nowhere to flee. By the time a ceasefire took hold in October 2025, the health ministry in Gaza had counted more than sixty-seven thousand dead, a figure that United Nations agencies regard as broadly reliable, and a large share of them were women and children. Most of the housing had been damaged or destroyed, and nearly the whole population had been driven from place to place, many of them several times. In August 2025 the international body that monitors hunger declared a famine in Gaza's largest city.

Israel says that it fought a just war against an enemy that hid among civilians and had sworn to repeat the massacre. South Africa brought a case against it for genocide at the world court, and a United Nations commission concluded in 2025 that genocide had been committed. Israel rejects the charge. A separate court, which tries individuals, issued warrants for the Israeli prime minister and his defence minister, and for the military chief of Hamas, who was by then dead. Under the ceasefire, the last living hostages were released and Israeli forces drew back from part of the strip. Whether it would hold, and who would govern Gaza, were unsettled at the end of that year.

## The bill

Researchers at an American university have kept a running account of the wars that followed 11 September. By their count the wars have cost the United States eight trillion dollars, including the future care of its veterans. More than nine hundred thousand people were killed in the fighting, in Afghanistan, Iraq, Syria, Pakistan and Yemen, and thirty-eight million were driven from their homes.

Bin Laden had hoped to draw America into a long, costly war in Muslim lands, and in that he succeeded. He had also hoped to rouse Muslims to overthrow their rulers and unite. That did not happen. The great majority of the people killed by his movement and its offspring were themselves Muslims.

The single member of Congress who voted against the resolution of September 2001 was a Black congresswoman from California. She said on the floor of the House that some of them must urge restraint, and that in acting they should take care not to become the evil that they deplored. She received death threats for it, and needed police protection for months.

Its working sentence was sixty words long. Four presidents have relied on it to justify military action in more than twenty countries, against organisations that did not exist when it was written. At the end of 2025 it was still the law.
