## Factual flags

1. **Minor — misleading surveillance chronology introduced by the revision.**

   > “The public learned of it only because of a leak.”

   Followed immediately by the Snowden account, this makes 2013 sound like the first public disclosure of the NSA’s post-9/11 surveillance of Americans. That surveillance had already been exposed in December 2005. Snowden revealed additional programmes and their scale. Distinguish those disclosures. Source: [Risen and Lichtblau’s original reporting, reproduced by the Pulitzer Prizes](https://www.pulitzer.org/winners/james-risen-and-eric-lichtblau).

2. **Minor — inspectors had not found “nothing.”**

   > “The inspectors on the ground had found nothing, and asked for more time.”

   They had not found the alleged active WMD stockpiles, but they had discovered undeclared empty chemical warheads. They also identified prohibited Al Samoud 2 missiles and supervised their destruction before the invasion. The correct distinction is between those findings and evidence supporting the invasion’s central WMD claims. This concerns the **prewar inspections**, not the postwar weapons sentence corrected after the earlier check. Source: [UNMOVIC’s May 2003 report, paragraphs 8–10](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?l=en&s=S%2F2003%2F580&t=pdf).

3. **Minor — the looting claim has a documented exception.**

   > “As the regime fell, crowds stripped every public building in Baghdad down to the wiring, including the hospitals and the national museum, while American troops, who had no orders to interfere, looked on.”

   “Every public building” is false, and the blanket description of military inaction compounds it. Some buildings—notably the Oil Ministry—were guarded by American troops. Widespread looting and failures to protect hospitals and cultural institutions are supported; universal destruction is not. Source: [House of Commons Foreign Affairs Committee’s 2003 report](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200203/cmselect/cmfaff/405/40507.htm).

4. **Minor — the new Gaza sentence makes an existing generalisation absolute.**

   > “Because the people could not leave, the war fell on them wherever they went.”

   Most residents were trapped, but departure was not universally impossible throughout the war. WHO documented evacuations of thousands of patients, including departures after the Rafah crossing closed. The accurate claim concerns the overwhelming majority’s inability to escape, rather than nobody being able to leave. Source: [WHO’s January 2025 evacuation statement](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/statement-by-who-director-general-02jan25/).

## Clarity flags

- **Exact repetition introduced by the revision:**

  > “His death did not end the war that had been started because of him. The war that had been started to catch him went on for ten more years.”

  The first sentence contributes nothing the second does not say more precisely. “The war that had been started” also repeats almost verbatim.

- **An ambiguous chronological transition:**

  > “By then the fight in Afghanistan was against the Taliban.”

  “By then” can refer to 2011 or to the following ten years, and suggests the Taliban became the enemy at that stage. The chapter has already described fighting them in 2001. They were targets from the opening campaign. Source: [NATO’s Afghanistan chronology](https://nato.int/en/what-we-do/operations-and-missions/nato-and-afghanistan).

- **A new sentence undermines an earlier correction:**

  > “The first piece was a prison beyond the law.”

  The following sentences carefully distinguish the government lawyers’ belief from the actual reach of American courts. This new categorical introduction blurs that distinction again. “First piece” also leaves the reader unsure what system is being assembled and what chronology is claimed. This is a problem with the **new transition**, not a repetition of the earlier flag against the corrected sentence.

- **Unspecified priority:**

  > “Congress answered first, with a law.”

  First before whom, or before which response? The next sentence supplies the useful chronology. It also says Congress passed the resolution on 14 September; presidential approval made it law on 18 September. Source: [Public Law 107–40’s legislative history](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PLAW-107publ40/summary).

- **Signposting and padding:**

  > “Their case came in stages.”

  > “The case was made to the world in a single famous scene.”

  > “Taken together, the wars have a price.”

  These announce the material that follows instead of helping explain it. The first also promises identifiable “stages” that the paragraph does not actually distinguish. The last is particularly empty immediately under “The bill.”

- **Another summary immediately repeated:**

  > “Judged by his own aims, bin Laden half succeeded.”

  The following three sentences already explain what succeeded and what failed. This is redundant framing, not a factual objection to the interpretation.

- **An unnecessary absolute:**

  > “The next great upheaval in the region began with no plan at all.”

  “No plan at all” leaves its subject unclear: Bouazizi’s individual action, the first demonstrations, or the entire regional uprising. Spontaneity can be stated without implying that no participant planned anything.

- **A heading no longer matches its section:**

  > “Eleven days”

  The Afghanistan section now gives the collapse as “nine days.” Its only nearby eleven-day offensive belongs to Assad’s fall in the preceding Syria account. The heading sends the reader towards the wrong event.

- **An unclear unit of time:**

  > “In the worst months three thousand civilians were killed.”

  Does this mean three thousand **per month**, or across those months combined? The distinction matters.

- **Anonymous institutions make an important distinction harder to follow:**

  > “South Africa brought a case against it for genocide at the world court…”

  > “A separate court, which tries individuals, issued warrants…”

  The distinction is now present, but naming the International Court of Justice and International Criminal Court would help readers retain it. These names serve the explanation rather than add decorative detail.
