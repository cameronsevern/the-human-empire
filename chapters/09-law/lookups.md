# Chapter 9 fact check: lookups

Law numbers follow the Avalon Project (L. W. King) numbering, which matches Roth for the laws cited here. Note: King's text says "gold mina" in law 198; modern translations read "silver mina".

Short source keys:
- AV = https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp
- LV = https://www.louvre.fr/en/the-code-of-hammurabi
- WCH = https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Hammurabi
- WUN = https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Ur-Nammu
- WHAM = https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammurabi
- MUN = https://www.uni-muenster.de/Altoriental/institut/sammlung/foyer/Kodex_Hammurapi.html

## Section 1: Opening (title to "Before the king's court")

1. "*Iraq, from about 4,100 years ago to about 3,750 years ago.*" Verdict: correct. Source: WUN, WHAM. Ur-Nammu c. 2100 BC (about 4,100 years ago); Hammurabi reigned c. 1792-1750 BC and the stele dates to his last years (about 3,760 years ago).
2. "In the winter of 1901, a French team was digging in the ruins of Susa". Verdict: imprecise. Source: LV (says 1902), WCH (fragments found Dec 1901 to Jan 1902, first edition 1902). Evidence: the find straddles the new year; the Louvre dates it 1902. Replacement: "In the winter of 1901-02, a French team was digging in the ruins of Susa, an ancient city in the south-west of Iran."
3. "Susa, an ancient city in the south-west of Iran." Verdict: correct. Source: WCH, MUN (Susa, Khuzestan). Evidence: Susa is in Khuzestan, south-west Iran.
4. "Over a few weeks the workmen pulled three large pieces of black stone out of the ground... a pillar a little taller than a tall man". Verdict: correct. Source: MUN (found in three parts, 225 cm), LV (over 2.25 m, black basalt). Evidence: three fragments, found Dec 1901 to Jan 1902 (WCH).
5. "A king stands with one hand raised to his mouth... Facing him, on a throne, sits a god with flames rising from his shoulders. He is the sun god... the god of justice." Verdict: correct. Source: LV. Evidence: Hammurabi before Shamash, identified by the flames from his shoulders; Shamash is the sun and justice god.
6. "the whole stone is covered in writing, front and back, in neat columns". Verdict: correct. Source: WCH, LV. Evidence: text in columns on front and back (about 4,130 lines).
7. "It took scholars less than a year to read it." Verdict: correct. Source: WCH. Evidence: Scheil published the first edition in 1902, months after the find.
8. "The writing is a list of rules, about two hundred and eighty of them, and each one starts with the same word: if." Verdict: imprecise. Source: LV (282 judgements), AV (laws 36-41, about fields, are phrased as statements, not "if" clauses in King's text; text skips 13 and 66-99). Evidence: 282 is right; "each one" is too strong, since a few rules (e.g. law 36, "The field, garden, and house of a chieftain... can not be sold") do not open with "if". Replacement: "The writing is a list of rules, about two hundred and eighty of them, and almost every one starts with the same word: if."
9. "The king's name was Hammurabi, and he ruled the city of Babylon about 3,750 years ago." Verdict: correct. Source: WHAM. Evidence: reign c. 1792-1750 BC (middle chronology); stele c. 1754 BC.
10. "His stone is the most famous legal text of the ancient world. It is not the oldest". Verdict: correct. Source: WUN. Evidence: Ur-Nammu's laws (c. 2100 BC) are older, as are the Laws of Eshnunna (c. 1930 BC, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_Eshnunna).
11. "the people who lived under it seem hardly ever to have looked at it." Verdict: correct. Source: WCH. Evidence: no surviving Mesopotamian legal record mentions the Code; many Old Babylonian judgments contradict it.

## Section 2: Before the king's court

12. "That system is older than farming, and it works better than it sounds." Verdict: could not verify. Source: none found; no source dates feud and blood-price to before farming. Replacement: "That system is far older than cities, and in some places it still works today."
13. "In a city of thirty thousand strangers they cannot." Verdict: could not verify. Source: none opened; an illustrative figure, not a claim about a named city. Replacement (if wanted as fact): "In a city of tens of thousands of strangers they cannot."
14. "From the earliest royal inscriptions onward, the kings of the plain boasted of the same thing: that they had protected the widow and the orphan, and had not handed the poor man over to the rich." Verdict: imprecise. Source: WUN (prologue: "The orphan was not delivered up to the rich man; the widow was not delivered up to the mighty man"); WHAM-adjacent Urukagina page (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urukagina) does not confirm the boast. Evidence: the claim is verified for Ur-Nammu (c. 2100 BC) and Hammurabi; "earliest royal inscriptions" is not shown. Replacement: "From the time of Ur-Nammu onward, the kings of the plain boasted of the same thing: that they had not handed the orphan over to the rich man, or the widow to the mighty."

## Section 3: Two shekels for a tooth

15. "The oldest written laws that survive come from the city of Ur... about 4,100 years ago." Verdict: correct. Source: WUN. Evidence: Code of Ur-Nammu, c. 2100 BC, oldest surviving law code.
16. "That is more than three centuries before Hammurabi." Verdict: correct. Source: WUN, WHAM. Evidence: 2100 to 1754 BC is about 350 years.
17. "Akkad had fallen by then, and a king of Ur had brought the cities under one rule again. The laws were set down in his name." Verdict: correct. Source: WUN. Evidence: Ur-Nammu is "king of Ur and of Sumer and Akkad"; the preface credits him, though some scholars give the writing to his son Shulgi, so "in his name" is accurate.
18. "Only part of the text has been recovered, on broken clay tablets, and around forty rules can be read." Verdict: imprecise. Source: WUN. Evidence: about 30 of the 57 laws can be reconstructed; the article lists 29. Replacement: "Only part of the text has been recovered, on broken clay tablets, and around thirty rules can be read."
19. "A murderer was put to death, and so was a robber." Verdict: correct. Source: WUN. Evidence: law 1 (killing, death), law 2 (robbery, "will be killed", Roth numbering).
20. "A man who cut off another man's foot paid ten shekels." Verdict: correct. Source: WUN. Evidence: law 18, ten shekels.
21. "A man who knocked out a tooth paid two." Verdict: correct. Source: WUN. Evidence: law 21, two shekels of silver.
22. "There was a fixed price for a broken bone and another for a cut nose." Verdict: correct. Source: WUN. Evidence: law 19 (limb broken with a club, one mina), law 20 (nose cut with a copper knife, two-thirds of a mina).
23. "For almost everything else, the penalty was a payment in silver." Verdict: could not verify. Source: WUN lists mainly silver fines for injuries, but I did not check the full text for other death penalties (e.g. adultery, rape of a betrothed woman). Replacement: "For injuries, the penalty was a payment in silver."
24. "A shekel was a small weight of silver, about as much as a modern coin, and at the time it was roughly a month's pay for a hired worker." Verdict: correct for Hammurabi's time. Source: AV law 273 (hired labourer paid 6 she a day for part of the year, 5 she after), https://www.studylight.org/dictionaries/hdb/w/wages.html (180 she = 1 shekel). Evidence: 180 she at about 5-6 she a day is about 30-36 days. A shekel weighed about 8.3 g. (I did not check Ur III wages.)
25. "Under the older laws of Ur, an eye or a tooth had a price in silver." (Section "If a man", restated here.) Verdict: correct. Source: WUN. Evidence: law 17 eye, half a mina of silver; law 21 tooth, two shekels.
26. "The laws of Hammurabi, three hundred years later, took a harsher view." Verdict: correct. Source: AV laws 196-200 (talion). Evidence: about 350 years later; the eye, bone and tooth laws demand the same injury back.

## Section 4: One king among fifteen

27. "It was a middling town on the Euphrates, in the centre of the plain, and its kings ruled a strip of land that a man could walk across in a few days." Verdict: could not verify. Source: none opened; Babylon was a minor state before Hammurabi, but the "few days" figure is not sourced. Replacement: "It was a middling town on the Euphrates, in the centre of the plain, and its kings ruled only a few nearby cities."
28. "A letter written by an official of the time describes the position exactly. No king is strong on his own, he says. Ten or fifteen kings follow Hammurabi of Babylon, and as many follow the king of each of four other cities." Verdict: imprecise. Source: https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/political_landscapes_after_mari (Itur-Asdu letter to Zimri-Lim of Mari). Evidence: 10 or 15 kings follow each of Hammurabi, Rim-Sin of Larsa, Ibal-pi-El of Eshnunna and Amut-pi-El of Qatna; 20 follow Yarim-Lim of Yamhad. So "as many... each of four" is wrong for the fourth. Replacement: "Ten or fifteen kings follow Hammurabi of Babylon, he says, and as many follow each of three other kings; only the king of Yamhad has twenty."
29. "For thirty years Hammurabi played this game carefully. He dug canals, built temples and walls". Verdict: correct. Source: WHAM. Evidence: reign began c. 1792; Larsa fell in 1763-4, about 29 years later; his year-names record canals, temples, walls.
30. "A great kingdom to the east attacked and was beaten." Verdict: correct. Source: WHAM. Evidence: Elam attacked Eshnunna and threatened Babylonia and was repelled by Babylon and allies.
31. "He turned south and besieged the richest city on the plain for six months, until it fell." Verdict: could not verify. Source: WHAM (Larsa annexed 1763 BC; no siege length given), Britannica (siege "several months"). Replacement: "He turned south and besieged Larsa, the richest city on the plain, until it fell."
32. "He turned north-west, up the Euphrates, and took the trading city of Mari, whose king had been his ally for years." Verdict: correct. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari,_Syria. Evidence: Zimri-Lim allied with Hammurabi and sent troops; Babylon conquered Mari c. 1761 BC.
33. "Two years later he sent his men back to Mari to pull down its walls and burn its palace." Verdict: imprecise. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari,_Syria, WHAM. Evidence: Mari rebelled and was razed c. 1759 BC; the palace was destroyed; "walls" is not confirmed in the pages I could open. Replacement: "Two years later, when Mari rebelled, he sent his men back to raze the city and burn its palace."
34. "By the end, Hammurabi held the whole plain, from the Gulf to the edge of Syria." Verdict: correct. Source: WHAM, Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hammurabi). Evidence: control of the whole lower plain c. 1763 and the middle Euphrates up to Mari after 1761.
35. "He was by then an old man, with perhaps a dozen years of his reign left." Verdict: correct. Source: WHAM. Evidence: born c. 1810, died c. 1750; 1763 to 1750 is 13 years.
36. "It was in those last years that he had the black stones carved, and set them up in the temples of his cities." Verdict: imprecise. Source: WCH (stele c. 1754; fragments of a second and possibly a third stele found at Susa; the Louvre stele probably stood in one temple, possibly Sippar). Evidence: the dating is right; "temples of his cities" is not documented. Replacement: "It was in those last years that he had the black stone carved and set up in a temple. Pieces of a second stele show that there was more than one."

## Section 5: If a man

37. "If a builder builds a house for a man and does not make it sound, and the house falls down and kills its owner, the builder shall be put to death." Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 229. Evidence: builder is put to death if the owner is killed.
38. "If a man owes a debt, and a storm floods his field or a drought kills his crop, he does not have to pay his creditor any grain that year, and no interest is added." Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 48. Evidence: storm or failed harvest or lack of water; no grain is paid that year, and the interest tablet is washed out.
39. "if a man was robbed and the robber was never caught, the city in which it happened had to make good what he had lost." Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 23 (and 24). Evidence: the community pays the victim's loss; the victim first claims the amount under oath.
40. "If a man puts out the eye of another man, his own eye shall be put out. If he breaks another man's bone, his bone shall be broken. If he knocks out the tooth of a man of his own rank, his own tooth shall be knocked out." Verdict: correct. Source: AV laws 196, 197, 200. Evidence: the text is almost word for word.
41. "This is the principle of an eye for an eye, and it was new." Verdict: correct. Source: WUN, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_Eshnunna. Evidence: Ur-Nammu and Eshnunna use silver fines; Hammurabi's is the earliest surviving code with same-injury punishment.
42. "People in Hammurabi's kingdom came in three kinds... men of full standing... free commoners... slaves." Verdict: correct. Source: WCH. Evidence: awilum, muskenum, wardum; the muskenum is a lower free class.
43. "If a man of standing put out the eye of a commoner... He paid a weight of silver, about a year's wages." Verdict: wrong. Source: AV law 198 (one mina; King prints "gold" but modern translations read silver), law 273 (wages), https://www.studylight.org/dictionaries/hdb/w/wages.html. Evidence: a mina is 60 shekels; a hired labourer earned about a shekel a month (item 24), so a mina is about five years' wages. Replacement: "He paid a mina of silver, about five years' wages."
44. "If he put out the eye of another man's slave, he paid the owner half of what the slave was worth". Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 199.
45. "A surgeon who saved the life of a man of standing with his bronze knife was paid ten shekels. For a commoner he was paid five, and for a slave two." Verdict: correct. Source: AV laws 215-217. Evidence: 10, 5 and 2 shekels. ("Bronze" is Roth's reading of the knife; King says "operating knife".)
46. "If the man of standing died under the knife, the surgeon's hand was cut off." Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 218. Evidence: "his hands shall be cut off" (plural in King; Roth has "hand").
47. "If the house that fell down killed not the owner but the owner's son, it was the builder's son who was put to death." Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 230.
48. "A wife brought property with her when she married, and it stayed hers. If her husband divorced her, he had to give it back." Verdict: correct. Source: AV laws 137-142 (law 142 read in full; 138-140 give back the dowry and sometimes bride-price on divorce).
49. "If he neglected and belittled her, and she had been a good wife, she could take her property and go home to her father's house." Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 142. Evidence: if she is blameless and he neglects her, she takes her dowry and returns to her father's house.
50. "A man accused of sorcery was sent to the river and made to jump in. If he drowned, he was guilty, and his accuser took his house. If the river let him go, the accuser was put to death, and the man who had jumped took the accuser's house." Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 2. Evidence: matches (the accused leaps into the river; if he sinks the accuser takes his house; if he survives the accuser is killed and the accused takes the accuser's house).
51. "The very first rule on the stone... If a man accuses another of murder and cannot prove it, the accuser shall be put to death." Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 1. Evidence: false accusation of a capital charge is punished by death.

## Section 6: Did anyone use it?

52. "There are tens of thousands of tablets: sales of land, loans, marriage contracts, adoptions, wills, and the records of actual trials." Verdict: could not verify. Source: none opened gives a count; the categories are standard Old Babylonian archive types. Replacement: "There are many thousands of tablets: sales of land, loans, marriage contracts, adoptions, wills, and the records of actual trials."
53. "A case was heard by a group of judges, who were usually leading men of the town." Verdict: correct. Source: https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/legal-system. Evidence: courts sat in panels of professional judges (dayyanu) with elders and local notables.
54. "A contract of the time was often sealed inside a thin clay envelope with the same words written on the outside, so that nobody could change the text without breaking the cover." Verdict: correct. Source: https://collections.penn.museum/collections/object/589304. Evidence: Old Babylonian contracts had a clay case repeating the text, bearing seals; a damaged case weakened the contract as proof.
55. "one side was told to swear an oath in the temple, in front of the god's own emblem." Verdict: correct. Source: https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/legal-system, https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/132712/4/02.Wells.pdf. Evidence: oaths were sworn before the god's symbol; the court chose who swore, usually the defendant. The symbol was sometimes brought to the court instead.
56. "Again and again, a man who had argued his case with confidence in court went to the temple door and then refused to swear. He settled instead." Verdict: imprecise. Source: Wells et al., link above (the oath, or a refusal to swear, usually decided the case). Evidence: refusal normally meant losing the case, not settling. Replacement: "Again and again, a man who had argued his case with confidence in court went to the temple and then refused to swear. He lost his case."
57. "In all those thousands of records, however, the judges almost never mention the king's stone. They do not quote it." Verdict: correct. Source: WCH. Evidence: no surviving Mesopotamian legal record mentions the Code. ("Almost never" could be "never"; the page says none.) Replacement: "In all those thousands of records, however, the judges never mention the king's stone."
58. "Sometimes their verdicts match its rules, and sometimes they do not. The penalties they hand down are usually milder, and are very often payments in silver." Verdict: could not verify. Source: WCH (many judgments contradict the Code). Evidence: contradiction is confirmed; "usually milder... payments in silver" was not found in an opened source. Replacement: "Sometimes their verdicts match its rules, and often they do not."
59. "Their medical texts have the same shape: if a man's skin is yellow and his eyes are yellow, he has such an illness." Verdict: correct. Source: LV (laws share the "if" sentence structure); the Babylonian diagnostic handbook and omen series are in the same "if... then" form (not re-opened here).
60. "The gods, he says, called him by name to make justice appear in the land, to destroy the wicked, and to see that the strong did not oppress the weak." Verdict: correct. Source: AV prologue. Evidence: Anu and Bel call Hammurabi "to bring about the rule of righteousness in the land, to destroy the wicked and the evil-doers; so that the strong should not harm the weak".
61. "He lists the cities he has taken, one by one, and for each he names the good he has done its temple." Verdict: correct. Source: AV prologue. Evidence: a long list of cities, each with temple works.
62. "Let any man who has been wronged come and stand before this stone, and have it read out to him... let him be calm." Verdict: correct. Source: AV epilogue ("let the oppressed, who has a case at law, come and stand before this my image as king of righteousness... read the inscription").
63. "If they keep to his judgements, the sun god will lengthen their reigns. If one of them should change his words, or rub out his name and write another, there follows a curse that fills several columns." Verdict: correct. Source: AV epilogue. Evidence: Shamash lengthens the reign of the king who keeps the laws; long curses follow for one who alters the text or erases the name.
64. "Hammurabi's letters survive as well, about two hundred of them, sent to his governors in the cities he had taken." Verdict: correct. Source: WHAM. Evidence: just over 200 letters by Hammurabi survive, many to Sin-iddinam, governor at Larsa.
65. "He orders a field given back to a soldier who had been pushed off it. He hears that officials have taken a bribe and sends for the men, the witnesses and the silver. He tells a governor to stop arguing and read the tablet that proves who owns a piece of land." Verdict: could not verify. Source: https://cdli.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/artifacts/384863 (AbB 4 79: Hammurabi orders a field returned to a man to whom the palace had granted it, with an inquiry about the barley taken from it). Evidence: I could confirm the field-return type of letter, but not the soldier, the bribe or the "read the tablet" letters. Replacement: "He orders a field given back to a man to whom the palace had granted it, and he orders an inquiry into the barley taken from it in the meantime."

## Section 7: Written where all can see

66. "Hammurabi understood this very well. He told the wronged man to have the words read out to him, because the words would say what he was owed." Verdict: correct. Source: AV epilogue (item 62).
67. "The lender knows what he may not take from a debtor after a flood." Verdict: correct. Source: AV law 48.
68. "If the stone says that a soldier's field cannot be sold to pay his debts, and a governor takes that field, the soldier has something to point to." Verdict: imprecise. Source: AV laws 36-37. Evidence: law 36 bars any sale of the field, garden and house of a soldier ("chieftain" in King); law 37 voids a sale and the buyer loses his silver. Law 36-37 say nothing about debts or a governor. Replacement: "If the stone says that a soldier's field cannot be sold, and a buyer takes that field anyway and loses his silver, the soldier has something to point to."
69. "Almost every wrong it lists had once been a private matter between two groups of kin." Verdict: could not verify (interpretation, not a checkable fact).
70. "The notion that the law should be the same for a landowner and his slave appears nowhere on it." Verdict: correct. Source: AV laws 196-201, 215-223 (penalties differ by rank).

## Section 8: Carried off

71. "Hammurabi's kingdom did not last much better than Sargon's had." Verdict: correct. Source: WHAM. Evidence: Hammurabi's state broke up within about 150 years; Akkad lasted about 140.
72. "His son faced a revolt of the southern cities within a few years of taking the throne." Verdict: correct. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsu-iluna (not opened; search listing only). Evidence: Samsu-iluna's revolt of the south under Rim-Sin II, about 1741 BC, within ten years. Flag: confirm on the page.
73. "About a hundred and fifty years after Hammurabi died, an army from the mountains of Turkey marched down the Euphrates, sacked the city, and went home again." Verdict: correct. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mursili_I (not opened). Evidence: the Hittite sack of Babylon, c. 1595 BC, about 155 years after c. 1750. Flag: confirm on the page.
74. "It stood in its temple for some six hundred years." Verdict: correct. Source: WCH. Evidence: taken as plunder at Susa about 600 years after it was made.
75. "a king from the east raided the plain... He was the same king who carried off the carved stone of Naram-Sin of Akkad... He took Hammurabi's pillar to Susa, his own capital". Verdict: correct. Source: LV (Shutruk-Nahhunte, Elamite king, reigned c. 1185-1160 BC, took the stele to Susa as spoil), https://www.louvre.fr/en/explore/the-palace/stele-of-naram-sin (not opened). Evidence: he also took the Naram-Sin stele and added his own inscription.
76. "that is why the French found it there three thousand years later." Verdict: correct. Source: WCH, LV. Evidence: c. 1160 BC to 1902 is about 3,060 years.
77. "At Susa, somebody chiselled away several columns of the rules near the bottom... about thirty of Hammurabi's rules are missing." Verdict: imprecise. Source: AV note ("apparently comprising thirty-four paragraphs"), WCH (the page describes seven columns erased; the usual count is five). Evidence: about 35 laws, not 30. Replacement: "...so about thirty-five of Hammurabi's rules are missing."
78. "The space was meant for an inscription of the new owner, like the one he added to Naram-Sin's stone. It was never carved". Verdict: imprecise. Source: WCH (Scheil's suggestion that the Elamite king had the columns erased to carve his own inscription). Evidence: this is the standard explanation, not a recorded fact. Replacement: "He had the columns smoothed away near the bottom, as he did on other stones he took, and no inscription of his was ever carved there."
79. "A few of them have since been filled in from other copies." Verdict: imprecise. Source: WCH. Evidence: later copies restore much of the erased text. Replacement: "Most of them have since been filled in from other copies."
80. "In the schools of the plain, boys who were learning to write copied out the laws of Hammurabi on clay... They went on doing it for more than a thousand years... Pieces of those school copies turn up all over Iraq." Verdict: correct. Source: WCH. Evidence: more than 50 manuscripts from many Mesopotamian cities; some about 1,000 years after the stele.
81. "If an ox gores a man to death while walking down the street, nobody is to blame. However, if the ox was known to gore, and its owner had been warned and had not tied it up or padded its horns, then the owner has to pay." Verdict: correct. Source: AV laws 250-251 (half a mina if a free man is killed; one-third of a mina for a slave, law 252).
82. "A thousand years later, and a thousand kilometres to the west, the laws in the Hebrew Bible take up the same case." Verdict: imprecise. Source: WCH (laws 250-252 parallel Exodus 21:28-32; most scholars attribute it to shared tradition), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_Eshnunna (a goring-ox group of laws, c. 1930 BC). Evidence: the parallel is real, but the ox case is older than Hammurabi, so it did not start with his way of writing. Replacement: "The ox case is older than his stone, for the laws of Eshnunna have it too. A thousand years later, and a thousand kilometres to the west, the laws in the Hebrew Bible take up the same case."
83. "His carved portrait hangs today in the chamber of the United States House of Representatives, among the lawgivers of history." Verdict: correct. Source: https://www.aoc.gov/explore-capitol-campus/art/hammurabi-relief-portrait. Evidence: one of 23 marble relief portraits of lawgivers over the gallery doors, installed 1949-50.
84. "Hammurabi asked that his name be remembered for ever". Verdict: correct. Source: AV epilogue (his name to be named and praised for ever; curses on whoever erases it).
