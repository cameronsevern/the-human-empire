1. **Unsupported inference at the heart of the argument — high confidence.** [Line 13](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:13): “It is not the oldest, and the people who lived under it seem hardly ever to have looked at it.”

   The absence of citations in surviving court records does not establish whether people read or consulted the text. The following claim that judges “ignored” it, and the final sentence about judges seldom needing to read it, make the same unsupported leap. The defensible fact is that its relationship to judicial practice remains debated; lack of explicit citation is evidence about citation, not readership. See Pamela Barmash, [*The Legal Authority of the Laws of Hammurabi*](https://academic.oup.com/book/31901/chapter-abstract/267578408).

2. **Internal contradiction and reader confusion.** [Line 117](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:117): “He cannot bend a stone.”

   Earlier, the chapter says judges were not bound by the collection and sometimes delivered different verdicts. Here, an unalterable inscription suddenly becomes an effective restraint on their decisions. Writing fixes wording; it does not prevent selective interpretation or non-enforcement. The subsequent builder, lender and soldier examples need to distinguish an advertised standard of justice from a demonstrated legal remedy. The [Louvre’s account](https://www.louvre.fr/en/the-code-of-hammurabi) describes a collection of jurisprudence, rather than a modern legal code.

3. **Overstated royal monopoly — high confidence.** [Line 123](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:123): “The right to decide what happened next, however, was no longer theirs.”

   Families retained important legal powers. Section 129, for example, allows a husband to spare his adulterous wife. More broadly, household heads, local assemblies and arbitrators exercised jurisdiction alongside royal authorities. Royal involvement did not simply replace family authority. Sources: [Hammurabi §129](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp); Sophie Démare-Lafont, [“Judicial Decision-Making”](https://www.academia.edu/44182718/Judicial_Decision_Making).

4. **Wrong monetary comparison — high confidence.** [Line 77](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:77): “He paid a weight of silver, about a year's wages.”

   Section 198 specifies **one mina of silver: sixty shekels**. Using the chapter’s earlier comparison of one shekel to a month’s pay, that would be five years, not one. Actual wage comparisons need a specified period and occupation; the statutory amount is secure. Sources: [§198, with Huehnergard’s translation](https://ehammurabi.org/law/198); [Mesopotamian weight conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Ur-Nammu#Content).

5. **Misleading account of early Hammurabi — high confidence.** [Line 53](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:53): “He looked like a useful ally to everybody and a danger to nobody.”

   Hammurabi was already campaigning early in his reign: his seventh-year formula records victories over Uruk and Isin. His later expansion was dramatic, but the preceding decades were not simply thirty years of harmless waiting. The universal claim also breaks the rule against absolutes. See the reign’s dated campaigns in the [*Encyclopedia of the Bible* entry](https://www.biblegateway.com/resources/encyclopedia-of-the-bible/Hammurabi).

6. **Inaccurate paraphrase of the Mari letter — high confidence.** [Line 51](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:51): “Ten or fifteen kings follow Hammurabi of Babylon, and as many follow the king of each of four other cities.”

   The letter gives comparable followings to **three** other rulers, then **twenty** followers to Yarim-Lim of Yamhad. That distinction matters because the paragraph uses the letter to establish equal strength. See Jack Sasson’s transcription and translation in [“Bible Research and the Mari Archives,” note 20](https://cdn-dev.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my-dev/wp-content/uploads/sites/240/2020/03/Sasson-2019-Bible-Research-Mari-Archives.pdf).

7. **Misleading summary of Ur’s penalties — high confidence.** [Line 39](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:39): “For almost everything else, the penalty was a payment in silver.”

   After naming only murder and robbery as capital offences, this obscures other severe provisions: death for specified sexual offences, and a slave woman’s mouth scoured with salt for insolence. The strong, supportable contrast is that **the surviving bodily-injury provisions commonly prescribe compensation**. It should not become a general portrait of a mild penal system. See the [surviving laws of Ur-Nammu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Ur-Nammu#Surviving_laws).

8. **An uncertain social classification presented as settled — medium-high confidence.** [Line 75](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:75): “At the top were the men of full standing, the landowners and officials.”

   The text distinguishes *awīlum*, *muškēnum* and slaves, but the precise social identities of the first two remain disputed. They cannot securely be mapped onto an exhaustive hierarchy of landowners/officials versus ordinary free commoners. The penalty differences are clear; the occupational definitions are not. See [Wohl, “Toward a Definition of muškenum”](https://janes.scholasticahq.com/article/2231-toward-a-definition-of-muskenum.pdf), and the competing interpretations discussed in [“Popular Power in Ancient Near Eastern and Archaic Greek Polities”](https://www.academia.edu/5067762/Popular_power_in_ancient_Near_Eastern_and_archaic_Greek_polities_A_reappraisal_of_Western_and_Eastern_political_cultures).

9. **Ambiguity with chapter 6 and an overbroad “oldest” — high confidence about the distinction.** [Line 35](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:35): “The oldest written laws that survive come from the city of Ur, in the far south of the plain, about 4,100 years ago.”

   Chapter 6 already describes an earlier ruler of Lagash recording debt cancellation and restrictions on officials. Ur-Namma’s text is conventionally identified as the oldest surviving **law collection**, a more specific claim than the oldest written legal rules. Explain that distinction so the reader does not think the earlier reforms have been forgotten. Sources: [chapter 6](chapters/06-taxes/chapter.md:176); [Oxford’s guide to Mesopotamian legal sources](https://libguides.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/law-menat/mesopotamia).

10. **Contradiction with chapter 7.** [Line 29](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:29): “A war leader is needed only when there is a war.”

    Chapter 7 explicitly explains the opposite development: “A commander who is needed every other summer soon becomes a commander who is needed all the time.” It also gives the commander permanent armed followers and control of grain. Judicial authority can add another reason to need a king, but this sentence resets the explanation the reader has already received. See [chapter 7, line 41](chapters/07-kings/chapter.md:41).

11. **Chronological mismatch — high confidence.** [Line 57](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:57): “He was by then an old man, with perhaps a dozen years of his reign left.”

    The preceding paragraph has reached Mari’s destruction, conventionally dated around 1759 BCE; Hammurabi died around 1750. That leaves roughly **nine years**, not twelve. His age is also less securely known than his regnal chronology. Sources: [the chronology of Mari’s destruction](https://www.vandenhoeck-ruprecht-verlage.com/downloads/productPreviewFiles/LP_978-3-8471-0887-0.pdf); [Louvre reign dates](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010174436).

12. **A concrete textual error — high confidence.** [Line 79](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:79): “If the man of standing died under the knife, the surgeon's hand was cut off.”

    Section 218 specifies the surgeon’s **hands**, plural. See [Hammurabi §218](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp).

13. **An explanatory step is missing.** [Line 73](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:73): “If the price of a tooth is his own tooth, his silver cannot help him.”

    The preceding example is a rich man injuring a poor man. The next paragraph then says retaliation operated within a privileged legal category. A general reader can easily take wealth and legal rank to be interchangeable, making the example appear to establish protection that the following paragraph withdraws. Explain that two men could differ in wealth while sharing the relevant legal status.

14. **Unsupported absolute about vulnerable people.** [Line 25](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:25): “A widow, an orphan or a stranger passing through has no brothers to stand behind them.”

    Widowhood does not remove a woman’s brothers; orphanhood does not remove siblings or extended kin. The next sentence—“Anybody can cheat them, and nothing will follow”—compounds the problem. The relevant vulnerability is **lacking effective kin protection**, not membership of any of these categories automatically. These are exceptions that directly break the sentences, not objections to a confident narrative voice.

15. **An absolute contradicted by the book’s own examples.** [Line 101](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:101): “A list of cases, each starting with ‘if’, was how the scholars of the plain wrote down everything they knew.”

    Chapter 6 has already introduced lexical lists; chapter 8 discusses hymns and a king list. Those are not conditional case collections. The resemblance to medical and divinatory writing supports the narrower comparison without “everything.” See [chapter 6, line 144](chapters/06-taxes/chapter.md:144).

16. **An unestablished universal negative.** [Line 121](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:121): “Nobody in Babylon took that step.”

    Nothing presented establishes that no Babylonian ever invoked a standard of justice against a king. The following “It was a very long time before anybody did” adds an undefined worldwide priority claim. Specify the distinction intended—criticising a ruler, invoking divine justice, or legally compelling royal obedience—or remove this assertion. As written, it breaches *style.md*’s rule on “nobody” and makes the origin story carry more than the evidence shown.

17. **Geographical overload without orientation.** [Line 55](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:55): “A great kingdom to the east attacked and was beaten.”

    This follows unnamed kingdoms and precedes an unnamed southern city, then Mari. Readers must track several political entities through compass directions alone. Naming Elam and Larsa at their first useful appearance, with a brief geographical description, would make this sequence easier to follow. The outline’s planned map would also help; the current chapter contains none.

18. **Explicit signposting.** [Line 13](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:13): “To see why a king would go to such trouble over rules that his own judges ignored, it helps to start with how people settled their quarrels when there were no kings.”

    This announces the explanatory route instead of entering the story. Other clear instances are “The surprise is how mild they are” (line 37), “Notice what a list like that does” (line 43), and “Part of the answer lies in its form” (line 101). These conflict with the explicit ban on announcing a point, answer or surprise.
