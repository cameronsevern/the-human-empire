# Chapter 9 — Who wrote the first laws?

**Line under the title:** Iraq, from about 4,100 years ago to about 3,750 years ago.
**Length:** 3,500 to 4,500 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Wrongs were once settled between families, by revenge or by payment. Kings took that business over, because a king who judges is a king people need. The oldest written laws are lists of fines. The famous ones, carved on a black stone for Hammurabi of Babylon, set a penalty by the rank of the victim and made "an eye for an eye" the rule among equals. The judges of the time did not cite the stone. It was there to show gods and subjects that the king was just. Even so, writing a rule down in public changed what a rule is: it could be read back to the powerful, it outlived the king, and it was copied for a thousand years.

## The story

1. **The black stone.** Answer first. Susa, the winter of 1901: three pieces of black stone, a king before a god, and columns of small writing.
2. **Before the king's court.** How a wrong was put right in a village: kin, revenge, payment, elders. Why a city of strangers needs a judge, and why a king wants to be one.
3. **Two shekels for a tooth.** The oldest laws that survive, from Ur, three centuries before Hammurabi. Almost all fines.
4. **One king among fifteen.** Babylon as a middling city. Thirty years of waiting, then five years of conquest.
5. **If a man.** Reading the stone: the builder, the surgeon, the eye, the river, the debt, the wife. Three ranks of person.
6. **Did anyone use it?** Thousands of court records and almost no mention of the stone. Oaths before the god. The king's letters. What the stone was for.
7. **Written where all can see.** What changes when a rule is public and fixed.
8. **Carried off.** The stone taken to Susa; the text copied for a thousand years; the ox that gores.
