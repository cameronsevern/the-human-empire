# Chapter 6 lookups (all 26 items)

Numbering follows the brief. Verdicts: Confirmed / Wrong / Overstated / Disputed / Could not verify.

## 1. Bevelled-rim bowls
Claims: "pressed into shape by hand"; "three out of every four pieces"; "Iran to Syria"; "oldest about five and a half thousand years old"; "all roughly the same size"; "probably held a day's food for one worker".
Verdict: Overstated (hand-pressing) / Disputed (ration use).
- Production: the exact method is unknown; the most accepted theory is moulding (mobile mould, ground mould or another bowl). Hand-forming is the LESS accepted theory, because Goulder's replicators could not get straight sides and a defined base by hand. She also argues the heavy straw/mineral temper made wheel-throwing unsuitable and uniform stackable shapes are easier with moulds.
- Share: "roughly three-quarters of all ceramics at Uruk-culture sites" (Mallowan 1965, as cited by Wikipedia); a generalisation, not per-site data.
- Range: Iraq, Syria, Iran (Zagros, north, centre, south), Anatolia (one outlier in Pakistan). "Iran to Syria" is fine.
- Age: appear in Early Uruk c. 3900-3600 BC (5,900-5,600 years ago), common in Middle Uruk c. 3600-3400 BC. "About five and a half thousand years" is fine for the bulk.
- Size: usually described as uniform (c. 10 cm tall, 18 cm mouth) but measured capacities vary: Chogha Mish 385-2,580 mL, clustering near 700 mL; most hold 0.78-1.21 L. "Roughly" is fair; "all" is not.
- Function: the ration-bowl reading (Nissen, Johnson, Pollock, Bernbeck) is the leading view, but it is challenged. Goulder (Antiquity 2010) argues they were bread moulds, the bread possibly handed out as pay to administrators/workers; Potts 2009: votive; residue at Shakhi Kora: ruminant meat/dairy stew (Perruchini et al. 2023). Ration-bowl is a strong hypothesis, not a settled fact. Bread-mould and ration-bowl views are compatible with the chapter's point (rations for workers): the bowl may have produced the loaf that was the ration.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beveled_rim_bowl ; https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/administrators-bread-an-experimentbased-reassessment-of-the-functional-and-cultural-role-of-the-uruk-bevelrim-bowl/1F7E7522C1BA952347B319E2D14441A6
Replacement (paragraph 7, 11): "This bowl, by contrast, was pressed into a mould in a few seconds, baked, used and thrown away." and "Each one probably held a day's ration for one worker, either grain or a loaf baked in the bowl itself." (If a firmer line is wanted: "Many archaeologists think each one held a day's food for one worker" is a true statement, not a hedge.)

## 2. NINDA sign = bowl; GU7 = bowl + head
Verdict: Disputed (NINDA derivation); GU7 description Confirmed in form.
- Wikipedia (citing Englund 1998): early scholarship suggested a proto-cuneiform sign, and perhaps the later cuneiform GAR/NINDA/AKALU, derived from the bevelled-rim bowl; "an early suggestion, not a settled conclusion".
- Unicode proto-cuneiform proposals describe a head with a bowl beside it used for 'eat' or 'drink'; GU7 is the standard reading "to eat, consume" (ePSD frequency data). So "bowl plus a head = eating" is supported. I did NOT find a page that gives the exact sign composition of GU7 (bowl + head) in sign-list terms, nor one that states the bowl in the sign is the bevelled-rim bowl as established fact.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beveled_rim_bowl ; https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2017/17157-n4797-proto-cuneiform.pdf ; https://unicode.org/L2/L2016/16267-n4760-proto-cuneiform.pdf
Replacement: "Their sign for a ration looks like this bowl, and their sign for eating is that sign with a human head added." (True description of the signs; the derivation from the pottery type is left as resemblance.)

## 3. Farming preceded first tax-collecting states by "about five thousand years"
Verdict: Overstated or understated depending on count; safer to rephrase.
- Scott, Against the Grain: the gap between domestication of the main cereals and the first states is "now reckoned to stretch for 4,000 years" (Inference review quotes). Counting from c. 9000 BC domestication to c. 3300 BC proto-cuneiform accounts is nearly 6,000 years. Both readings exist.
Sources: https://inference-review.com/article/great-scott ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Against_the_Grain_(Scott_book)
Replacement: "People were growing wheat and barley in the Middle East for more than four thousand years before anybody collected a share of it." (true on either count). The heading "Five thousand years without a collector" would then need "Four thousand years..." or "Thousands of years without a collector".

## 4. Cassava can stay in ground a year or two; harvested roots rot within days
Verdict: Confirmed.
- Crop Trust / CGIAR genebank pages: roots can be left in the ground up to 24 months (some varieties more), harvest delayed until market or processing conditions. Post-harvest physiological deterioration starts within 24-48 hours; microbial rot follows within about 5-7 days. A 1975 USAID publication notes in-ground roots become woody/fibrous if left too long.
Sources: https://www.croptrust.org/knowledge-hub/crops/cassava/ ; https://genebanks.cgiar.org/resources/crops/cassava ; https://pdf.usaid.gov/pdf_docs/pnaae775.pdf
No replacement needed. ("a year or two" and "within days" both hold.)

## 5. Almost every early state relied on grain (Scott; Mayshar et al.); highland New Guinea taro/roots, no kings or states until colonial contact
Verdict: Grain claim Confirmed. New Guinea claim Overstated/partly wrong.
- Scott (quoted in reviews): "virtually all classical states were based on grain, including millets. History records no cassava states." Mayshar, Moav, Pascali, JPE 130(4), 2022: cereals, not land productivity, drove hierarchy because cereals are visible, seasonal, storable, and easy to appropriate; roots/tubers are perishable, bulky (c. 70% moisture) and non-seasonal.
- New Guinea: Kuk Swamp agriculture dates from c. 6,950-6,440 years ago (taro among the early crops; Denham et al. 2003). Highlands societies were small and egalitarian/"big-man" without states; first Australian contact in the 1930s (Leahy 1930s; some villages 1928). BUT sweet potato arrived within the last few hundred years (c. 300-400 years) and replaced taro as the staple in the highlands, and the dense populations and big pig herds are linked to sweet potato. So "grew taro and other roots for thousands of years in crowded valleys" is shaky: the crowded-valley, pig-herd highlands were sweet-potato societies. "Never had a king or a tax collector until foreigners brought them" is fine in substance (no state before colonial contact), though I could not find a single source stating it in those words.
Sources: https://reason.com/2017/11/26/statist-just-so-stories/ ; https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/staff/omoav/mayshar_et_al_jpe_2nd_11_oct_2019.pdf ; https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65def4734c5aef494fdea6ec ; https://carta.anthropogeny.org/node/308855 ; https://savageminds.org/2008/07/03/what-counts-as-first-contact-an-example-from-papua-new-guinea/
Replacement: "The people of highland New Guinea, meanwhile, were farming taro and other crops in the highland swamps by about seven thousand years ago, and until Australian patrols arrived in the 1930s they had no kings or tax collectors." (Both halves are sourced; drops "crowded valleys".)

## 6. Southern Mesopotamia ecology
Claims: rainfall far below barley's needs; summer heat above 45 C; no stone, metal, good timber; irrigated yields several times rain-fed north; settled ~8,000 years ago.
- Rainfall: south receives under 200 mm a year, insufficient for rain-fed agriculture; irrigation essential (Springer 2015 study of southern Mesopotamian landscapes). Penn Museum Expedition: rainfall "was, and still is, insufficient for dry-farming in this zone"; first southern settlers c. 6000 BCE (8,000 years ago) met marshland. Confirmed.
- Heat above 45 C: not found in a source retrieved (Wikipedia describes "hot, dry climate", river flow at "the time of greatest heat"). Could not verify the number; summer maxima in southern Iraq do exceed 45 C today, but I did not find a citable ancient/archaeological source.
- No stone, metal, timber: the plain is alluvial; Wikipedia (Agriculture in Mesopotamia) says palms, poplars and tamarisks served small-scale building, while temples/palaces needed imported cedar, ebony, cypress. "No timber worth the name" is fair for construction timber; "no stone, no metal" not found stated in a retrieved source (very widely stated though).
- Yield multiple: Wikipedia says only that southern irrigated farming "could produce high returns" and northern returns were "usually lower"; no "several times" figure retrieved. Could not verify "several times".
- Settlement date: Ubaid-period settlements in the south date c. 5300-4700 BC radiocarbon (Wikipedia, Sumer); Penn: first settlers c. 6000 BCE. "About eight thousand years ago" is Confirmed on the Penn date; 7,000-7,300 on the Ubaid date.
Sources: https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12685-015-0127-9.pdf ; https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/adaptation-and-change/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Mesopotamia ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer
Verdict: Confirmed (rain, timber, settlement); Could not verify (45 C; "several times" yield).
Replacement for the unverified bits: "In summer the heat is fierce." and "A family that cut a ditch from the river to a field could harvest far more than the same work earned in the rainy hills to the north." (no figures).

## 7. Climate drier after ~6,000 years ago; rivers into fewer channels; people concentrated (Nissen; 5.9 ka)
Verdict: Overstated / Disputed.
- Reculeau (History.com): around 4000 BC "the climates slowly became drier and the rivers more unpredictable"; the marsh retreated from Lower Mesopotamia, leaving settlements surrounded by land needing irrigation. This supports "drier, rivers more unpredictable" at about 6,000 years ago.
- "Rivers shrank into fewer channels" and "people crowded together along the ones that were left": not found. Penn: aridity effects are debated ("ongoing debate about the degree to which any period of drought... affected cultural trends"). Wikipedia's 5.9 ka page does not mention Mesopotamia. Giosan & Goodman, PLOS ONE 2025: the Gulf reached far inland and tides pushed fresh water into the lower rivers c. 7,000-5,000 years ago, allowing short-canal irrigation; delta growth then cut off tidal access and the later large canal systems followed. That paper's driver is delta/coastline change, not climate drying. I could not find Nissen's argument online.
Sources: https://www.history.com/articles/how-mesopotamia-became-the-cradle-of-civilization ; https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/adaptation-and-change/ ; https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0329084 ; https://www.sci.news/archaeology/ancient-sumer-rise-14183.html
Replacement: "About six thousand years ago the climate grew drier and the rivers less predictable, and the marshes drew back, leaving villages with fields that only a canal could water. Hunters with a grievance had once been able to walk away. These farmers had a crop that could be counted and a home that they could not leave."

## 8. Eridu
Claims: first shrine ~7,500 years ago; single mud-brick room "about the size of a garden shed" with offering table; eighteen superimposed temples over more than two thousand years; final one a great temple on a platform; fish bones; near the old shoreline.
Verdict: Mostly Confirmed; two details need fixing.
- Eighteen levels: "at least eighteen" superimposed temple levels (Wikipedia lead; some accounts count 17 Ubaid levels).
- First shrine: Level XVIII/XVII, c. 5400-5300 BC (7,300-7,400 years ago); single room, c. 2.8 x 2.8 m to 3 x 4 m (so shed-sized); offering table and altar against an end wall (gatesofhistory summary of Safar & Lloyd; a primary Safar/Lloyd/Mustafa 1981 volume is the source). "About seven and a half thousand" is slightly high: use "about seven thousand four hundred years ago" or "more than seven thousand years ago".
- Span: first shrine c. 5400 BC to the Ur III ziggurat (c. 2050-2000 BC) is more than three thousand years. Ubaid temples alone: c. 5400-3800 BC (about 1,600 years). "More than two thousand years" is true but understated if the ziggurat is counted.
- Last/final: the top level is the unfinished Ur III ziggurat of Amar-Sin, a stepped tower; the last Ubaid temple (Level VI, c. 22 x 9 m, 4000-3800 BC) stood on a raised platform. The chapter's "final one a large temple on a platform" fits Temple VI better than the topmost level.
- Fish bones: thick deposits of catfish and carp bones mixed with ash around the altars in the early temples (and in Temple VI), read as offerings to Enki/Abzu cult. The chapter places them "in its floors" (the last temple); better "in the floors of the early shrines".
- Shoreline: Wikipedia: "close to the Persian Gulf near the mouth of the Euphrates" (about 90 miles inland today). Confirmed.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eridu ; https://gatesofhistory.com/en/article/temple-of-eridu-first-city
Replacement: "About seven thousand four hundred years ago, its people built a shrine there... Archaeologists digging down through the mound found eighteen shrines stacked one upon another, built over more than three thousand years. In the floors of the early ones lay heaps of fish bones, from offerings made to the god." (and drop "The last was a great temple, standing on a platform" or change to "The last was a stepped tower, a ziggurat, built by a king of Ur.")

## 10. Uruk
Claims: ~5,000 years ago, largest settlement yet, tens of thousands, wall nine km round; Eridu to Uruk "a day or two's walk"; Eanna belonged to Inanna.
Verdict: Overstated (wall date); distance Could not verify.
- Size/pop: Wikipedia: c. 250 hectares by end of Uruk period c. 3100 BC; up to 50,000 residents c. 3100 BC; "largest urban area in the world at the time". Other sources 40,000-50,000. Confirmed.
- Wall: about 9 km, 12-15 m high; dated from sediment layers to c. 3000 BC (Uruk III/Jemdet Nasr, 3100-2900 BC), later credited to Gilgamesh. So the wall is c. 5,000 years old but post-dates the Late Uruk city (3400-3100 BC); "by five thousand years ago ... a wall" is correct only if you mean c. 3000 BC. Fine for "five thousand years ago". Confirmed on that reading.
- Distance Eridu to Uruk: not stated in sources retrieved. Map coordinates (Eridu c. 30.82 N 45.996 E; Uruk c. 31.32 N 45.64 E) put them roughly 65 km apart (my own calculation, not a cited source), which is two to three days on foot, not "a day or two". Could not verify a published figure.
- Eanna: temple district dedicated to Inanna from the earliest Uruk period (Wikipedia). Confirmed.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk
Replacement: "a few days' walk from Eridu" (or "about sixty kilometres from Eridu").

## 11. Warka Vase
Claims: ~1 m tall; ~5,200 years old; from Inanna's precinct; bands from the bottom: wavy water, barley and flax, sheep, naked men with baskets and jars, goods to a woman.
Verdict: Mostly Confirmed; plant detail uncertain.
- Height c. 105-110 cm incl. base (relief c. 92 cm). Date c. 3500-2900 BC; findspot stratum 3100-2900 BC; found by German excavators 1933/34 in a temple complex of Inanna at Uruk (Iraq Museum IM19606). "About five thousand two hundred years" fits a date near 3200 BC.
- Registers: bottom: marsh/delta vegetation and plants (reeds, date palms, barley, wheat per Wikipedia; one source: "alternating stalks of barley and reeds"); then rams and ewes; then nude men carrying baskets and jars of offerings (fruit, grain); top: female deity before her shrine with reed bundles, a nude figure presenting an offering, and a ruler in a kilt (the top scene possibly a ritual marriage). The "wavy line of water" at the base is commonly described (not explicitly in Wikipedia text I retrieved). Flax is NOT supported by what I found.
- Top figure: a female deity standing at her shrine; some describe a priestess. The chapter's "a woman receives them" is safe.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warka_Vase ; https://brewminate.com/?p=38257
Replacement: "Above the water grow date palms and ears of barley." (drop flax) and "At the top, the goods arrive at the shrine, where a woman, usually read as the goddess, receives them."

## 12. Labour duty and rations
Verdict: Confirmed (general).
- Englund/Engelsberg essay: archaic texts track "rations given out to labourers"; dependent labourers on Eanna's estates included reed-cutters, fishermen, potters, cooks, barbers, ploughmen, stockmen and weavers. Corvee ("each family owed a number of days each year") is attested for later periods (Ur III, Early Dynastic) rather than clearly for archaic Uruk; the bevelled-rim bowl literature uses corvee rationing (Pollock 2003) as the model. Barley, oil, wool: later ration texts (Ur III) give barley, oil, wool; beer is attested for Uruk (Kushim) and later.
Source: https://engelsbergideas.com/?p=992
No change needed; consider "later records show families owed labour days" if the author wants to be exact.

## 13. Textile workshops, captive women, geme, "counted like cattle"
Verdict: Sign claim Confirmed; workshop-at-Uruk claim Overstated; "same way as cattle" Overstated.
- Sign: archaic SAL KUR ("woman" + "mountain/foreign land") first meant female and male slaves, later only female slaves, Sumerian geme2; most specialists read KUR as the Zagros/highland homeland of non-native slaves (CDLI commentary on Englund's slave tablets). Confirmed.
- Counting: Englund's commentary says the count of slaves was performed in the sexagesimal system, and Kelley (Oxford thesis) notes "herded humans" texts (Englund 2009) are under 1% of proto-cuneiform texts, using KUR/SAL/TUR signs for male/female/child. I found no source that says slaves were counted in the same way as cattle or that archaic Uruk had large women-staffed temple weaving workshops. Textile weaving by women with rations is well attested for later Lagash and Girsu (Maekawa 1980, "Female Weavers and Their Children in Lagash - Pre-Sargonic and Ur III").
Sources: http://cdli.earth/cdli-tablet/327 ; https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:afa3362e-1182-43aa-a2b9-d675bd8c585a/files/mb14de61d14383c62ee78433ee981340a ; https://www.thecollector.com/women-ancient-mesopotamia
Replacement: "Not everyone who worked for the temple had a choice in the matter. In the later temples of Lagash, women wove the cloth in large workshops and were paid in rations. The oldest way of writing 'slave woman' joins two signs: the sign for a woman, and the sign for the mountains. The slaves came from the hills, where the raiding parties went to get them. In the oldest accounts, slaves are listed by number alongside sheep and goats." -- I could not confirm the last sentence; safer: "The oldest accounts list slaves by number and give each a name; the scribes counted them in the same sixty-based system they used for everything else." (Englund: counted sexagesimally; individuals named.)

## 14. Stationary bandit (Olson)
Verdict: Confirmed.
- Olson, "Dictatorship, Democracy, and Development," APSR 87(3), 1993, 567-576: under anarchy roving bandits destroy incentive to produce; a bandit who settles and monopolises theft becomes a stationary bandit, takes regular taxes, and supplies order and public goods because he has an "encompassing interest" in the territory's output. Matches the chapter's description (takes a part, drives off rivals, may mend the canal). Olson's own example is a Chinese warlord, not Mesopotamia, and critics note the theory does not explain why a roving bandit settles; the chapter applies it as an analogy, which is fine.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_bandit_theory ; https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:apsrev:v:87:y:1993:i:03:p:567-576_10 ; https://sites.oxy.edu/whitney/xaccess/csp2004/property_olson.htm

## 15. Hydraulic theory rejected; early canals small and local; big canals came after cities
Verdict: Confirmed.
- Wittfogel, Oriental Despotism (1957), named Mesopotamia a "hydraulic civilization." R. McC. ## 17. Tokens, envelopes, tablets (Schmandt-Besserat)
Verdict: Overstated as a flat account; Confirmed as "her account".
- Her schema: cone = small measure of grain, sphere = large measure of grain, ovoid = jar of oil; plain tokens from c. 8000 BC; complex tokens with cities; bullae/hollow envelopes with tokens inside; impressed marks on the outside; then solid tablets. Most scholars accept the broad outline, but disagree on details. Critics: Zimansky 1993 (tokens meant whatever their users wanted; no fixed lexicon), Englund 1993/1998 (token-to-sign comparisons rarely demonstrable apart from numerical notation), Damerow, Brown, Friberg, Michalowski; her evidence base is almost entirely southern Mesopotamia/Susa. Her own tablet date "by about 3000 BC" is later than the Uruk numerical tablets (c. 3500-3350 BC). Tokens continued alongside cuneiform tablets (Ziyaret Tepe).
Sources: https://myslu.stlawu.edu/~dmel/mesomath/tokens.html ; https://urkesh.org/attach/Zimansky1993.pdf ; https://cdli.ucla.edu/staff/englund/publications/englund2011a.pdf ; https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/news/worlds-oldest-accounting-system-withstood-invention-of-writing/
Replacement: "The first answer was already old. For thousands of years, the farmers of the Middle East had kept count with small clay tokens. In the account of the archaeologist Denise Schmandt-Besserat, a cone stood for a small measure of grain, and a ball for a large one." Then "The keepers of the storehouse put the tokens to work. Archaeologists have found hollow clay balls with tokens inside and the same marks pressed into the outside, and flat numbered tablets followed." and cut "That pad is the first written tablet" to "Flat tablets followed."

## 18. First tablets, Uruk archive, accounts vs lists, Standard Professions List, literature 700 years later
Verdict: Overstated (nine in ten; "about 120"); rest Confirmed.
- Date: numerical tablets Uruk V c. 3500-3350 BC; first proto-cuneiform (Uruk IV) c. 3350-3200 BC. "About five thousand three hundred years ago" is fine.
- Number: about 5,060 tablets from Uruk (190 Uruk V, 1,776 Uruk IV, 3,094 Uruk III); CDLI: "almost 5000 tablets and fragments."
- Split: Englund: the usual figure is 85% administrative, 15% lexical, and these are averages: under 1% of Uruk IV tablets are lexical, close to 20% of Uruk III. So "roughly nine in ten" is too high; "more than eight in ten" is right.
- Standard Professions List (Lu2 A): hierarchically ordered, best-attested archaic list (163 secure exemplars), canonised in Uruk III. The later Early Dynastic composition is 129 lines; I did not find a source giving the exact archaic count. "About a hundred and twenty" is close to the 129 lines but unverified for the Uruk version.
- Literature: earliest literary texts (Zame hymns, proverbs, Instructions of Shuruppak, Kesh Temple Hymn) come from Fara/Abu Salabikh c. 2600-2500 BC, roughly 700 years after c. 3300 BC. Confirmed. Letters and legal texts also come in the Early Dynastic period.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform ; https://cdli.ucla.edu/staff/englund/publications/englund2011a.pdf ; https://5300jahreschrift.materiale-textkulturen.de/artikel/5300jahre_schrift_sauer_archaische_keilschriftlisten.pdf ; https://cdli.earth/articles/cdlj/2003-3 ; https://wikiwand.com/en/Zame_(hymns)
Replacement: "Roughly eight in ten are accounts... The remainder are lists of signs for apprentice clerks to copy. One of the most copied is a list of job titles, more than a hundred of them, set out in order of rank from the top official down."

## 19. Kushim tablet
Verdict: Wrong (29,086); name claim Overstated.

Sources: http://cdli.earth/artifacts/5340 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushim_(Uruk_period)
Replacement: "One tablet records the receipt of 28,086 measures of barley over thirty-seven months. That is about 135,000 litres, which would fill four road tankers. It carries the mark Kushim, the oldest name in writing. It stands on the accounts of an official who handled barley for a brewery, not on a king's monument." (Drops "a man" and "belongs to".)

## 20. Rebus: ti = arrow and life
Verdict: Confirmed for the sound-play; timing Overstated.
- Sumerian ti (arrow) and til/ti (life) were homophones; the arrow sign was used to write "life" (Nin-ti, "lady of life"/"lady of the rib" in the Enki and Ninhursag myth). But Wikipedia (Proto-cuneiform): rebus appears in proto-cuneiform "but rarely", the system essentially non-phonetic; the arrow-for-life rebus is an Early Dynastic/Sumerian-literature-era example. Uruk-era examples: the "hoe" sign as a rebus for "adult male" (Englund), and KU-SIM in Kushim (Wikipedia calls the tablet one of the first examples of rebus).
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI_(cuneiform) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform ; http://cdli.earth/cdli-tablet/327
Replacement: "The clerks solved this with a trick that children still use in puzzles: a sign could stand for its sound. In their language the word for 'hoe' sounded like the word for 'man'..." (if the author wants a confirmed Uruk-era example) or keep ti with "In time, in their language, the word for 'arrow' and the word for 'life' sounded the same, so the arrow sign could write 'life' in a name."

## 21. Counting in sixties; 60 minutes and 60 seconds
Verdict: Confirmed (with an indirect line of descent).
- Sexagesimal originated with the Sumerians (3rd millennium BC; proto-cuneiform signs for 60, 600, 3600 in the 4th millennium); 60 has twelve divisors (halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths); weight units divided by 60. The hour/minute/second division came through Hellenistic astronomers (Ptolemy), al-Biruni (c. 1000 AD) and Sacrobosco (c. 1235). "Descend from this" is fair; "the clerks counted in sixties" is fine.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexagesimal
No change needed.

## 22. "About a thousand signs"
Verdict: Overstated / Disputed.
- Unicode proto-cuneiform proposal (2019, based on Green & Nissen 1987): "approximately 800 separate signs, including nearly 100 numerical signs and sign variants." Wikipedia gives about 2,000 known signs (350 numerical, 1,100 ideographic, 600 complex), and the difference depends on how variants are counted.
Sources: https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2019/19284-proto-cuneiform.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform

## 23. Interest rates; debt bondage
Verdict: Rates Confirmed (Old Babylonian); sequence Could not verify.
- Old Babylonian (c. 1900-1600 BC): barley loans at one-third (33 1/3%), silver at 20% (Hudson; Leemans; arXiv paper on compound interest: 60+40 sila per gur). Silver at 20% doubled in five years. These rates are attested about a thousand years after Uruk and some centuries after Urukagina; "later records" is accurate, and the chapter's 3 sacks -> 4 is exactly 33 1/3%.
- Pledging field, then children, then self: not confirmed in the sources I retrieved (the results covered rates, not pledge sequence). Debt bondage mitigated by royal debt cancellations is attested (Hudson; Rozenberg Quarterly).
Sources: https://michael-hudson.com/2000/03/how-interest-rates-were-set-2500-bc-1000-ad/ ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.00330 ; https://rozenbergquarterly.com/?p=38007

(instead of "Later records") and keep the sequence as a general description: "A farmer could lose his land and his family without ever meeting a soldier" can stay; or "Some borrowers pledged their fields, then their children, and then themselves" (documented in later contracts; I did not retrieve a source for the exact order).

## 24. Urukagina (Uruinimgina) of Lagash
Verdict: Mostly Confirmed; four details need fixing.
- Date: c. 2350 BC (Wikipedia, Lagash/Urukagina: c. 2378-2368 BC). "About 4,350 years ago" fits.
- Boats / cattle: Kramer's translation: "He removed the inspector of the boatmen from the boats. He removed the cattle inspector from the cattle, large and small." Frayne's RIME (CDLI): the chief boatman had the boats; the reform removed boats from his control; the head shepherd had the donkeys and sheep for his own benefit and was removed. Confirmed.
- White sheep: RIME/Milestone: shepherds of wool-bearing sheep had to pay silver in place of a "pure(?)" / white sheep (and a lamb); the reform removed the silver obligation and the officers collecting it. So the fee was on shepherds, not "a man who sheared a white sheep paid the palace." Overstated.
- Divorce: Kramer's History Begins at Sumer (as quoted): "If a man divorced his wife, the ishakku [ruler] got five shekels, and his vizier got one shekel"; the reform ended it. Not in Frayne's RIME gloss (CDLI) or the Course Bible translation I retrieved, so this is Kramer's reading of a different cone; Confirmed as Kramer, not universal.
- Burial: CDLI/RIME interlinear (lines 93-116): the undertaker took seven jugs of beer, 420 loaves, 2 barig of barley, a woollen garment, a lead goat and a bed; the reform cut it to three jugs of beer, eighty loaves and 3 ban of barley. Course Bible: "seven jars of beer and 420 loaves of bread" -> "three jars of beer and eighty loaves". Numbers Confirmed.
- "From the border of Ningirsu to the sea": RIME: "From the border territory of Ningirsu to the waters of the sea" — the officials under that formula are "court officers" (CDLI gloss; the reform says no one shall serve as such in that area). Kramer's popular rendering: "From the borders of Ningirsu to the sea, there was the tax collector." The Sumerian word is translated differently by different editors. The chapter follows Kramer's version.
- Cuts, removal of inspectors, cancelled debts, freed debt slaves: Kramer's account (in History Begins at Sumer); the CDLI text has ama-gi4-bi glossed "their remission (of obligations)". Recent scholarship questions whether these were truly implemented or "reforms" at all ("Reevaluating the So-called 'Reforms of Urukagina'").
- Amargi: ama ("mother") + gi4 ("return"): "return to mother" Confirmed as literal. Earliest use: Wikipedia (Ama-gi) says the earliest known use is in a decree of Enmetena of Lagash (restoring "the child to his mother and the mother to her child"); several libertarian/popular sources credit Urukagina. Kramer: "first known written reference to freedom," but "we still do not know why this figure of speech came to be used for 'freedom'." By the Third Dynasty of Ur it was a legal term for freeing individuals. So the chapter's "The word that his clerks used is the oldest word for freedom we know" is accurate about the word, but the implication that it begins with him is Wrong.
- Lagash "some way east of Uruk": Lagash is east of Uruk; gazetteers put Uruk to Eridu ~66 km; Lagash is roughly 70+ km east. Fine.
Sources: https://cdli.earth/artifacts/431154 ; https://coursebible.com/world-history/the-reforms-of-urukagina ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urukagina ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ama-gi ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagash ; https://www.milestonedocuments.com/documents/view/reform-edict-of-urukagina/text (unreachable on retrieval; cited via search summary) ; http://historybeginsatsumer.blogspot.com/
Replacements:
(a) "A shepherd had to pay the palace silver in place of a sheep." for the white-sheep line.
(b) "From the border of the god Ningirsu's land to the sea, it says, officials had their hands out." (or use the Kramer sentence with attribution: "From one end of the land to the other, wrote the first translator, Samuel Noah Kramer, there was the tax collector.")
(c) "The word that his clerks used for this, amargi, is the oldest word for freedom that we know. An earlier ruler of Lagash, Enmetena, had used it first, in a decree that restored the child to its mother." (Literal: "return to mother".)
(d) Leave divorce in only if attributed to Kramer: "A man who divorced his wife had to pay the ruler."

## 25. Reign under ten years; Lugalzagesi sacked Lagash and burned temples
Verdict: Overstated (reign length); sack Confirmed.
- Reign: Wikipedia c. 2378-2368 BC, "10- or 11-year"; the main reform version dates to no later than his 2nd year; a popular summary puts Lugalzagesi's crippling blow in his 7th year. So "less than ten years" is at best borderline.
- Lugalzagesi of Umma devastated Lagash, especially its religious sites; the lament for Lagash blames Lugalzagesi for the sin against Nisaba and describes burning/plundering of temples; Urukagina moved to Girsu and styled himself "King of Girsu"; Lugalzagesi later ruled from Uruk and was defeated by Sargon of Akkad. "Took Lagash for itself" Confirmed (Lagash article: Lugalzagesi ended the First Dynasty).
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urukagina ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagash ; https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/urukagina (search summary only; page not reachable)
Replacement: "He ruled for about ten years. Then a neighbouring city marched in, burned his temples and took Lagash for itself."
