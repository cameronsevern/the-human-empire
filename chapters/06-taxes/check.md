## Factual flags

“Major” means the problem affects the chapter’s explanation or a substantial historical claim. “Minor” means a local detail or an overstatement. Related sentences are grouped.

1. **Major — The opening presents a disputed explanation as a fact.**  
   > “Because grain cannot be hidden, and because the people who grew it had nowhere else to go.”

   Grain can be hidden; standing cereal crops are comparatively easy to observe, assess and appropriate. Farmers also migrated and escaped. **Correct fact:** grain’s properties and constraints on mobility could facilitate extraction, but they do not constitute a settled explanation of taxation’s origins. This is broadly James C. Scott’s interpretation, which specialists treat as debatable. [Archaeological review](https://ajaonline.org/book-review/3781/)

2. **Minor — The bowl’s ubiquity and manufacture are overstated.**  
   > “The commonest thing that comes out of the ground in the world's oldest cities is a bowl, and it is an ugly one.”  
   > “This bowl, by contrast, was pressed into shape by hand in a few seconds, baked, used and thrown away.”

   The first sentence generalises a characteristic of particular Uruk-period assemblages to the world’s earliest cities. The second supplies an unestablished production time and universal disposable lifecycle. **Correct fact:** bevel-rimmed bowls were mass-produced and extremely abundant at many Uruk-period sites; manufacture and function remain debated. [Research on manufacture and use](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0026265X17309001)

3. **Major — Uniform bowls containing a daily ration are not established.**  
   > “They are all roughly the same size.”  
   > “Each one probably held a day's food for one worker.”

   Capacities vary substantially, which is one objection to the standard-ration interpretation. **Correct fact:** ration distribution is an influential hypothesis; bread moulds and other food uses have also been proposed. “Probably” does not adequately communicate the disagreement when the chapter builds its opening argument on this interpretation. [Potts’s assessment](https://www.academia.edu/1898204/Potts_2009_Bevel_rim_bowls_and_bakeries_Evidence_and_explanations_from_Iran_and_the_Indo_Iranian_borderlands_JCS_61_)

4. **Major — The bowls cannot establish the workers’ occupations, exclusive dependence or food.**  
   > “Those people were builders, weavers, potters, porters and priests, and they did not grow their own food.”  
   > “The barley in their bowls had been grown by farmers, and the farmers had handed it over.”

   This moves from an archaeological object to a detailed social arrangement without acknowledging the inferential steps. **Correct fact:** institutional provisioning existed, but the bowls do not demonstrate that every recipient was a full-time nonfarmer or that their contents were barley. Residues at Shakhi Kora indicate meat or marrow-based dishes. [Excavation research](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B4346D8FD24BB63BA2B58B236FEB28CD/S0003598X24001893a.pdf/there-and-back-again-local-institutions-an-uruk-expansion-and-the-rejection-of-centralisation-in-the-sirwanupper-diyala-region.pdf)

5. **Major — An unverifiable historical first.**  
   > “For the first time, a large number of people were working to feed strangers, by way of a storehouse that they did not control.”

   Neither “first” nor the producers’ lack of control can be established from this evidence. **Correct fact:** Uruk-period evidence reveals institutional collection and distribution on a large scale; it does not identify humanity’s first provisioning of strangers.

6. **Major — Five thousand tax-free years are asserted without evidence.**  
   > “People were growing wheat and barley in the Middle East for about five thousand years before anybody collected a share of it.”  
   > “Nevertheless, as far as anyone can tell, a farmer's harvest went into his own storeroom and stayed there.”  
   > “Nobody came to the door with a measuring jar.”

   Absence of written tax records cannot establish absence of dues, tribute or redistribution. Storage was not uniformly private: early Neolithic granaries include likely communal facilities. **Correct fact:** farming preceded documented state fiscal systems by millennia; the earliest arrangements for collecting shares are unknown. [Neolithic storage evidence](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19549877/)

7. **Major — Mobility is made both universal and a necessary condition for taxation.**  
   > “Above all, they can do what hunters had always done about a bully, which is to pack up and move a day's walk away from him.”  
   > “He also needs farmers who cannot leave.”  
   > “For five thousand years, hardly anyone had both.”

   Hunters did not always have accessible, unoccupied alternatives, and tax systems can survive despite emigration. **Correct fact:** the costs of leaving affect rulers’ ability to extract resources; complete immobility is unnecessary. The five-thousand-year claim is unsupported.

8. **Minor — Cassava’s resistance to taxation becomes an absolute.**  
   > “Nobody can tell by looking how much is there.”  
   > “If a collector wants a share, he will have to dig it up himself, plant by plant.”

   Yield can be estimated, and collectors can require farmers to harvest or provide other payments. **Correct fact:** cassava’s flexible harvesting and rapid deterioration after lifting make direct collection less convenient than dry-grain taxation; they do not make it impossible. [FAO on cassava storage](https://www.fao.org/4/x5415e/x5415e04.htm)

9. **Major — The grain/state correlation is presented as confirmation of causation.**  
   > “So the root farmer is hard to tax, and the grain farmer is easy. The record bears this out.”

   The examples establish an association, not that crop properties caused states or taxation. **Correct fact:** cereals supported many early states, but political organisation, labour control, livestock and other resources also mattered. [Research on livestock and state finance](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/political-economy-of-livestock-in-early-states/A94F5FBA63DE00BCE461E86D13F4FD89)

10. **Major — Agricultural productivity and population growth are insufficiently supported.**  
    > “A family that cut a ditch from the river to a field could harvest several times what the same work earned in the rainy hills to the north.”  
    > “Their numbers grew as no farming people's had before.”

    The first conflates yield per area with return per unit of labour; the second claims an unprecedented growth rate without comparative demographic evidence. **Correct fact:** southern irrigated farming supported productive agriculture and major urban growth, but neither comparison is established here.

11. **Major — Southern Iraq is incorrectly reduced to irrigated fields surrounded by lifeless desert.**  
    > “Beyond the last ditch there is nothing but desert.”  
    > “A family that walked away from its village did not arrive at an empty valley. It arrived at a place where nothing grows.”

    This contradicts the chapter’s own marshes and fisheries and misrepresents the ancient landscape. **Correct fact:** southern Mesopotamia contained shifting waterways, wetlands, grazing areas and diverse subsistence opportunities. Leaving could be costly without being impossible. [Environmental account](https://assets.cambridge.org/97810093/32965/excerpt/9781009332965_excerpt.pdf)

12. **Major — The drying-and-concentration sequence is disputed.**  
    > “After about six thousand years ago, the climate turned drier. The rivers shrank into fewer channels, and people crowded together along the ones that were left.”

    This is a particular explanatory model, not a secure regional sequence. **Correct fact:** climate and settlement histories vary geographically; a 2025 synthesis places early urban development within increasing moisture, reaching potentially peak conditions around 5,500 years ago. [Climate synthesis](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10213310/)

13. **Major — The emergence of religious and political authority is made artificially sequential.**  
    > “Everything was ready, and what came next was not a king. It was a god.”

    This implies that the preceding conditions are sufficient and that temple authority demonstrably preceded political rulership. **Correct fact:** religious, administrative and political institutions developed together in ways that cannot be reduced to this sequence; Uruk evidence includes ruler imagery as well as cult institutions. [Overview of the Uruk phenomenon](https://academic.oup.com/book/37433/chapter-abstract/331532053)

14. **Minor — Eridu’s archaeological sequence is narrated more confidently than its interpretation permits.**  
    > “Archaeologists digging down through the mound found eighteen shrines stacked one upon another, built over more than two thousand years.”

    Eighteen levels should not automatically become eighteen securely identified shrines. **Correct fact:** excavators identified a long building sequence traditionally interpreted as successive temples; the earliest structures’ cultic identification is less secure than that of later monumental buildings. [Architectural reference](https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/39372/1/17.pdf)

15. **Major — The shrine-to-tax-system story is conjecture.**  
    > “Nobody planned that growth, any more than anybody had planned farming.”  
    > “Each generation gave its god a slightly better house than the last.”  
    > “The keepers must eat, so the god needs a share of the harvest.”

    Archaeology cannot establish nobody’s intentions, improvement every generation, or this particular causal progression. **Correct fact:** rebuilding and increasing monumentality are observable; how contributions, priesthoods and institutional estates developed must be reconstructed rather than narrated as witnessed events.

16. **Major — A much later myth is projected backwards.**  
    > “The people of the plain had a story to explain why this was right.”

    The ensuing strike-of-the-gods narrative is *Atrahasis*, known from the second millennium BCE, long after the emergence of Uruk institutions. **Correct fact:** later Mesopotamian literature explains humanity as labourers for the gods; it cannot directly establish what sixth- or fourth-millennium farmers believed. [Museum dating](https://www.themorgan.org/seals-and-tablets/225906)

17. **Minor — The myth’s creation scene is incomplete.**  
    > “So the great gods made human beings out of clay, to do the digging in their place.”

    In *Atrahasis*, clay is mixed with a slain god’s flesh and blood. **Correct fact:** humanity is made from clay and divine material to relieve the gods’ labour. [Translation](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/anet/104-106-the-epic-of-atrahasis/)

18. **Major — Religious ideology is treated as the farmer’s own judgment.**  
    > “A farmer who carried his barley to the temple was not being robbed. He was doing what people were for.”

    The sentence asserts acceptance that the evidence cannot establish. **Correct fact:** temple ideology could portray agricultural contributions as service owed to gods; individual farmers’ responses are largely inaccessible.

19. **Minor — Uruk’s wall needs separate dating.**  
    > “By five thousand years ago, Uruk was the largest town there had ever been, with tens of thousands of people and a wall nine kilometres round.”

    Population growth and the surviving wall should not be treated as a single securely dated event. **Correct fact:** Uruk was an exceptionally large late-fourth-millennium city; its approximately nine-kilometre wall belongs to the transition into, or early part of, the third millennium, with chronology requiring care. [Site chronology](https://whc.unesco.org/document/169030)

20. **Major — The entire institutional economy is assigned to Inanna’s household.**  
    > “The biggest of the gods' households was at Uruk, a day or two's walk from Eridu.”  
    > “By then the household was vast. It owned fields, flocks, fishing boats and workshops.”

    The superlative and the unified ownership model exceed what can securely be reconstructed. **Correct fact:** Uruk had large institutions managing resources and workers; identifying every relevant building, archive and economic activity with one temple estate is problematic. [Discussion of institutional economies](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/worlds-of-the-indian-ocean/birth-of-the-state/B1C0C980A44DFF3EC38C4FFBBA0F7D12)

21. **Major — Universal labour dues and bowl-based payments are asserted.**  
    > “It also took labour, since each family owed a number of days each year to dig the canals, bring in the god's harvest or carry bricks.”  
    > “In return, it handed out rations: barley, oil, wool and beer, measured out by the bowl.”

    There is no demonstrated universal annual obligation for every Uruk family here. Wool was not distributed as a bowlful. **Correct fact:** institutions organised dependent and compulsory labour and distributed provisions and textiles using different accounting and measurement systems. [Early accounting evidence](https://cdli.earth/files-up/publications/englund2004a.pdf)

22. **Major — The account of female captivity is too categorical.**  
    > “The temple's cloth was woven by women in large workshops, and many of them were captives.”  
    > “The slaves came from the hills, where the raiding parties went to get them.”

    The meanings of early labour classifications and the status and origins of individual workers are not uniformly recoverable. A sign’s association with mountains or foreign lands cannot establish every worker’s route into dependency. **Correct fact:** female dependent labour and captivity are important, but the proportions and acquisition histories require qualification. [Study of early labour classifications](https://www.academia.edu/105692052/Gender_age_and_labour_organization_in_the_earliest_texts_from_Mesopotamia_and_Iran_c_3300_2900_BC_)

23. **Major — Possible famine relief becomes an established service.**  
    > “It fed people in bad years, and it took from them in every year, and they had no way of refusing.”

    Earlier, the storehouse merely *can* feed flood victims; here that possibility becomes a demonstrated practice, coupled with an absolute about compliance. **Correct fact:** institutional storage and provisioning are documented; systematic relief for the wider farming population needs specific evidence.

24. **Minor — The bandit model is overgeneralised.**  
    > “One who raids a village once takes everything, since he will not be back.”  
    > “The villagers are better off under him than under a string of raiders, and that is as much as can be said for him.”

    These outcomes follow only under assumptions about extraction, violence and protection. **Correct fact:** a settled ruler may have incentives to preserve future production; this is an explanatory model, not a universal historical progression or guaranteed improvement.

25. **Major — Rejection of hydraulic determinism becomes a different unsupported certainty.**  
    > “That view has not survived the evidence.”  
    > “Irrigation did not create the storehouse. The storehouse arrived first, and built the big canals later, for its own fields.”

    Evidence against irrigation *requiring despotic central government* does not establish this single reverse sequence. **Correct fact:** small-scale irrigation preceded states; relationships between larger irrigation networks, institutions and urbanisation were more complex. [Archaeological analysis](https://open.uapress.arizona.edu/read/irrigation-s-impact-on-society/section/f70e97e4-0f77-42ff-81e4-efeaa59b4857)

26. **Major — Flight records are turned into a collective verdict and an established purpose of walls.**  
    > “Meanwhile, the people themselves have left a verdict.”  
    > “A city wall kept raiders out. It also helped to keep the taxpayers in.”

    Administrative records about fugitives are not the population speaking collectively. Taxpayer confinement is an interpretation, not an established general function of these walls. **Correct fact:** institutions attempted to retain labour, and people escaped; walls also provided protection. [Assessment of the coercion argument](https://ajaonline.org/book-review/3781/)

27. **Major — The conclusion contradicts the evidence of escape.**  
    > “It lasted because, on that plain, they could not leave.”

    This repeats a demonstrably excessive claim immediately after describing runaways. **Correct fact:** coercion and the costs of departure could sustain institutions, but departure remained possible.

28. **Major — The token-to-writing sequence is presented as undisputed.**  
    > “For thousands of years, the farmers of the Middle East had kept count with small clay tokens.”  
    > “A cone stood for a small measure of grain, and a ball stood for a large one.”  
    > “Then somebody noticed that if the marks were on the outside, the tokens inside were not needed.”

    The broad reconstruction owes much to Schmandt-Besserat; the functions and continuity of Neolithic objects and the meanings assigned to particular shapes are contested. The moment of discovery is invented. **Correct fact:** some tokens and clay envelopes clearly relate to accounting and numerical notation; a uniform system extending back through the Neolithic is not established. [Specialist assessment](https://is.muni.cz/el/1421/jaro2013/PAPVA_43/um/Englund_1998.pdf)

29. **Major — Counting without numbers is misleading.**  
    > “It was a way of counting for people who had no numbers.”

    Lack of written numerals does not imply lack of numbers or number words. **Correct fact:** tokens could represent quantities before writing developed. [Research on early numeracy](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hm.2020.08.002)

30. **Minor — The envelope does not eliminate fraud.**  
    > “Nobody can cheat without breaking the clay.”

    It protects a sealed record against certain alterations, not against dishonest initial entries or other fraud. **Correct fact:** an intact authenticated envelope could help verify that its enclosed counters had not been changed.

31. **Major — Earliest evidence is mistaken for a known invention event.**  
    > “That pad is the first written tablet, and it appeared at Uruk about five thousand three hundred years ago.”

    Numerical tablets, proto-writing and language-recording writing are distinct stages; “first” cannot be assigned to this narrated moment. **Correct fact:** Uruk preserves some of the earliest proto-cuneiform tablets, dating to the late fourth millennium BCE, following earlier accounting devices. [Writing’s early development](https://cdli.earth/articles/cdlj/2006-1)

32. **Minor — Scribal lists are described too narrowly.**  
    > “The remainder are lists of signs for apprentice clerks to copy.”

    These are organised lexical lists of words, categories and professions, not simply sign-copying sheets; their uses were broader than the sentence establishes. **Correct fact:** a minority of the earliest texts are lexical lists associated with classification and scribal learning. [Corpus introduction](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dcclt/intro/arch_intro.html)

33. **Minor — Several written genres are assigned one arrival date.**  
    > “There are no prayers, no letters, no laws and no histories. Those came some seven hundred years later.”

    These genres have different earliest attestations, and legal records are not interchangeable with law collections. **Correct fact:** literary and other extended texts emerge over the third millennium; there was no single seven-hundred-year transition for everything listed. [Study of early legal writing](https://www.academia.edu/105026498/AT_THE_DAWN_OF_LEGAL_HISTORY_TEACHING_LAW_IN_ANCIENT_MESOPOTAMIA)

34. **Major — Accounting is reduced to debt.**  
    > “Writing was not invented to record what people thought or felt. It was invented to record what they owed.”

    Early accounts also recorded inventories, allocations, production and transactions; lexical lists were present early. **Correct fact:** administration was central to the earliest surviving Mesopotamian writing. Neither exclusive purpose nor the inventors’ intentions can be established. [Early text corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dcclt/intro/arch_intro.html)

35. **Minor — The Kushim quantity and transaction description need correction.**  
    > “One tablet records the receipt of 29,086 measures of barley over thirty-seven months.”

    The current CDLI edition renders the quantity as 5,617⅕ N1 units, approximately 135,000 litres; the familiar popular conversion is 28,086 smaller measures, not 29,086. “Receipt” also overspecifies an account interpreted as exchange or delivery accounting. **Correct fact:** retain approximately 135,000 litres accounted for over 37 months. [Tablet edition](https://cdli.earth/artifacts/5340)

36. **Major — Kushim becomes a securely identified individual.**  
    > “It is probably the oldest personal name that we can read.”  
    > “It belongs to a man who looked after barley for a brewery.”

    “Probably” qualifies oldest, but not whether Kushim is a person at all. **Correct fact:** KU.ŠIM is conventionally read “Kushim” and may identify an administrator; interpretation as a personal name rather than an office or institution is uncertain. [Discussion of the archaic texts](https://is.muni.cz/el/1421/jaro2013/PAPVA_43/um/Englund_1998.pdf)

37. **Major — Taxation is assigned sole responsibility for phonetic writing.**  
    > “Names, in fact, were what pushed writing forward.”  
    > “Because a tax needs names, writing learned to record speech.”

    Personal names were important to the development of phonetic notation, but accounts need not be taxes, and this exclusive causal chain is unproved. **Correct fact:** sound-based signs expanded what writing could represent, including names and eventually connected language. [Account of names and phonetic signs](https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/from-accounting-to-writing/)

38. **Minor — The rebus example and its consequences are simplified too far.**  
    > “In their language, the word for ‘arrow’ and the word for ‘life’ sounded the same.”  
    > “Once a sign could stand for a sound, any name could be spelled, and in time so could any word.”

    The conventional forms are *ti* and *til*: similar, not simply identical. One sound-bearing sign did not instantly supply a complete spelling system. **Correct fact:** homophony and near-homophony supported a gradual expansion of phonetic writing. [Assyriological explanation](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/nn227.pdf)

39. **Minor — A single numerical system replaces several.**  
    > “The clerks also counted in sixties, which divide neatly into halves, thirds, quarters, fifths and sixths.”

    Sexagesimal counting existed, but archaic accounting used multiple systems depending on what was measured. **Correct fact:** base sixty was one important component; its later mathematical and astronomical descendants contributed to modern time divisions. [Numerical systems](https://cdli.earth/files-up/publications/englund2004a.pdf)

40. **Major — Illiteracy is made equivalent to having no means of challenging an account.**  
    > “He could not check it against another. He could not call on the village's memory, because the storehouse did not run on memory any longer. What the clay said was what had happened.”

    This is an invented legal situation. Written administration did not erase witnesses, oral testimony or other people able to read. **Correct fact:** specialist literacy could create unequal power; the absolute inability to challenge records is not established.

41. **Minor — Interest rates are insufficiently dated and qualified.**  
    > “Silver was lent at a fifth a year, and barley at a third.”

    These familiar rates were not universal across periods and contracts; the charging period also needs care, especially for seasonal grain loans. **Correct fact:** later Mesopotamian evidence commonly gives 20 percent on silver and 33⅓ percent on barley, with contractual and chronological variation. [Robson’s account](https://uruk-warka.dk/mathematics/robson-selin-2000.pdf)

42. **Major — Two failed harvests become inevitable lifelong insolvency.**  
    > “If that harvest failed too, he owed more than he could ever grow.”  
    > “First he pledged his field. Then he sent his children to work in the lender's house. Finally he went himself.”

    Neither the arithmetic nor a universal legal sequence supports this. **Correct fact:** harvest failure could lead to escalating debt, loss of property or dependent labour, but outcomes depended on debt size, assets, contract terms and available relief.

43. **Major — A royal reform inscription becomes a neutral report and a secure first.**  
    > “He had an account of the state of the city written out on clay, and it is the oldest complaint about taxes that survives.”

    The text legitimises Uruinimgina’s rule by contrasting his reforms with earlier abuses. “Oldest complaint about taxes” is an imprecise modern superlative. **Correct fact:** it is an early surviving royal account of abuses, fees and reforms, not an independent survey of conditions. [Penn Museum explanation](https://collections.penn.museum/collections/videos/video/1267)

44. **Major — Announced reforms become comprehensive, verified implementation.**  
    > “The new ruler announced that he had cut the fees, dismissed the inspectors and cancelled the debts.”  
    > “People who had been sold for what they owed were sent home.”

    The first sentence appropriately says “announced,” but “the debts” suggests universal cancellation, and the second states implementation outright. **Correct fact:** the inscriptions claim specified remissions and protections; their scope and actual implementation require separate evidence. [Research on implementation](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/aofo-2025-2006/html)

45. **Minor — “Freedom” needs historical definition and earlier attestation.**  
    > “The word that his clerks used for this is the oldest word for freedom that we know. Literally, it means ‘return to mother’.”

    *Ama-gi* is already attested under the earlier ruler Enmetena. Its relevant meaning is release or remission, not necessarily freedom in the broad modern sense. **Correct fact:** this early Sumerian term, conventionally explained as “return to mother,” described restoration or release from obligations. [Study of early remission edicts](https://www.divlaw.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:fdbda831-078c-450f-b850-066c1e864b42/Johnson,%20Lawgiving,%20Manuscript%20Version.pdf)

46. **Minor — The reign and conquest are too neatly closed.**  
    > “He ruled for less than ten years. Then a neighbouring city marched in, burned his temples and took Lagash for itself.”

    His chronology and continuing authority at Girsu complicate this simple end-of-reign sequence. **Correct fact:** Lugalzagesi’s forces devastated Lagash’s territory; Uruinimgina’s later title and position suggest a more complicated contraction of power. [Historical discussion](https://www.divlaw.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:fdbda831-078c-450f-b850-066c1e864b42/Johnson,%20Lawgiving,%20Manuscript%20Version.pdf)

47. **Major — Earlier warfare is reduced to small dawn raids.**  
    > “They quarrelled over fields and over water, and their quarrels were no longer the dawn raids of hunters.”

    Hunting societies’ warfare was not uniformly brief raiding, and organised conflict preceded these city-states. **Correct fact:** institutional provisioning helped support larger military forces and campaigns; it did not invent sustained or organised violence.

## Clarity flags

1. **The central term is never defined.**  
   > “The barley in their bowls had been grown by farmers, and the farmers had handed it over.”

   The chapter treats taxes, offerings, rent, estate production, tribute, labour obligations and loan repayments as interchangeable. A general reader needs to know which transaction counts as a tax and why.

2. **Explicit signposting conflicts with the style sheet.**  
   > “Compare two farmers.”  
   > “The second thing was still missing.”  
   > “Everything was ready, and what came next was not a king. It was a god.”

   These announce the comparison, missing explanation or coming revelation instead of allowing the next concrete scene to supply it.

3. **The main premise is repeated too often.**  
   > “The farmers of the Middle East had grown grain for five thousand years and kept it.”  
   > “These farmers had a crop that could be counted and a home that they could not leave.”  
   > “It lasted because, on that plain, they could not leave.”

   Together with the opening and the collector’s two requirements, these repeatedly restate the same argument.

4. **“Storehouse” changes meaning without warning.**  
   > “Irrigation did not create the storehouse.”  
   > “The storehouse arrived first, and built the big canals later, for its own fields.”

   A building has become an institution, landowner and government. The metaphor obscures who actually makes decisions.

5. **The hypothetical bandit risks becoming historical evidence.**  
   > “He has become a ruler.”

   The surrounding discussion moves between excavated institutions and an imagined model without clearly distinguishing them.

6. **The story’s position in time becomes hard to follow.**  
   > “Later records show the usual price.”

   “Later” conceals a potentially substantial chronological jump. The chapter moves among early farming, Uruk institutions, later literary evidence and later lending practices without consistently identifying the transition.

7. **The barley example violates “one number per point” and “round it.”**  
   > “One tablet records the receipt of 29,086 measures of barley over thirty-seven months. That is about 135,000 litres, which would fill four road tankers.”

   Four quantities compete for attention. The litres-to-tankers comparison already conveys the scale.

8. **“Measures” has no usable meaning.**  
   > “One tablet records the receipt of 29,086 measures of barley over thirty-seven months.”

   The reader cannot tell whether a measure is a bowl, jar, sack or larger unit. The subsequent conversion does not explain the original unit.

9. **Temperature lacks its unit.**  
   > “In summer the heat passes forty-five degrees.”

   Specify Celsius.

10. **Several terms are not explained on first appearance.**  
    > “Once it is cut and threshed…”  
    > “From her precinct comes a stone vase…”

    “Threshed” and “precinct” are avoidable obstacles for the intended reader. “Flax” also arrives without explaining its practical significance.

11. **The farmer’s alleged exclusion from oral evidence is confusing even internally.**  
    > “He could not call on the village's memory, because the storehouse did not run on memory any longer.”

    Why would an institution’s use of writing prevent neighbours or witnesses remembering a delivery? The causal link is missing.

12. **Freedom’s key word is omitted.**  
    > “The word that his clerks used for this is the oldest word for freedom that we know.”

    The passage explains and translates a word without ever giving it. This is a case where the historical term would help rather than burden the reader.

13. **The final reformer remains unnecessarily anonymous.**  
    > “About four thousand three hundred and fifty years ago, the city of Lagash, some way east of Uruk, got a new ruler.”

    He carries several paragraphs and the closing historical episode. Naming him would satisfy the style sheet’s allowance for people the reader meets repeatedly.

14. **The conclusion summarises and previews rather than landing on the scene.**  
    > “The gods' households had learned how to gather the work of ten thousand families into one place and write it down.”  
    > “What remained to be settled was who would command the men that all that barley could feed.”

    The first is a recap; the second abstracts away from the concrete destruction of Lagash to announce a coming struggle.

15. **The prohibition on brittle absolutes is repeatedly broken.**  
    Examples include “Nobody came to the door,” “nothing but desert,” “Nobody planned that growth,” “Nobody can cheat,” and “more than he could ever grow.” These are also substantive factual problems, as detailed above.

There are **no explicit chapter self-references, body cross-references or hedging stacks**. The headings, short paragraphs and generally plain vocabulary mostly follow the style sheet.

## Overall

Vivid, readable and well organised around bowls, grain and clay. It needs substantial factual revision before publication: the main weakness is turning plausible models into certain history, especially about immobility, temple control and writing’s origins. Preserve the concrete objects; rebuild the causal claims around what the evidence actually establishes. No files were edited.
