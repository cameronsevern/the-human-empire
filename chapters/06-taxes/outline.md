# Chapter 6 — Why do we pay taxes?

**Line under the title:** Southern Iraq, from about 7,400 years ago to about 4,300 years ago.
**Length:** 4,000 to 5,000 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Carry the thought across each section join.

The movements are the story, not section headings. Facts get a lookup pass after drafting.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Farming villages lasted more than four thousand years with nobody collecting anything. Taxes began when two things came together. The first was a crop that a collector can see, measure, store and carry, which is grain. The second was a people who could not walk away, on irrigated land with desert on every side. In southern Iraq a god's storehouse grew, over two thousand years, from a shrine the size of a garden shed into a household that fed tens of thousands of strangers. It gave something back, and it also took what it liked from people with no way to refuse. Running it needed a memory bigger than any head, so its accountants invented writing. Writing was a tool for counting what was owed long before it was a way to tell a story.

## The story

1. **The ugliest bowl.** Answer first. Then the crude throwaway bowl found by the thousand in the oldest cities: a day's ration. Somebody was feeding thousands of people who grew nothing.

2. **Four thousand years without a collector.** Farming alone did not bring rulers. Villages had granaries and no taxman. Two things were missing.

3. **The crop that cannot hide.** Why grain can be taxed and roots cannot. Almost every early state lived on a grain. New Guinea farmed for thousands of years and had no state.

4. **The land between the rivers.** Southern Iraq: no rain, no stone, no timber, and the richest soil on Earth if water is led to it. Leave the canal and you are in the desert. The hunters' old answer to a bully, walking away, was gone.

5. **The god's household.** Eridu's shrine rebuilt on one spot for two thousand years. The belief that people were made to do the gods' work. The carved vase from Uruk that shows the whole system in five bands. Uruk itself. Rations, labour duty, weavers, and the word for a slave woman.

6. **The bandit who stayed.** Was it a service or a racket? What the taxpayer got. Why the canals did not need a ruler. Runaways.

7. **Memory made of mud.** A city of strangers cannot run on memory. Tokens, the sealed clay ball, the tablet. Nine tablets in ten are accounts. The org chart. Kushim. How names forced writing to record sounds. Who could read, and what a farmer could do if the count was wrong.

8. **Return to mother.** What can be counted can be owed: interest, debt, and people sold for it. The first recorded complaint about tax collectors, at Lagash, and the oldest word for freedom. End on a dozen walled cities with full storehouses, and the men those storehouses could feed.
