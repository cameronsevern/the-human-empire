## Factual flags

1. **Major — The opening contradicts the chapter’s later account of departure.**

   > “Because grain cannot be hidden, and because the people who grew it had nowhere else to go.”

   Later, families can leave to fish or herd sheep, and workers run away. “Nowhere else to go” contradicts those passages. Harvested grain can also be concealed; standing cereal crops are comparatively visible. **Correct fact:** grain was relatively easy to assess and collect, while leaving irrigated farmland could be costly. Neither required literal impossibility. Sources: the chapter’s own “The land between the rivers” and “The bandit who stayed”; [research on concealed household grain storage](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236673285_Private_pantries_and_celebrated_surplus_Storing_and_sharing_food_at_Neolithic_Catalhoyuk_Central_Anatolia).

2. **Minor — The oldest bowls are older than stated.**

   > “They turn up by the thousand, from Iran to Syria, and the oldest are about five and a half thousand years old.”

   Bevelled-rim bowls occur at Shakhi Kora in contexts dated approximately 3780–3648 BCE, with examples in an earlier occupation phase too. **Correct fact:** examples are at least about 5,700–5,800 years old; “nearly six thousand years” would accommodate this evidence. Source: [Glatz and colleagues, excavation report, phases 2–3](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/483911388/AAMGlatzEtal2024AThereAndBackAgain.pdf).

3. **Minor — The proposed contents exclude foods identified by analysis.**

   > “Each one probably held a worker's ration, either of grain or of bread baked in the bowl itself.”

   The ration interpretation can stand, but the grain-or-bread restriction conflicts with residue evidence. **Correct fact:** these bowls also held meat- or marrow-based foods, with possible dairy contents. That supports food provisioning without making every bowl evidence of cereal distribution. Source: [Perruchini and colleagues, *Revealing invisible stews*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X22003935).

4. **Major — Receiving rations does not mean someone was not a farmer.**

   > “It also means that those people were not growing their own food, or they would have had no need of a ration.”

   This directly contradicts the later description of farmers performing institutional labour and receiving rations in return. Farmers could receive meals while working away from their own holdings. **Correct fact:** provisioning demonstrates that an institution supplied food, not that recipients never grew food themselves. Source: the chapter’s own “The god’s household,” beginning “Farmers owed days of work each year.”

5. **Major — Early storage was not uniformly confined to individual households.**

   > “Nevertheless, as far as anyone can tell, a farmer's harvest went into his own storeroom and stayed there.”

   Archaeology supplies contrary evidence: early cereal cultivators built substantial granaries outside residential buildings, interpreted by the excavators as communal storage. **Correct fact:** household storage and shared storage have different histories; the period before states cannot be described as uniformly private retention of harvests. This does **not** establish that those communal contributions were taxes. Source: [Kuijt and Finlayson’s excavation study](https://naturedocumentaries.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/Kujit-Finlayson_2009_Granaries.pdf).

6. **Minor — The tablet passage conflates numerical tablets with later writing.**

   > “The oldest come from Uruk and are about five thousand three hundred years old.”

   The preceding sentence defines the object simply as a clay tablet, and the following sentence specifies numerical marks. Numerical tablets predate the stated date: one from Tell Brak lay below a hearth dated around 3500 BCE. **Correct fact:** numerical tablets existed by approximately the middle of the fourth millennium BCE; Uruk’s early proto-cuneiform writing belongs later in that millennium. Source: [Oxford research on the earliest Mesopotamian and Iranian texts](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Aafa3362e-1182-43aa-a2b9-d675bd8c585a/files/mb14de61d14383c62ee78433ee981340a).

7. **Major — Written records did not automatically defeat remembered testimony.**

   > “In a quarrel between a farmer's memory and the clay, therefore, the clay won.”

   Illiteracy does not establish that outcome. Documented Mesopotamian legal practice relied heavily on witnesses, whose testimony could take precedence over documents. **Correct fact:** specialist literacy could disadvantage a farmer, but writing did not automatically override oral evidence. The surviving legal examples are later than Uruk and should not themselves be projected backwards as a precise Uruk procedure. Source: [Assyriologist Cécile Michel on witnesses and written evidence](https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/publications/mesopotamia/2023-06-27.html).

## Clarity flags

1. **The definition of taxation remains too broad.**

   > “Today we would call what they handed over a tax.”

   The preceding description—food delivered to a storehouse outside the producer’s control—could also describe rent or an offering. A short distinction between compulsory public dues and other transfers would clarify the chapter’s central term.

2. **The central explanation is repeated more often than it develops.**

   > “The farmers of the Middle East had grown grain for four thousand years and kept it, because they could still walk away.”

   > “These farmers had a crop that could be counted, and a home that was very hard to leave.”

   These repeat the opening and the collector’s two requirements; the end of “The bandit who stayed” returns to the same explanation again. The repetition slows an otherwise clear progression.

3. **“Storehouse” shifts between building and governing institution.**

   > “The men who could read it worked for the storehouse.”

   Across the chapter, the storehouse stores grain, employs clerks, collects dues and becomes a ruler. The metaphor is understandable initially, but eventually obscures whether temple officials, a household or political authorities are acting.

4. **Some transitions announce the explanation rather than give it.**

   > “It is easy to follow how.”

   > “One tablet shows this better than any other.”

   Both conflict with the style sheet’s prohibition on announcing that an explanation or illustrative point is coming.

5. **The reformer’s anonymity makes the final episode harder to follow.**

   > “About four thousand three hundred and fifty years ago, the city of Lagash, some way east of Uruk, got a new ruler.”

   This ruler carries several paragraphs and the concluding historical episode. Naming Urukagina would fit the style sheet’s explicit allowance for recurring people.

6. **The ending recaps and previews.**

   > “The gods' households had learned how to gather the work of ten thousand families into one place and write it down.”

   > “What remained to be settled was who would command the men that all that barley could feed.”

   The first sentence summarises; the second announces a coming struggle. Both run against the instruction to end on a concrete image or consequence rather than a recap and preview.

## Overall

The revision is clearer and fixes many earlier flags. Its chosen interpretation can remain confident, but the contradictions about mobility and ration recipients, the tablet chronology, and the claim that written records automatically prevailed still need correction. No files were edited.
