1. **Factual error — high confidence.** “Artificial intelligence is software that has learned from examples, in place of following rules that somebody wrote down.” This defines **machine learning**, a branch of AI, as the whole field. AI also includes rule-based systems—the very systems described under “A summer’s work.” The opening therefore creates an internal contradiction as well as a misleading definition. [OECD’s explanation of AI approaches](https://oecd.ai/en/inside-artificial-intelligence).

2. **Factual error — high confidence.** “It had been given the records of earlier cases and left to find the patterns for itself, and one of the patterns that it found was that an applicant who held a second nationality scored higher.” The Dutch model used nationality as an explicitly supplied risk factor; the relevant distinction was **Dutch versus non-Dutch nationality**, not simply possession of a second nationality. Unlawful retention and other uses of dual-nationality records were related but distinct practices. The sentence makes human design decisions disappear into autonomous discovery. [Amnesty’s investigation, pp. 14–16](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/EUR3546862021ENGLISH.pdf).

3. **Misleading causal account — high confidence.** “A family that the system flagged received a letter.” And: “When parents telephoned to ask what they had done wrong, the officials could not tell them, because the officials did not know either.” High scores selected applications for **manual investigation**; officials subsequently made fraud determinations. Their inability to explain a risk score is different from having no explanation for a repayment decision. The passage collapses selection, investigation and punitive recovery into one automatic action. [Amnesty’s investigation, pp. 15 and 25](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/EUR3546862021ENGLISH.pdf).

4. **Misleading statistic; mismatch with chapter 107 — high confidence.** “The buildings that house those chips already consume about one and a half per cent of the world's supply, and the companies that own them have been signing contracts to restart closed nuclear power stations.” The 1.5% figure covers **all data centres in 2024**, including ordinary cloud computing and storage. Here, following the training-chip paragraph, it reads as AI’s electricity consumption. Chapter 107 correctly introduces that figure for data centres generally. [IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary).

5. **Overstatement — high confidence.** “The whole is owned by five or six companies.” Concentration is defensible; ownership of “the whole” is not. Chip design, fabrication, cloud infrastructure and model development involve different firms, alongside public research and openly released models. Specify which market is concentrated. DeepSeek alone supplies an important example omitted from this account, despite appearing in the outline. [DeepSeek’s technical report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437).

6. **Doubtful generalisation — high confidence.** “Nearly all of the ones used for training are designed by a single American firm, which began by making cards for video games and in 2025 became the most valuable company in the world, and they are manufactured in the factories of Taiwan.” Nvidia’s dominance of AI GPUs does not establish its share of **all training chips**. Google’s TPUs and Amazon’s Trainium are substantial alternatives; Anthropic described using all three chip families in 2025. “Nearly all” needs a clearly defined denominator and evidence. [Anthropic’s October 2025 announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/expanding-our-use-of-google-cloud-tpus-and-services).

7. **Factual overgeneralisation — high confidence.** “One chip costs about as much as a car, and a large model is trained on hundreds of thousands of them.” Some exceptionally large clusters reach that scale; large models do not generally require it. DeepSeek reports training V3 on **2,048 H800 GPUs**. Distinguish the largest installations from a typical large-model training run. [DeepSeek researchers’ hardware account](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09343).

8. **Overstatement and style-breaking absolute — high confidence.** “A network with a billion weights cannot answer in any way that a person can check.” Also: “Nobody chose those weights, and nobody can read them.” Weights can be inspected; aspects of internal behaviour can be interpreted and experimentally tested. A complete, faithful explanation of a particular decision often remains difficult. That narrower claim supports the argument without declaring explanation impossible. [Anthropic’s interpretability research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/mapping-mind-language-model).

9. **Misleading benchmark account — high confidence.** “The best programs of the previous year had been wrong about one picture in four.” And: “Theirs was wrong about one in seven.” The celebrated comparison was **15.3% against 26.2% for the runner-up in the same 2012 competition**. These were *top-five* error rates: a result counted as correct if the right label appeared among five guesses. Without that explanation, readers will understand ordinary single-answer accuracy. [Original AlexNet paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Paper.pdf).

10. **Overgeneralisation — high confidence.** “Within three years such networks were making fewer mistakes than people.” This was a result on a particular ImageNet classification benchmark, compared with a reported human benchmark—not general superiority at recognising the visual world. Keep the achievement tied to the test. [2015 research paper](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_iccv_2015/html/He_Delving_Deep_into_ICCV_2015_paper.html).

11. **Unsupported historical absolute — high confidence.** “It was the move that won, and no human player in three thousand years had thought of it.” Nobody can establish what every player had considered over three millennia. DeepMind reported that its model assigned a human playing that move a probability of **one in ten thousand**. That supports “highly unexpected,” not “never previously thought of.” [DeepMind’s contemporary account](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/what-we-learned-in-seoul-with-alphago/).

12. **Historical conflation — high confidence.** “The power loom destroyed the trade of the handloom weavers, some of whom, in the English Midlands in 1811, took hammers to the new machines.” The Nottinghamshire disturbances of 1811 centred on **framework knitters smashing stocking frames**, including disputes over wages and how existing machinery was used. Attacks associated with handloom weavers and power looms belong principally to Lancashire in 1812. The current sentence joins different trades, machines and places. [National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/teaching-resources/why-did-the-luddites-protest/).

13. **Misleading legal context — high confidence.** “Authors, artists and publishers have sued, and in 2025 one of the companies agreed to pay writers a billion and a half dollars.” The amount is sound, but the context makes it look like a settlement establishing that training without permission was unlawful. The Anthropic case distinguished **training**, which the court held fair use in that case, from acquiring and retaining pirated books. The settlement covered authors and publishers. That distinction matters to the argument. [Court-authorised settlement explanation](https://www.anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com/faq%20).

14. **Overbroad claim — high confidence.** “The models were trained on books, newspapers, paintings and photographs that were copied without asking and without payment.” This treats unlicensed copying as the universal provenance of these models. Training collections also include licensed and public-domain material; Adobe’s original Firefly is a concrete counterexample. Identify the models or substantial portions of their training material to which the claim applies. [Adobe’s 2023 congressional submission](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/2023-07-12_pm_-_qfr_responses_-_rao.pdf).

15. **Unsupported causal certainty — high confidence.** “Such systems made far more mistakes with dark faces than with pale ones, because they had been trained mostly on pale ones.” Demographic disparities were documented, but their size and direction varied by algorithm and task. NIST explicitly did **not** establish their causes; training-data imbalance was a possible explanation, not a demonstrated explanation for this particular false match. [NIST’s 2019 findings](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2019/12/nist-study-evaluates-effects-race-age-sex-face-recognition-software).

16. **Misleading account of governance — high confidence.** “Little has followed.” Following Bletchley, governments established an international safety-institute network and commissioned an international scientific assessment. You can argue that these measures were inadequate, but the reader needs to know they happened. [Seoul agreement, May 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/global-leaders-agree-to-launch-first-international-network-of-ai-safety-institutes-to-boost-understanding-of-ai); [International AI Safety Report, January 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17805).

17. **Overbroad legal claim — high confidence.** “The United States withdrew its own safety rules in 2025.” Trump revoked Biden’s 2023 AI executive order and ordered review of measures implementing it. This was not withdrawal of every American AI safeguard; the replacement federal-use policy retained requirements for high-impact AI. Name the revoked order rather than treating American safety regulation as one abolished package. [Revocation implementation order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/removing-barriers-to-american-leadership-in-artificial-intelligence/); [replacement federal policy](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-21-Accelerating-Federal-Use-of-AI-through-Innovation-Governance-and-Public-Trust.pdf).

18. **Missing legal timing and scope — high confidence.** “The European Union passed a law in 2024 that bans some uses outright, such as scoring citizens for general trustworthiness, and requires that systems which make serious decisions about people be documented, tested for bias, and overseen by a human being who is able to overrule them.” The law’s requirements apply to **defined high-risk categories**, with exceptions, rather than every serious decision. Also distinguish enactment from application: under the original timetable, prohibitions applied in February 2025, while most high-risk requirements were scheduled for 2026 or later. [Original AI Act, Articles 6, 14 and 113](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng).

19. **Missing mechanism — high confidence.** “Each unit multiplies its inputs by a set of numbers called weights, adds up the results, and passes its answer to the units in the next layer, which do the same, and so on through many layers.” This omits the nonlinear transformation between layers. Multiplication and addition alone collapse into a single linear operation, however many layers are stacked. One plain sentence about a unit suppressing or transforming its result would preserve accessibility while explaining why layers matter. [*Dive into Deep Learning*](https://classic.d2l.ai/chapter_multilayer-perceptrons/mlp.html).

20. **Misleading historical continuity — high confidence.** “The first such machine was built in 1958, and a leading newspaper reported that the navy expected it to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence.” “Such” points back to a deep network trained by backpropagation. Rosenblatt’s perceptron was a much simpler system, not that machine. Distinguish the early perceptron from the multilayer training method popularised decades later. [Smithsonian’s perceptron record](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_334414); [1986 backpropagation paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/323533a0).

21. **Factual imprecision — high confidence.** “A large online retailer built a program to sort job applications, trained on the records of people whom it had hired over ten years, most of whom were men.” The original investigation describes **résumés submitted to Amazon**, not only records of people hired. That changes which selection process the example illustrates. [Reuters investigation](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/amazon-scraps-secret-ai-recruiting-tool-that-showed-bias-against-women-1637988).

22. **Reader gap and misleading chronology — high confidence.** “The shapes of two hundred million proteins were published free.” These were **predicted structures**, released at that scale in **2022**, not experimentally established shapes published in 2020. The preceding paragraph also needs a brief explanation of what a protein is and why its shape matters; otherwise the reader cannot appreciate the achievement. [EMBL-EBI’s release announcement](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/about/news/technology-and-innovation/alphafold-200-million/).

23. **Wrong description of measured work — high confidence.** “When a large company gave a language model to five thousand of its customer-service agents, they resolved about a seventh more calls an hour, and the improvement was largest among the newest and least skilled, who were in effect being handed the habits of the best.” The study concerned **text chats and issues resolved per hour**, not telephone calls. The distinction matters because agents could handle multiple chats simultaneously. The approximate improvement is supported by the original working paper. [NBER’s account](https://www.nber.org/digest/20236/measuring-productivity-impact-generative-ai).

24. **Factual compression — high confidence.** “He was fined five thousand dollars.” The court imposed a **single $5,000 penalty jointly and severally on two lawyers and their firm**, rather than a personal fine on the one lawyer described. The sanctions also concerned their subsequent false representations and persistence after the citations were challenged. [Court’s sanctions opinion](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-sd-new-yor/2335142.html).

25. **Unsupported absolute — high confidence.** “A hundred million people tried it within two months, which is faster than anything had been taken up before.” The source was an estimate of **100 million monthly active users**, described as the fastest-growing **consumer internet application** then recorded. It does not establish the fastest adoption of anything in history. [Reuters’s report of the UBS estimate](https://www.investing.com/news/economy/chatgpt-sets-record-for-fastestgrowing-user-base--analyst-note-2993773).

26. **Reader orientation.** “On 30 November 2022 a company in San Francisco put such a model on the internet with a box to type into.” The chapter never names **ChatGPT**, despite making it central to the following statistics and examples. Similarly, “a single American firm” leaves Nvidia unnamed while explaining its power. The “few names” rule permits names readers need to follow the story; suppressing these makes familiar subjects unnecessarily difficult to identify.

27. **Signposting and narrative self-reference.** “Three things were missing, and by 2012 all three had arrived.” This announces the coming explanatory list rather than stating its substance. “From this point the subject becomes prediction, and the people who know most about it disagree completely.” This explicitly marks the text’s position and announces the next section. The outline requires predictions to be distinguished from evidence, but that can be done without narrating the chapter’s structure. “Disagree completely” also overstates disagreement among overlapping positions.

I found no clear direct contradiction with chapters 06 or 109, and no hedging stacks. “This book was written with the help of one” is the outline’s expressly authorised exception, so I have not counted it as a style violation. No files were edited.
