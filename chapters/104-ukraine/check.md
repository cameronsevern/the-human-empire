I found the following issues, ordered by seriousness. I found no clear factual contradiction with chapters 78 or 100; the famine estimate and independence referendum agree. The Bosnia comparison below is a potential reader-facing inconsistency, not a direct contradiction. No files were edited.

1. **NATO membership is presented as impossible rather than politically unlikely.**  
   > “No member proposed admitting it, and everyone concerned, including Putin, knew that it would not be let in while part of its territory was disputed.”

   There was no agreement to admit Ukraine imminently, but there were advocates for advancing its membership: Lithuania’s parliament supported granting a Membership Action Plan in 2021. Territorial disputes were a factor in admission decisions, not an automatic prohibition. “Everyone … knew” also claims knowledge of people’s private assessments. The defensible point is that admission lacked allied consensus and was not imminent. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [Lithuanian parliamentary resolution](https://www.lrs.lt/sip/getFile3?p_fid=33259), [NATO’s enlargement study, paragraphs 6–7](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/1995/09/03/study-on-nato-enlargement).

2. **The Donbas death toll belongs to a different period.**  
   > “Fourteen thousand people died.”

   Following “For the next seven years,” this assigns the deaths to the period after the 2015 ceasefire. OHCHR’s estimate of 14,200–14,400 covers **April 2014–December 2021**, including the intense fighting before that ceasefire. It also includes combatants on both sides, not just civilians. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [OHCHR casualty accounting](https://ukraine.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-02/Conflict-related%20civilian%20casualties%20as%20of%2031%20December%202021%20%28rev%2027%20January%202022%29%20corr%20EN_0.pdf?target=popup).

3. **The election comparison mixes different kinds of election.**  
   > “But the crowds numbered in the hundreds of thousands, and when Ukraine voted that year, and again in 2019, the far-right parties took about two per cent.”

   About 2% works for the combined far-right **presidential candidates** in 2014 and the united far-right **parliamentary list** in 2019. In the 2014 parliamentary election, Svoboda received 4.71% and Right Sector 1.80%—about 6.5% combined. Their limited electoral appeal remains a sound point, but the comparison needs consistent elections. **Confidence: very high.** Sources: [European Parliament’s 2014 results](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/IDAN/2014/529089/IPOL_IDA%282014%29529089_EN.pdf), [Swedish Institute of International Affairs election analysis](https://www.ui.se/globalassets/ui.se-eng/publications/ui-publications/2020/ui-brief-no.-3-2020.pdf).

4. **Diplomatic condemnation is confused with participation in sanctions.**  
   > “Most of Asia, Africa and Latin America declined to take sides, and India and China bought the oil that Europe would no longer take.”

   Many governments declined Western sanctions or military involvement while explicitly condemning Russia’s invasion. The March 2022 UN resolution passed with 141 votes, including substantial majorities from those regions collectively. Specify the action they declined; “take sides” erases their recorded positions. **Confidence: high.** Source: [UN country-by-country vote](https://www.un.org/sites/un2.un.org/files/voting_rec_a-es-11-l.1.pdf).

5. **The Maidan killings are incorrectly compressed into one sniper massacre.**  
   > “In the third week of February 2014 police snipers opened fire and killed about a hundred people.”

   The overall protest death toll accumulated across different days, locations and methods of killing. It cannot all be attributed to police snipers. OHCHR’s later accounting identified 83 protesters, a journalist, a bystander and 13 law-enforcement officers killed or dying violently during January–February. The European Court’s account distinguishes numerous separate incidents, including the killing of 48 protesters attributed in prosecutions to Berkut officers on 20 February. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [OHCHR retrospective](https://ukraine.ohchr.org/en/six-years-after-killings-ukraine-justice-remains-elusive), [European Court of Human Rights](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/rum?i=001-207418).

6. **The drone section makes battlefield exposure universal.**  
   > “Between them and the commercial satellites that photograph the front every day, there is no longer anywhere to hide.”  
   > “A vehicle that moves within fifteen kilometres of the line is seen within minutes and struck.”  
   > “Tanks, which had dominated land warfare for a century, became too dangerous to use.”

   Surveillance and drones greatly restrict movement, but detection and successful attack are not automatic. Tanks continued operating, with altered tactics, protection and frequency of use. RUSI’s field research describes a Ukrainian platoon operating four captured tanks for over a year despite repeated hits. These sentences also violate the prohibition on absolutes that exceptions break. **Confidence: high.** Source: [RUSI’s 2025 field research](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/insights-papers/emergent-approaches-combined-arms-manoeuvre-ukraine).

7. **The “first since 1945” claim needs a narrower definition.**  
   > “It was the first time since 1945 that a European state had taken a neighbour’s territory by force.”

   Turkey’s seizure and continuing occupation of northern Cyprus in 1974 is an obvious counterexample to this broad wording. Formal annexation is a narrower and more defensible distinction. Readers have also just encountered territorial conquest in Bosnia in chapter 103; explain what makes Crimea different rather than appearing to forget that history. **Confidence: high on the wording problem.** Source: [UN account of the 1974 Cyprus operations](https://unficyp.unmissions.org/en/tripartite-conference-geneva-declaration).

8. **Popular support for the EU agreement is overstated.**  
   > “In November 2013 he was about to sign a trade and political agreement with the European Union, for which most Ukrainians had been waiting for years.”

   Contemporary polling shows a divided country, not an established majority eagerly awaiting the agreement. KIIS’s pre-protest figures put preference for EU integration at roughly 39%, against roughly 36% for the Russian-led Customs Union. Those questions do not measure precisely the same thing as support for signing the agreement, but they do not substantiate this sweeping claim. **Confidence: high that “most … for years” is unsupported.** Source: [KIIS’s comparison with its November 2013 polling](https://www.kiis.com.ua/?cat=reports&id=336&lang=eng&page=5).

9. **A feared democratic success is turned into an accomplished economic success.**  
   > “Ukraine twice did exactly that.”

   “That” refers to throwing out a corrupt government **and prospering**. Ukraine twice mobilised against governments, but its subsequent economic history was troubled: stagnation, severe recessions and persistent governance problems. Putin could fear the prospect of a successful democratic neighbour without Ukraine already having fulfilled that prospect twice. **Confidence: high on the misleading implication.** Source: [World Bank’s assessment of Ukraine’s development](https://ieg.worldbankgroup.org/evaluations/world-bank-group-ukraine-2012-20/chapter-1-background).

10. **The invasion-force estimate is placed months too early.**  
    > “That autumn a hundred and ninety thousand Russian troops assembled on three sides of Ukraine.”

    The upper estimate of 190,000 belongs to **February 2022**, not autumn 2021. It also included Russian-backed forces inside eastern Ukraine, rather than exclusively Russian troops assembling outside it. The buildup began earlier and grew through the winter. **Confidence: very high.** Sources: [European Court’s chronology](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/?i=001-244292), [contemporary estimate and its scope](https://www.russiamatters.org/news/russia-review/russia-review-feb-11-18-2022).

11. **Ukraine’s declaration of independence is dated incorrectly.**  
    > “Ukraine declared independence in the chaos of 1917 and was conquered by the Red Army.”

    The Ukrainian National Republic was proclaimed in 1917 within a proposed federation with Russia. Full independence was declared in January **1918**. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [Encyclopedia of Ukraine](https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/display.asp?linkpath=pages%5CU%5CK%5CUkrainianNationalRepublic.htm).

12. **Medieval history is made into a misleading two-nation split.**  
    > “After that the two halves of its people lived apart for four hundred years.”

    Rus’ had already fragmented into numerous principalities before the Mongol conquest. Its population did not divide neatly into two halves corresponding to modern Russians and Ukrainians; the Belarusian historical inheritance also disappears here. Different regions followed different political paths over centuries. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Encyclopedia of Ukraine’s account of Rus’ and its successor principalities](https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/display.asp?linkpath=pages%5CK%5CY%5CKyivanRushDA.htm).

13. **The nuclear-disarmament agreement is assigned entirely to Budapest.**  
    > “In December 1994, in Budapest, Ukraine signed them away.”

    Ukraine had already undertaken disarmament commitments, and the January 1994 Trilateral Statement established arrangements for warhead transfers, compensation and security assurances. Budapest supplied the memorandum associated with Ukraine’s accession to the Non-Proliferation Treaty. This is misleading compression rather than an entirely wrong association. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Account by US negotiator Steven Pifer](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-trilateral-process-the-united-states-ukraine-russia-and-nuclear-weapons/).

14. **The language ban is stated without its material exceptions.**  
    > “In 1876 the tsar banned the printing of books in it.”

    The Ems Ukase severely restricted Ukrainian publishing, but allowed historical documents and certain literary works under prescribed conditions. “Banned most Ukrainian publishing” would preserve the point without making the ban total. This also falls under the style rule against exception-breaking absolutes. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [Encyclopedia of Ukraine’s description of the decree](https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/display.asp?linkpath=pages%5CE%5CM%5CEmsUkase.htm).

15. **Putin’s recruitment is described misleadingly for a British-English readership.**  
    > “Putin was born in Leningrad in 1952, in a communal flat, and joined the secret police out of school.”

    He joined the KGB after graduating from Leningrad State University with a law degree in 1975. “Out of school” suggests secondary school, particularly alongside “flat” and the book’s British usage. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [Brookings biography excerpt](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/mrputin_chapter-1.pdf).

16. **The alliance is never named or its protection explained.**  
    > “In February 1990, when the Soviet army still stood in East Germany, the American secretary of state was trying to persuade the Soviet leader to accept a united Germany inside the Western alliance.”

    Chapters 100 and 103 also use descriptive shorthand for the alliance. Here, where membership drives the argument, the reader needs “NATO” and a brief explanation of collective defence. Otherwise “membership,” “protection” and cooperation with Ukraine remain insufficiently distinguished. **Reader-orientation issue.**

17. **The few-names rule is applied so strictly that the central political sequence becomes hard to follow.**  
    > “The loser of that contest was elected fairly six years later.”

    This is Viktor Yanukovych, who becomes the central actor throughout the next several paragraphs. Naming him once would help readers track the 2004 loser, 2010 winner and 2014 fugitive as the same person. He meets the style guide’s explicit exception for people whose names are needed to follow the story. **Reader-orientation issue.**

18. **The criminal court appears to contradict what the reader just learned.**  
    > “In March 2023 the international criminal court issued a warrant for Putin’s arrest on that charge.”

    Chapter 103 says Russia has not joined the court. That is compatible with the warrant, but a general reader needs the missing explanation: Ukraine accepted the court’s jurisdiction over crimes committed on its territory. This is an explanatory gap, not a factual contradiction. **Confidence: high.** Source: [ICC’s Ukraine situation page](https://www.icc-cpi.int/situations/ukraine).

19. **The 1954 transfer is dismissed with an unnecessary absolute.**  
    > “In 1954 the Soviet leadership moved the Crimean peninsula, on the Black Sea, from the Russian republic to the Ukrainian one, which at the time changed nothing, since both were part of the same state.”

    It changed administrative jurisdiction even though it did not create an international frontier. “Changed nothing” breaks the style rule and obscures why a republican boundary later became consequential. **Style and clarity issue.**

20. **Several sentences announce the argument instead of stating it.**  
    > “The argument has a weakness.”  
    > “The explanation was simple.”  
    > “One thing was new.”

    Each announces the point that the next sentence supplies. These are direct examples of the signposting prohibited by `style.md`; the following substantive sentences can carry the transitions themselves. I found no comparable hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or body cross-references.
