## Factual flags

1. **Major — new sentence contradicts the chapter’s artillery account.**
   
   > “If neither men nor shells could get across no man's land, perhaps something new could.”
   
   Shells crossed no man’s land routinely. Their problem was failing to destroy protected defenders, obstacles and other targets sufficiently to allow a sustained infantry advance. The preceding Somme account itself describes shells reaching German positions. [Imperial War Museums’ gallery guide](https://www.iwm.org.uk/sites/default/files/files/2023-10/first_world_war_large_print_guide.pdf) distinguishes bombardment reaching the defences from its failure to destroy them.

2. **Minor — new absolute about tanks is false.**
   
   > “The British answer to the trench was a machine, something that bullets and wire could not stop.”
   
   Early tanks resisted ordinary small-arms fire, but armour-piercing bullets could penetrate their hulls. Protection against ordinary bullets is the correct claim; immunity to bullets is not. [National Army Museum, “Attack of the tanks”](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/attack-tanks).

3. **Minor — the revision reintroduces a previously corrected animal-transport error.**
   
   > “Since the animals could not survive there, most supplies had to be carried on human heads.”
   
   Horses and oxen were used in the East African campaign, although disease caused severe losses and restricted their usefulness. The new sentence restores the blanket impossibility that the earlier revision had removed. Human porterage was indispensable because animal and mechanical transport were severely limited, not universally impossible. [International Encyclopedia of the First World War, “East and Central Africa”](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/east-and-central-africa/?format=pdf).

4. **Minor — portable wireless existed during the period described.**
   
   > “There was no radio that a man could carry, and the telephone wires had been cut by shellfire, so he could not tell where to send help.”
   
   Portable trench wireless sets existed; the British introduced a forward wireless set in 1916 and used it extensively in autumn 1917. Their limitations, availability and difficulty of use during an advance support the chapter’s communications argument, but their nonexistence does not. [Kurrajong Radio Museum’s surviving equipment and description](https://www.kurrajongradiomuseum.com.au/loop.htm); [Australian War Memorial’s German portable set captured in September 1917](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/C116713).

5. **Minor — the great-power death toll has a clear exception.**
   
   > “By the end of 1916 every one of the great powers had lost hundreds of thousands of its young men.”
   
   Japan was a belligerent great power and suffered roughly four hundred military deaths over the war, not hundreds of thousands. Restricting the statement to the principal European belligerents would preserve the intended point. [New Zealand Ministry for Culture and Heritage, “Empire of Japan”](https://nzhistory.govt.nz/war/japan-facts).

6. **Minor — the new truce summary gives a misleading uniform duration.**
   
   > “For one day, men who had spent weeks trying to kill each other behaved like neighbours.”
   
   Christmas Day was central, but these were separate local truces; some ceasefires continued beyond New Year’s Day. “For one day” makes their duration appear uniform when the paragraph describes activity across a broad front. [National Army Museum, “The Christmas Truce”](https://ww1.nam.ac.uk/1331/news/christmas-truce/).

## Clarity flags

- **Empty setup sentences:** “Their reasoning was simple.” and “The change came in stages.” announce explanations that the next sentences already deliver. “Here the generals' theory would be put to its fullest test” is a more explicit signpost and adds an unexplained superlative. These differ from useful transitions that supply a missing connection.

- **Repeated Verdun explanation:** “On his own account, then, the battle was meant to bleed France rather than to break through.” repeats the two preceding sentences. The necessary attribution already appears in “Their commander wrote afterwards”; the addition supplies little.

- **Unclear ownership:** “It belonged to neither side” should convey that neither army securely controlled no man’s land. “Belonged” can suggest that the land had no owners or national sovereignty.

- **Unclear comparison:** “Workers came from even further away.” Further away than India, Africa, or France’s Asian colonies? China does not furnish a clean geographical contrast with all the places just mentioned.

- **Unnecessary exclusion:** “The second use is what mattered once the fighting began.” The intended point is that ammonia enabled explosives production. The wording unnecessarily suggests its agricultural use ceased to matter, especially awkward before the account of fertiliser shortages.

- **Ambiguous backward reference and absolute:** “This is also why nobody could stop.” Immediately after the blockade paragraph, “This” initially seems to mean civilian starvation; the explanation that follows concerns sunk military sacrifice. “Nobody” also clashes with the chapter’s later account of Russia leaving the war.

- **Repeated payment wording:** “Finally, all of this had to be paid for. They paid for most of it by borrowing…” repeats “paid for” without adding much orientation.

- **Unclear subject:** “They advanced eleven kilometres by evening.” The new paragraph break separates “They” from the attacking troops with several sentences about guns and ranging. Repeating the troops’ identity would avoid the momentary ambiguity.

- **Numerical crowding:** “At half past seven on the morning of 1 July…” and “For seven days, fifteen hundred British guns fired a million and a half shells…” stack figures against the sheet’s “one number per point” preference. The exact attack time and bombardment scale serve the story; the accumulation makes both harder to retain.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit chapter cross-references. The instruction “To picture it, start at the front and walk backwards” is useful spatial orientation and fits the sheet’s permission to guide the reader.

## Overall

The revision largely preserves the factual record and improves some connections, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. The shells sentence needs correction; the tank and animal sentences introduce false absolutes. Several other additions repeat explanations already clear.
