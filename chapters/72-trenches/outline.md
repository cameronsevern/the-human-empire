# Chapter 72 — Why did the First World War last so long?

**Line under the title:** The Western Front and the world, from 1914 to 1919.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

The war lasted four years because the weapons of 1914 made defence far stronger than attack, and because the industrial states that fought it could replace their losses for years. A trench with machine guns and barbed wire could be reinforced by rail faster than attackers could walk across broken ground, so every breakthrough closed. Governments answered by throwing in more: more shells, new weapons, whole economies, the men of their empires. After such sacrifices no government could accept a compromise, since only victory could justify the dead. It ended when one side ran out. Germany gambled on starving Britain with submarines, brought the United States in, made one last offensive in 1918 and collapsed. The peace that followed humiliated Germany without disabling it.

## The story

1. **Opening: Christmas in no man's land.** Answer first. December 1914: the soldiers stop the war for a day, and are ordered not to do it again.
2. **The ditch.** What a trench system was. Why an attack on it failed even when it succeeded.
3. **Seven days of shells.** Verdun, and the first day on the Somme, 1 July 1916.
4. **Clouds.** The search for a way through: gas, tanks, aircraft. The chemist who gave Germany both its explosives and its gas.
5. **The whole nation.** The war of factories. Women in the shell works, rationing, the blockade and the turnip winter. Why nobody could make peace.
6. **A world war.** Soldiers and labourers from India, Africa and China. The Ottoman and Italian fronts.
7. **The gamble.** Submarines against Britain's food. A telegram to Mexico. America comes in.
8. **The black day.** 1918: Russia leaves, Germany attacks, the attack runs out, the line breaks. The eleventh hour.
9. **Ending: the mirrors.** The count of the dead, the influenza, and the treaty signed in the hall where the German empire had been proclaimed.

## Left for later

The Russian revolution. The break-up of the Ottoman empire and the Middle East. The Depression and the rise of Hitler.

- Trench layout; width of no man's land; share of casualties from artillery.
- Verdun: dates, casualties, the supply road. The Somme: the bombardment (days and shells), first-day casualties and dead, a volunteer battalion's losses, total casualties and ground gained.
- Gas: date and place of first chlorine attack, Haber's role, his wife's death, deaths from gas. Tanks: first use. Nitrogen fixation and German explosives.
- Blockade deaths in Germany; the turnip winter. British conscription (1916).
- Indian soldiers and dead; African carriers and dead in East Africa; French colonial troops; the Chinese Labour Corps.
- The Lusitania: date, dead, Americans. Unrestricted submarine war: date, peak sinkings, Britain's grain reserve, convoys. The telegram of January 1917 and its offer. US declaration; size of US army before and after; arrivals per day in 1918.
- French mutinies of 1917. Brest-Litovsk. The offensive of 21 March 1918: bombardment and advance. 8 August 1918. The naval mutiny, the abdication, the armistice (place, hour), deaths on the last morning.
- War dead by country; France's share of young men. Influenza deaths and why "Spanish".
- The treaty: date, place, terms (territory, army, reparations, guilt clause). The French marshal's remark.
