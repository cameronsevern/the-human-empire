Read the requested files; no files changed. Findings below are ordered by seriousness. I found no substantive contradiction with chapters 67 or 70, and no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit chapter cross-references.

1. **Misleading central explanation — high confidence.** “It ended when one side ran out of men and food.” Also: “None of these inventions decided anything.”

   Germany suffered severe shortages, but defeat came through military reverses, failing morale, Allied material superiority and its allies’ collapse—not literal exhaustion of men and food. Aircraft, tanks and improved artillery methods contributed to those reverses. The second sentence also conflicts with the chapter’s own account of Amiens. The defensible distinction is that no invention won the war *by itself*. [National Army Museum: 1918](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/1918-victory).

2. **Overstated explanation of failed peace — high confidence.** “Neither side could break through, and neither government, after such losses, could accept anything less than victory.”

   These were coalitions containing several governments, with different objectives. Austria-Hungary’s emperor pursued negotiated peace in 1917; Russia subsequently accepted a disastrous separate peace. Sacrifice helped make compromise politically difficult, but incompatible territorial and security demands also mattered. “Neither government” both confuses the participants and makes their positions falsely uniform. [Habsburg history project: peace initiatives](https://ww1.habsburger.net/en/chapters/individual-peace-initiatives-julius-meinl-und-heinrich-lammasch).

3. **Disputed interpretation presented as established fact — high confidence that it is disputed.** “Their commander's stated aim was not to capture ground at all.”

   Falkenhayn’s account of deliberately bleeding France white comes from his postwar memoirs. Historians dispute whether this accurately describes his original plan, and his operational commanders intended to capture Verdun. A safe statement is that he hoped an attack at Verdun would force France towards peace; the exclusively attritional intention needs attribution or a brief acknowledgment of disagreement. [Verdun Memorial: objectives and historiographical debate](https://memorial-verdun.fr/fr/ressources/la-bataille-de-verdun).

4. **False historical contrast; breaks the rule against absolutes — high confidence.** “In earlier wars, an army fought and the country went about its business.”

   Earlier wars repeatedly mobilised economies, destroyed civilian livelihoods and caused mass displacement and starvation. The American Civil War alone defeats this claim. What changed was the scale and organisation of industrial mobilisation, not whether civilians participated or suffered. [US National Park Service: civilian suffering in the Civil War](https://home.nps.gov/articles/death-and-dying.htm).

5. **Contradiction with chapter 71.** “Each therefore raised its aims as the cost rose.” Followed by: “France must have its lost provinces back.”

   Chapter 71 already describes France after 1871 as a country “which burned to recover its lost provinces.” Recovering Alsace-Lorraine therefore cannot illustrate a newly enlarged objective caused by wartime losses. Distinguish an existing aim becoming harder to abandon from an aim becoming more ambitious. [Chapter 71](chapters/71-sarajevo/chapter.md).

6. **Conflated offensives and false absolute — high confidence.** “For the first time since 1914 the front moved, and in a few weeks the Germans advanced sixty kilometres and stood again on the Marne.”

   The Western Front had moved before, notably during Germany’s withdrawal to the Hindenburg Line in 1917. More importantly, March 1918’s offensive advanced towards Amiens; the Germans reached the Marne through a separate offensive beginning on 27 May. The present sentence turns several campaigns into one uninterrupted advance. [Australian War Memorial: Hindenburg Line](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84703); [US Army medical history: the separate operations](https://achh.army.mil/history/book-wwi-fieldoperations-chapter11/).

7. **Misleading submarine-war chronology — high confidence.** “It had tried this in 1915, and had stopped after a submarine torpedoed the British liner *Lusitania* off Ireland, drowning nearly twelve hundred people, among them a hundred and twenty-eight Americans.”

   The sinking did not immediately end the campaign. Restrictions developed through further incidents and diplomatic confrontations, including the *Arabic* in 1915 and the *Sussex* pledge in May 1916. The sentence makes the *Lusitania* a cleaner turning point than it was. [US Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/wwi).

8. **Overstated trigger for American entry — high confidence.** “The last straw was a secret telegram from the German foreign minister to his ambassador in Mexico, which British codebreakers intercepted and passed to Washington.”

   The telegram intensified public hostility, but unrestricted submarine warfare and subsequent attacks on American shipping were central to Wilson’s decision. Calling the telegram “the last straw” makes the causal sequence misleading: it was published in early March, before further sinkings helped precipitate the April declaration. [US Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/wwi).

9. **Overstates Wilson’s promise — high confidence.** “The American president had promised a settlement in which every nation would govern itself, and a league of nations to keep the peace.”

   Wilson’s Fourteen Points did not promise independent self-government to every people. They proposed balancing colonial populations’ interests against imperial claims, and “autonomous development” for peoples within Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire. The universal promise gives the reader a false benchmark for judging the settlement. [National Archives: the Fourteen Points](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/president-woodrow-wilsons-14-points).

10. **Wrong scope and sole-source implication — high confidence.** “Until then, every army's gunpowder had depended on nitrate dug from the deserts of Chile, and when the war began, the British navy cut Germany off from Chile.”

    Chilean nitrate was crucial to modern explosives production, but it was not the universal historical source of gunpowder’s nitrate. Indian saltpetre and European production had supplied gunpowder for centuries. Restrict the claim to Germany’s prewar dependence on imported Chilean nitrate for its explosives industry. [Royal Society of Chemistry: saltpetre sources](https://edu.rsc.org/feature/discovering-iodine/2020126.article).

11. **Tanks mattered before 1918 — high confidence.** “The first ones broke down or stuck in the mud, and it was two more years before there were enough of them, used properly, to matter.”

    Some tanks achieved useful results even in their first deployment. More decisively, massed tanks helped penetrate German defences at Cambrai in November 1917, although the gains were not sustained. “To matter” wrongly erases that development. [Australian War Memorial: Cambrai](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84316).

12. **Wrong army proportion and incomplete supply description — high confidence.** “The French held Verdun by sending their whole army through it in turn, supplied along a single road on which a lorry passed every fourteen seconds, day and night.”

    Roughly three quarters of the French army served at Verdun, not the whole army. The celebrated road was vital, but a narrow-gauge railway also supplied the sector. Keep the road’s importance without implying that it was the sole transport route. [Verdun Memorial](https://memorial-verdun.fr/fr/ressources/la-bataille-de-verdun).

13. **Misleading picture of the Somme attack — high confidence about the artillery; moderate about the blanket infantry description.** “At half past seven on the morning of 1 July the guns stopped, whistles blew, and a hundred thousand men climbed out and walked forward in lines.”

    The artillery did not simply stop: fire lifted or moved onwards to support the infantry. Infantry arrangements also varied, with some troops already positioned in no man’s land before zero hour. The sentence presents one familiar image as the procedure across the attack. [National Army Museum: the barrage and assault](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/battle-somme); [Westminster Abbey’s centenary account](https://www.westminster-abbey.org/media/ozrhm1bw/somme-centenary-service-evening.pdf).

14. **Compressed chronology obscures a change of government — high confidence.** “In 1917 Russia fell into revolution and left the war, and the new government signed away a third of the old empire's population as the price of peace.”

    Russia experienced two revolutions; the government formed after the first continued fighting. The Bolsheviks agreed an armistice in December 1917 and signed Brest-Litovsk on **3 March 1918**. The current wording places the territorial settlement in 1917 and leaves “the new government” unidentified. [Treaty text, Yale Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/brest.asp).

15. **False claim about financing; absolute — high confidence.** “They paid for it all by borrowing from their own citizens, who were told on every wall that to lend was a patriotic duty.”

    Governments also raised taxes and borrowed abroad; Allied access to foreign credit was particularly important. Domestic war loans were a major instrument, not the entire funding system. [International Encyclopedia of the First World War: war finance](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/ams/959308/pdf/1914-1918-Online-war_finance-2018-02-08.pdf).

16. **An identifiable exception breaks the absolute — high confidence.** “Nobody had planned for a long war.”

    Kitchener anticipated a long struggle and organised Britain’s New Armies accordingly. The claim that governments were inadequately prepared for prolonged industrial warfare is defensible; “nobody” is not. [National Army Museum: Kitchener](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/herbert-kitchener).

17. **Wrong battle count — high confidence.** “Italy came in on the side of Britain and France in 1915 and fought eleven battles for the same Alpine river.”

    There were **twelve** battles of the Isonzo. Eleven were Italian offensives; the twelfth was the Austro-German offensive at Caporetto. If eleven is retained, specify eleven Italian offensives. The river and Italy’s opponent also need identifying for a general reader. [National Army Museum: Isonzo](https://ww1.nam.ac.uk/timeline/battles-isonzo/).

18. **Wrong date — high confidence.** “On 9 November the emperor fled to Holland, and two days later, in a railway carriage in a forest north of Paris, a German delegation signed an armistice.”

    His abdication was announced on 9 November; he crossed into the Netherlands on **10 November**. The armistice date is correct. [Museum Huis Doorn](https://www.huisdoorn.nl/en/world-war-i-knowledge-center/background-to-the-first-world-war/).

19. **Unsupported six-hour casualty total — high confidence about the distinction.** “In those six hours, on the last morning, nearly three thousand more men were killed.”

    The commonly cited figure of 2,738 deaths concerns **11 November as a whole**, not a separately established total between signing and ceasefire. Daily casualty records do not justify assigning that entire number to those six hours. [Adam Hochschild’s discussion of the records, hosted by UC Berkeley](https://journalism.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/If-You-Think-the-First-World-War-Began-Senselessly-Consider-How-it-Ended.pdf).

20. **Regular army confused with total available forces — high confidence.** “The United States had an army of about a hundred and thirty thousand men when it declared war.”

    Approximately 128,000 describes the **Regular Army**. It excludes National Guard personnel, including roughly 80,000 already in federal service. Adding “regular” preserves the intended point without understating the broader force. [US Army history of mobilisation](https://history.army.mil/portals/143/Images/Publications/catalog/104-10.pdf).

21. **Three unsupported absolutes — high confidence.** “In East Africa, where a small German force led the British a chase for four years through country with no roads and where the tsetse fly killed every pack animal, everything had to be carried on human heads.”

    Roads were scarce, not nonexistent; animal transport was possible in some areas; railways and other transport also operated. Human porterage was indispensable, but “no roads,” “every pack animal” and “everything” turn the logistical problem into a false universal. [Hew Strachan, *The First World War in Africa*](https://ccaf.africa/books/Strachan%20-%20The%20First%20World%20War%20in%20Africa%20%282004%29.pdf).

22. **False exclusivity in the influenza explanation — high confidence.** “It was known as the Spanish flu, because Spain was neutral and its newspapers, unlike everyone else's, were free to report it.”

    Spain’s conspicuous reporting helped produce the name, but it was not the only neutral country with newspapers reporting influenza. Swiss newspapers provide an immediate counterexample. Compare Spain with the censored belligerent press, not “everyone else.” [Zurich Central Library: contemporary Swiss coverage](https://www.zb.uzh.ch/en/fokus/beitrag/die-spanische-grippe-in-zurich-erzahlen).

23. **The French disappear from the Somme story — reader-orientation problem.** “The battle went on for another four and a half months, cost the three armies more than a million casualties, and advanced the British line about ten kilometres.”

    Until “three armies,” the account has presented a British attack against Germans. Introduce French participation and the offensive’s connection to relieving Verdun before counting their casualties. That also explains why the offensive continued despite its disastrous opening. [National Army Museum: Somme](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/battle-somme).

24. **Unexplained unit and ambiguous season — reader-orientation/style problem.** “In the winter of 1917 it moved fifty divisions from the east to the west by rail, and its commanders prepared a final offensive to win the war before the Americans arrived in force.”

    “Division” has not been explained, so fifty conveys little scale to this audience. “Winter of 1917” can also mean January–February, before the revolutions just described. Identify the period as winter 1917–18 and give a plain indication of what a division represents.

25. **The ending requires unstated knowledge — reader-orientation problem.** “He was out by sixty-five days.”

    The reader must infer both the start of the Second World War on 1 September 1939 and that the calculation runs from the treaty’s signing on 28 June 1919. Neither is stated. The arithmetic works on that basis, but the final line does not supply enough information for the book’s intended novice reader to understand it.

26. **Explicit signpost sentence — clear style breach.** “It was called a world war for a reason.”

    This announces an explanation instead of giving it. The following sentence already supplies the substance: Britain and France brought their empires into the conflict, while Germany’s colonies and allies extended the fighting.
