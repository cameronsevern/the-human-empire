Ranked by seriousness. Confidence labels concern factual corrections; reader-confusion and style judgments are identified separately. No files were edited.

1. **The conclusion makes a false universal claim central to the argument.**  
   > “Every large state adopted conscription, since an army of paid volunteers could no longer face a nation in arms.”

   Britain retained voluntary recruitment for its regular army until 1916, and that army fought successfully against Napoleon. Conscription spread widely across continental Europe; it did not make professional volunteer armies universally unviable. This also breaks the rule against absolutes that one exception defeats. **Confidence: high.** [UK Parliament, introduction of conscription](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/yourcountry/overview/conscription/).

2. **The contrast with earlier warfare contradicts the reader’s experience of chapter 55.**  
   > “Wars were the business of kings and their paid soldiers.”

   Chapter 55 describes Parliament’s army, soldiers motivated by religion and political conviction, promotion by merit, and ordinary soldiers debating who should govern. Its sentence “Many of its soldiers were fervent Puritans who carried Bibles in their packs, sang psalms on the march, and believed that they were doing God's work” directly undermines this blanket contrast. The revolutionary innovation needs to be the scale and organisation of national mobilisation, rather than the invention of committed soldiers or popular participation. **Confidence: high about the textual contradiction.** [Chapter 55](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md).

3. **The survival of the Code is substantially overstated.**  
   > “The code stayed in force in France, and in Belgium, Holland, the Rhineland and Italy, because the people who lived under it would not give it up.”

   Restoration governments repealed or replaced it in substantial parts of Italy. Modena largely abolished it in 1814; Tuscany restored much earlier law; Austrian civil law replaced it in Lombardy–Venetia. Its later influence is different from continuous operation. The explanation based solely on popular refusal is also unsupported. **Confidence: high.** [Treccani, history of codification](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/codice_(Enciclopedia-Italiana)/).

4. **The account of emancipation erases a consequential exception.**  
   > “Peasants were released from their dues to the lord.”

   Following “Wherever the French went,” this promises a universal result. In the Duchy of Warsaw, personal serfdom ended, but landlords retained the land and peasants continued owing labour and other obligations; some burdens increased. Personal freedom and freedom from dues were different changes. This is also an exception-breaking absolute under `style.md`. **Confidence: high.** [Fondation Napoléon, Poland and the Duchy of Warsaw](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/poland-through-the-ages/).

5. **The final historical example has the wrong year and duration.**  
   > “In 1871 its conscript army, moved by railway, crushed France in six weeks.”

   The rapid campaign culminating in Sedan occurred in **1870**. France continued fighting after Sedan; the Paris armistice came in January 1871. The sentence conflates the destruction of Napoleon III’s field army with the defeat of France, and moves the former into the year of German unification. **Confidence: high.** [Château de Versailles, chronology and armistice](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/decouvrir/ressources/bac-avec-versailles).

6. **The Code’s geographical reach includes a country where its introduction remained a proposal.**  
   > “Napoleon considered it his greatest achievement, and it was imposed from Lisbon to Warsaw.”

   Lisbon is misleading. Portuguese collaborators petitioned for a constitution and application of the French Civil Code in 1808; that project did not become an enduring Napoleonic legal settlement before French occupation ended. Portugal’s later borrowing from French codification is a separate development. **Confidence: high that “imposed … in Lisbon” needs correction.** [Cambridge, Portuguese constitutional proposals](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/first-fundamental-rights-documents-in-europe/portugal-tropical-versailles-in-the-beginning-of-the-nineteenth-century/85F1051BA811FE12DD34B7B41C601739), [Lusófona University, the unsuccessful 1808 petition](https://research.ulusofona.pt/en/publications/o-enigma-da-s%C3%BAplica-constitucional-portuguesa-de-1808/).

7. **The Code’s scope and authorship are wrong.**  
   > “In 1804 a committee under his chairmanship had boiled the whole tangle of French law down into a single code, short enough to carry in a pocket and written in French that a farmer could read.”

   This was the **Civil Code**, covering matters such as property, contracts and family relations—not all French law. Separate procedural, commercial and criminal codes followed. Four jurists drafted it from 1800 under Tronchet’s presidency; Napoleon chaired many subsequent Council of State discussions. The sentence merges those stages. **Confidence: high.** [French Senate, drafting history](https://www.senat.fr/connaitre-le-senat/lhistoire-du-senat/dossiers-dhistoire/bicentenaire-du-code-civil/bicentenaire-du-code-civil-lelaboration.html), [National Assembly, the separate codes](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/histoire-et-patrimoine/consulat-et-premier-empire/la-codification-juridique).

8. **The explanation of army movement gives Napoleon too clean a break with the past.**  
   > “An army of the old kind marched down a single road in one long column, with a train of wagons behind it carrying bread, and it could cover perhaps fifteen kilometres a day.”

   Earlier armies also used separate columns and divisions. Broglie experimented with divisional organisation during the Seven Years’ War, and Bourcet advocated separated bodies capable of concentrating. Napoleon developed and applied an existing evolution exceptionally effectively; the single-road description makes his predecessors artificially helpless. The related claim, “He moved that army faster than armies had ever moved,” is an unsupported all-time record. **Confidence: high on the predecessors; medium on comparisons of marching speed, which depend on campaign conditions.** [The Napoleon Series, development of the corps system](https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/organization/c_armycorps.html).

9. **The Prussian reform passage substitutes a patriotic legend for the scale of the actual programme.**  
   > “His ministers freed the serfs, opened the officer corps to commoners, and began training the whole male population for war in short shifts, so as to hide the numbers from the French.”

   The rotating-reserve system trained a limited additional pool, not the whole male population. Universal military obligation and mass mobilisation belong to subsequent reforms in 1813–14. Scholarship also disputes the familiar account of an enormous secret army created specifically to evade Napoleon’s restrictions. **Confidence: high on the scale error; medium-high on the stated purpose.** [German Federal Agency for Civic Education, Prussian army reforms](https://www.bpb.de/shop/zeitschriften/apuz/archiv/526140/die-preussische-armee-1807-1840/).

10. **The Russian invasion figure mixes different populations—and conflicts with chapter 57’s wording.**  
    > “In June 1812 he crossed the Russian frontier with the largest army that Europe had ever seen, about six hundred thousand men.”

    Roughly 450,000–480,000 entered in the initial invasion; totals around 600,000 include reserves and later forces. The subsequent survival comparison needs the same denominator. Also, chapter 57 has already called **800,000 men under arms** “the largest army Europe had ever seen.” Distinguish a country’s total armed forces from one invasion force, or the reader sees a numerical contradiction. **Confidence: high on the distinction; medium on any single total.** [Oxford reference account](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62399/reference-article-abstract/555374004), [chapter 57](chapters/57-france/chapter.md).

11. **The Spanish narrative reverses cause and chronology.**  
    > “He lured its royal family across the border, forced the king to abdicate, and gave the Spanish throne to his own brother Joseph.”

    Followed by the supposed response on 2 May, this makes the Madrid uprising a reaction to Joseph’s installation. The uprising occurred **before** the forced Bayonne abdications later in May and Joseph’s appointment in June. The sequence matters because it explains why the uprising began. **Confidence: high.** [McGill University, Napoleonic chronology](https://digital.library.mcgill.ca/napoleon/english/timeline-full.htm).

12. **The Spanish regular army is dismissed too completely.**  
    > “The Spanish regular army was soon beaten, and it made no difference.”

    Spanish regular forces won the major victory at Bailén in July 1808, forcing a French withdrawal from Madrid, and regular Spanish armies continued fighting despite repeated defeats. Guerrillas did not simply replace a finished regular army. “It made no difference” also erases the substantial consequences of those defeats. **Confidence: high.** [Spanish Army historical account of Bailén](https://ejercito.defensa.gob.es/Galerias/Descarga_pdf/Unidades/Madrid/rinf1/REVISTA_numero_28.pdf).

13. **The distribution of conquered kingdoms is inaccurate.**  
    > “He turned northern Italy, Holland and half of Germany into kingdoms and gave them to his brothers and his marshals.”

    Napoleon himself was king of the Kingdom of Italy. Major German allies such as Bavaria and Württemberg retained their own dynasties. Louis in Holland and Jérôme in Westphalia provide valid examples, but the sentence makes family appointments cover far more territory than they did. **Confidence: high.** [Fondation Napoléon, Italian kingship](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/how-napoleon-became-king-of-italy), [German Historical Institute, German territorial changes](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/the-holy-roman-empire-1648-1815/germany-and-italy-1803).

14. **Austerlitz becomes annihilation rather than decisive defeat.**  
    > “By the afternoon it had ceased to exist.”

    Much of the Allied army escaped. Estimates vary, but losses of roughly a third of its strength do not mean the army ceased to exist. “Routed” or “decisively beaten” would preserve the force without making a false absolute. **Confidence: high.** [History of War, Austerlitz and the Allied retreat](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_austerlitz.html).

15. **The 1807 claim has surviving exceptions.**  
    > “By 1807 every power on the continent had either been defeated by him or had made itself his ally.”

    Sweden remained at war with France and allied to Britain after Tilsit; coercing it required further war in 1808–09. If the intended claim concerns the principal continental powers, say that. “Every power” also breaks `style.md`’s absolute rule. **Confidence: high.** [Thomas Munch-Petersen, “The secret intelligence from Tilsit in 1807”](https://shs.cairn.info/article/E_NAPO_133_0022/pdf?lang=en).

16. **Annual recruitment is attached to the wrong reform.**  
    > “Every year a new class of young men was called up.”

    Immediately after the description of 1793, this makes annual classes appear to follow directly from the emergency levy. Regular conscription by age class was established by the **Jourdan–Delbrel law of 1798**. One bridging sentence would preserve the distinction between emergency mobilisation and a continuing recruitment system. **Confidence: high.** [French National Assembly, the 1798 law](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/histoire-et-patrimoine/revolution-francaise/loi-du-19-fructidor-an-vi-instituant-la-conscription-et-le-service-militaire-obligatoire).

17. **Waterloo is presented as an exclusively British defence.**  
    > “On the eighteenth he attacked the British, under the Duke of Wellington, on a ridge near the village of Waterloo.”

    Wellington commanded a multinational army; only about a third was British. Dutch, Belgian and several German contingents were integral to the defence. “Wellington’s Allied army” avoids giving the reader the wrong participants. **Confidence: high.** [Waterloo Association, composition of the armies](https://ageofrevolution.waterlooassociation.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/Who-Won-Waterloo.pdf).

18. **The Russians had already stood and fought before September.**  
    > “In September the Russians finally stood and fought at a village outside Moscow, in the bloodiest single day of the whole twenty years of war.”

    “Finally” implies an uninterrupted retreat without a substantial battle. The Russians had defended Smolensk in August. Borodino was the great battle before Moscow, not their first stand. **Confidence: high.** [Fondation Napoléon, the campaign from the Niemen to Moscow](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/napoleons-russian-campaign-from-the-niemen-to-moscow).

19. **The Toulon promotion skips an intervening rank.**  
    > “Within the week the captain was a general.”

    Napoleon became a brigadier general on 22 December, but he had already been promoted to *chef de bataillon*, a major-equivalent rank, in October. He arrived as a captain; he did not jump directly from captain to general that December week. **Confidence: high.** [Toulon chronology](https://www.napoleon-empire.org/contre-revolution/toulon.php), [Fondation Napoléon, promotion date](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/1769-1793-napoleon-bonapartes-early-years).

20. **The first-language detail is misleading.**  
    > “His family were minor nobles with little money, and his first language was Italian.”

    Distinguish spoken **Corsican** from the Italian in which he learned to read and write. Calling Italian his first language suppresses that distinction. **Confidence: medium-high; terminology sometimes treats Corsican as an Italian dialect.** [Andrew Roberts, biography excerpt hosted by the Library of Congress](https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/enhancements/fy1506/2013497791-s.html).

21. **An unknowable counterfactual is presented as a fixed career ceiling.**  
    > “Under the king, he would have ended his career as a major.”

    The Revolution plainly accelerated his opportunities, but neither his final rank nor a major’s ceiling can be established. This is not a complaint about confidence: it is a precise prediction about an unrealised life. Under the style guide’s “replace weak examples” rule, use an actual barrier to advancement rather than qualify this prediction.

22. **Prussia enters without enough geographical orientation.**  
    > “The following year he destroyed the Prussian army in a single day, and the year after that he beat the Russians again.”

    **Reader clarity:** Chapters 55 and 57 do not explain what Prussia was. Since the chapter later distinguishes Prussia, German states and a united Germany, identify it here as a powerful kingdom centred on Berlin, occupying parts of present-day Germany and Poland. Otherwise the eventual German unification can look like a change of name.

23. **The narrative omits the outcome of Waterloo before transporting Napoleon into exile.**  
    > “This time the British took no chances.”

    **Reader clarity:** The previous paragraph ends with the Prussians arriving. It never says the French army broke, Napoleon lost, or he abdicated and surrendered. A short causal bridge is needed before “They shipped him to Saint Helena”; otherwise the decisive end of his rule happens between paragraphs.
