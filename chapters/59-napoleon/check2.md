## Changed facts

No existing name, date, numerical total or quotation was removed or replaced. However, the revision adds factual explanations and strengthens several claims. Below, “added” means there was no corresponding old sentence; the quoted old text supplies its context.

1. **Toulon: the commanders’ thinking and British purpose.**  
   **Old:** “The town itself did not matter.”  
   **New:** “They had been looking at the town, and the town itself did not matter.”  
   **Added:** “The British were there because of their ships, and ships need a safe place to anchor.”  
   These introduce an account of what the commanders were concentrating on and why the British occupied Toulon.

2. **Napoleon’s nationality.**  
   **Old:** “He had been born in 1769 on the island of Corsica, which France had bought from the Italian city of Genoa the year before.”  
   **New:** The sentence remains, followed by “He was French, in other words, by a margin of one year.”  
   This makes the chronological implication explicit; it does not change the dates.

3. **Career opportunities and promotion.**  
   **Old:** “Most of the old noble officers fled abroad, the republic was fighting all of Europe at once, and it promoted whoever won.”  
   **New:** “Most of the old noble officers fled abroad, which left their places empty. The republic, meanwhile, was fighting all of Europe at once, and it could not afford to care who a general's father was. It promoted whoever won.”  
   **Added:** “Even there, he could not have expected to get very far.” / “Toulon had shown that he could win, and his rise after it was quick.”  
   These add causal explanations and a counterfactual career judgment.

4. **The imperial vote and coronation.**  
   **Old:** “More than three and a half million Frenchmen voted in favour, and a few thousand against.”  
   **New:** “The change was put to a vote, and more than three and a half million Frenchmen voted in favour, and a few thousand against.”  
   This explicitly identifies what was submitted to the vote; see factual flag 6.  
   **Old:** “Napoleon then picked it up and put it on his own head.”  
   **New:** The sentence remains, followed by “Nobody watching could miss the meaning. He owed the crown to no one but himself.”  
   These add an interpretation of the ceremony and a universal assertion about its audience.

5. **The height myth’s origin.**  
   **Old:** “The British cartoonists drew him as a tiny figure in an enormous hat, and the cartoons have outlasted the measurements.”  
   **New:** The sentence remains, preceded by “The mistake is the work of his enemies.”  
   The revision turns an example of hostile portrayal into a categorical explanation of the misconception.

6. **Corps support and supplies.**  
   **Old:** “Each corps had its own infantry, cavalry and guns, and could fight alone for a day if it had to.”  
   **New:** “Each corps was a small army in itself. It had its own infantry, cavalry and guns, and could fight alone for a day if it had to. A day was enough, because that was how long its neighbours needed to reach it.”  
   **Added:** “They also did without most of the bread wagons.”  
   These add a specific reinforcement timetable and a quantitative claim about dispensing with wagons.  
   **Old:** “In this way the whole army could move thirty kilometres a day and more.”  
   **New:** “In this way the whole army could move thirty kilometres a day and more, about twice the pace of an army of the old kind.”  
   The comparison makes explicit the arithmetic already implied by the existing figures.

7. **Ulm and Austerlitz.**  
   **Old:** “Napoleon's army was on the coast of the English Channel, eight hundred kilometres away.”  
   **New:** “It seemed to have plenty of time, because Napoleon's army was on the coast of the English Channel, eight hundred kilometres away.”  
   This adds an attribution of Austrian expectations.  
   **Old:** “The Austrian general surrendered with twenty-three thousand men.”  
   **New:** “The Austrian general, cut off, surrendered with twenty-three thousand men.”  
   This explicitly states the military cause.  
   **Old:** “When they marched off the high ground in the centre to attack the weak end, he sent two divisions up the hill through the morning fog and cut their army in half.”  
   **New:** “When they marched off the high ground in the centre to attack the weak end, they emptied the middle of their own line. He then sent two divisions up the hill through the morning fog and cut their army in half.”  
   “Emptied” introduces a stronger—and inaccurate—description.

8. **Britain: strategic incapacity and the effect of subsidies.**  
   **Old:** “One enemy remained out of reach.”  
   **New:** “One enemy remained out of reach, because it lay behind the sea.”  
   **Added:** “The battle left the two powers stuck.” / “Each was supreme in its own element, and neither could get at the other.”  
   **Old:** “Each tried to starve the other.”  
   **New:** “Since neither could strike directly, each tried to starve the other.”  
   **Added:** “A country that lived by selling would be beaten, he reasoned, if nobody was allowed to buy.”  
   These explicitly attribute the economic strategy to an inability to attack directly.  
   **Old:** “It used the money to hire allies.”  
   **New:** The sentence remains, followed by “For that reason, none of Napoleon's victories on land was ever final.”  
   This adds a universal causal claim about British subsidies.

9. **Spain: intentions and military limitations.**  
   **Old:** “Spain was his ally, and badly governed.”  
   **New:** The sentence remains, followed by “He decided to take charge of it himself.”  
   **Old:** “Napoleon then forced the king to abdicate and gave the Spanish throne to his own brother Joseph.”  
   **New:** “Napoleon was not deterred. He forced the king to abdicate and gave the Spanish throne to his own brother Joseph.”  
   These add explicit statements of intention and reaction.  
   **Old:** “The French burned villages in reprisal, which produced more recruits for the bands.”  
   **New:** “Against the bands, the French had no good answer. They burned villages in reprisal, which produced more recruits for the bands. An army built to win a war in one great battle could find no battle to win.”  
   The revision adds categorical claims about the army’s design and inability to defeat guerrillas through battle.

10. **Prussian reform.**  
    **Old:** “His ministers freed the serfs and opened the officer corps to commoners.”  
    **New:** The sentence remains, preceded by “If the French fought better because they were citizens, his own subjects would have to be given something to fight for.”  
    This adds an explanation of the reforms’ purpose.  
    **Old:** “The French had limited Prussia's army by treaty, so the ministers passed recruits through the ranks in quick rotation to build up a trained reserve, and when the moment came in 1813 they called the whole male population to arms.”  
    **New:** “The French had limited Prussia's army by treaty, so the ministers passed recruits through the ranks in quick rotation. A man was trained and sent home, and another took his place. In this way the army stayed small while a trained reserve built up behind it, and when the moment came in 1813 they called the whole male population to arms.”  
    This supplies procedural detail without changing the stated scale or date.

11. **Russia: absence of battle and Borodino’s outcome.**  
    **Old:** “Apart from one fight for a burning city on the way, they retreated for a thousand kilometres, destroying the crops and villages as they went.”  
    **New:** The sentence remains, followed by “So there was no battle, and there was no food.”  
    This introduces an absolute that contradicts the preceding sentence.  
    **Old:** “In September the Russians turned and offered battle at a village outside Moscow, in the bloodiest single day of the whole twenty years of war.”  
    **New:** “In September the Russians at last turned and offered battle at a village outside Moscow, in the bloodiest single day of the whole twenty years of war.”  
    “At last” revives the implication that the earlier check had removed.  
    **Old:** “The Russians withdrew in good order, and a week later Napoleon rode into Moscow.”  
    **New:** “It was a battle, but it was not decisive. The Russians withdrew in good order, which meant that the tsar still had an army. A week later Napoleon rode into Moscow.”  
    This adds an explicit judgment of the battle’s strategic result.

12. **The final coalitions.**  
    **Old:** “All of Europe now turned on him.”  
    **New:** “With his great army gone, all of Europe now turned on him.”  
    This adds a causal explanation to an existing overstatement.  
    **Old:** “In June 1815 he marched into Belgium to strike the British and Prussian armies before the Russians and Austrians could arrive.”  
    **New:** “The powers that had beaten him were gathering their armies again, and his one hope was to deal with them separately. In June 1815, therefore, he marched into Belgium to strike the British and Prussian armies before the Russians and Austrians could arrive.”  
    This adds mobilisation context and characterises separate defeat as his sole hope.

Other additions chiefly restate existing information: the Elba arrangement lasting less than a year, Russian resistance arising from the trade ban, revolutionary ideas spreading abroad, and conscription spreading with nationalism.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — the revision reintroduces the pre-Borodino error.**  
   > “So there was no battle, and there was no food.”

   “No battle” contradicts the immediately preceding acknowledgment of a battle at a burning city. Smolensk was fought in August, before Borodino in September; there were other engagements as well. “No food” also turns severe supply shortages into complete absence. The correct distinction is that Napoleon obtained neither the decisive victory nor the reliable supplies his campaign required. This is a regression introduced by the revision, not a repetition of a successfully fixed flag. [Fondation Napoléon, campaign chronology](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/napoleons-russian-campaign-from-the-niemen-to-moscow/).

2. **Minor — Austerlitz’s centre was weakened, not emptied.**  
   > “When they marched off the high ground in the centre to attack the weak end, they emptied the middle of their own line.”

   Allied troops remained around the Pratzen heights and resisted the French assault. The French exploited a weakened centre, then fought to take it; they did not simply enter an empty middle. [Jack Sigler, detailed study of the fighting at Austerlitz](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/the-battle-of-austerlitz-and-the-principles-of-war/).

3. **Major — the departure from Moscow is given a false immediate condition.**  
   > “In the middle of October, with winter coming and nothing to eat, the army turned for home.”

   The army left Moscow on 19 October carrying provisions intended to last twenty days. Its transport and supply arrangements subsequently failed, but it did not depart with nothing to eat. [Fitzwilliam Museum, “Napoleon in Russia”](https://fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/explore-our-collection/highlights/context/stories-and-histories/napoleon-in-russia).

4. **Minor — Europe did not unanimously abandon Napoleon after Russia.**  
   > “With his great army gone, all of Europe now turned on him.”

   Denmark remained allied to him through 1813, renewing its alliance that July. Several German allies also remained with him for part of the campaign. The accurate claim is that Russia’s victory enabled a new coalition, subsequently joined by Prussia and Austria. [Lex, history of the Franco-Danish alliance](https://lex.dk/Den_fransk-danske_alliance_1807-1814).

5. **Minor — the Prussian arrival at Waterloo is too late.**  
   > “Wellington's line held all afternoon against his cavalry, and in the evening the Prussians arrived on his flank.”

   Prussian forces began arriving during the afternoon and were already diverting French troops before the evening climax. Further Prussian formations arrived later. This also leaves “his flank” momentarily ambiguous between Wellington and Napoleon. [National Army Museum, battle narrative](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/battle-waterloo).

6. **Minor — the plebiscite’s subject is imprecise.**  
   > “The change was put to a vote, and more than three and a half million Frenchmen voted in favour, and a few thousand against.”

   Following “he had himself made emperor,” this makes the vote sound like the act that created his emperorship. Napoleon became emperor by the senatorial constitutional act of May 1804; the plebiscite specifically ratified hereditary succession in his family. The numerical statement need not change, but the object of the vote should be accurate. [Fondation Napoléon, constitutional chronology](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/from-life-consulship-to-the-hereditary-empire-1802-1804/).

## Clarity flags

- **Prohibited signposting:**  
  > “The difficulty is easy to picture.”  
  > “Its main rules can be said in a breath.”

  Both announce the explanation instead of giving it. The following sentences already do the work.

- **Redundant timing:**  
  > “The arrangement lasted less than a year.”  
  > “Ten months later Napoleon slipped away with a thousand men and landed on the French coast.”

  The second sentence immediately supplies the first sentence’s entire information.

- **Repetition around the naval stalemate:**  
  > “The battle left the two powers stuck.”  
  > “Each was supreme in its own element, and neither could get at the other.”  
  > “Since neither could strike directly, each tried to starve the other.”

  Alongside the existing Channel sentence and elephant-and-whale comparison, these explain the same obstacle repeatedly. “Neither could strike directly” also needs a narrower meaning than an inability to fight one another: the chapter soon describes Britain landing an army in Portugal.

- **An unnecessary audience absolute:**  
  > “Nobody watching could miss the meaning.”

  This claims a uniform response from every spectator. The interpretation of Napoleon’s self-coronation can stand confidently without that claim.

- **An unnecessarily universal consequence:**  
  > “For that reason, none of Napoleon's victories on land was ever final.”

  “Final” is undefined: final for a battle, a state or the European war? The following sentence about subsidised allies returning to war conveys the intended point more concretely.

- **The guerrilla passage momentarily contradicts its own setup:**  
  > “An army built to win a war in one great battle could find no battle to win.”

  The preceding paragraph describes repeated victories over Spanish regular armies. The intended distinction is between winning conventional battles and suppressing the guerrillas; the sentence needs to preserve that distinction.

- **A sentence foregrounds an unnamed example without explaining it:**  
  > “The Spanish regular armies won one startling victory and were then beaten again and again, without ever quite giving up.”

  A general reader is left wondering what made the victory startling or consequential. A short description of the French surrender and withdrawal would carry more meaning than the unexplained adjective.

There are no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit cross-references. Many added explanations—especially the rotating reserve and the survival of the Russian army—serve the style sheet’s instruction to give readers room.

## Overall

The revision improves continuity but is not entirely fact-neutral. Its clearest new problems are the empty centre at Austerlitz and the renewed assertion that there was no battle before Borodino. Several connecting sentences also duplicate explanations or introduce avoidable absolutes. Previously corrected flags have been excluded unless the revision reintroduced the problem.
