I found the following issues, ranked by seriousness. I found no clear direct contradiction with chapters 9–11, and no hedging stacks or explicit chapter-number cross-references. Several contradictions occur within chapter 22. No files were edited.

1. **Factual overstatement central to the argument — high confidence.**  
   > “Seen from a watchtower, then, the wall was not a barrier against an army.”

   Holding conquered territory and providing military defence were complementary functions. Walls, forts, gates and terrain formed a defensive system; an army’s ability to breach it does not make it cease to be a barrier. Even Di Cosmo’s expansionist interpretation describes fortifications intended to repel or contain nomads after conquest. The defensible distinction is between an obstacle and an impassable barrier. [Source: Nicola Di Cosmo, “The Origins of the Great Wall.”](https://edspace.american.edu/silkroadjournal/wp-content/uploads/sites/984/2017/09/The-Origins-of-the-Great-Wall.pdf)

2. **False absolute about Xiongnu society — high confidence.**  
   > “The herders had no towns.”

   Xiongnu territory contained permanent settlements, including the fortified agricultural and craft centre at Ivolga. Mobile pastoralism made conquest difficult, but the empire was not uniformly mobile or devoid of fixed targets. This also undermines “Second, he had nothing that could be captured.” [Source: archaeological research on Xiongnu settlements.](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00934690.2019.1598170)

3. **Misleading geography and internal contradiction — high confidence on regional agriculture; moderate on the immediate surroundings of Stein’s particular ridge.**  
   > “The farmland of China lay more than a thousand kilometres behind it to the east.”  
   > “There were no villages near it to protect, and no fields.”

   This conflates the old agricultural heartland with all farmland under Han control. Garrison families farmed in the Dunhuang oasis; the chapter later describes settlers, irrigation canals and garrison towns here. A particular stretch could face empty desert, but that does not establish that the frontier system protected no farming settlements. Distinguish old heartland from newly established frontier agriculture. [Source: Daniel Waugh’s account of Dunhuang and its garrisons.](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/lectures/wulec1.html)

4. **Incorrect expedition result — high confidence.**  
   > “The second, which was much larger, came home after four years of effort with about three thousand horses.”

   About 3,000 horses were obtained in Ferghana; only about 1,000 reached China. The four-year span, 104–101 BCE, covers the overall undertaking, not simply the second expedition. The distinction matters in a passage about the costs of obtaining horses. [Source: *History of the Former Han Dynasty*, translated by Homer Dubs, hosted by the University of Virginia.](https://www2.iath.virginia.edu/saxon/servlet/SaxonServlet?chunk.id=d2.29&doc.lang=english&source=xwomen%2Ftexts%2Fhanshu.xml&style=xwomen%2Fxsl%2Fdynaxml.xsl&toc.depth=1&toc.id=d2.1)

5. **Invented closure to a recorded disagreement — high confidence.**  
   > “Nobody had a reply.”

   The *Discourses on Salt and Iron* records replies: the scholars challenged military expansion and its costs, arguing for a different frontier policy rather than accepting the expenditure and merely failing to fund it. You can judge their answer inadequate, but cannot say they supplied none. The preceding paragraph already summarises their alternative. [Source: *Discourses on Salt and Iron*, especially “Basic Arguments” and “Frontiers, the Great Concern,” translated by Esson Gale.](https://xinxingren.gen.nz/classics/%E9%B9%BD%E9%90%B5%E8%AB%96_Salt_and_Iron.html)

6. **Incorrect generalisation about construction — high confidence.**  
   > “The wall in the desert is sixteen centuries older, and like almost every wall of its time it contains no stone at all.”

   Gravel itself consists of stone fragments; presumably the intended contrast is with masonry. More substantially, early northern fortifications did use stone, including stone-and-earth ramparts and stone forts. “Almost every wall of its time” is an unsupported generalisation, especially if read beyond China. [Source: Di Cosmo’s description of excavated construction materials.](https://edspace.american.edu/silkroadjournal/wp-content/uploads/sites/984/2017/09/The-Origins-of-the-Great-Wall.pdf)

7. **Counterexample breaks the technical explanation — high confidence.**  
   > “A wooden bow with real power has to be about as tall as the man who draws it, and nobody can swing such a thing across a horse's neck.”

   Long bows can be used from horseback: Japanese mounted archery is a clear counterexample. Compact composite bows offered practical advantages, but long-bow mounted shooting is not impossible. The claim about required length also needs specifications—wood, draw length and design—not the undefined “real power.” [Source: Japan’s government publication on *yabusame*, illustrating mounted use of a long bow.](https://www.gov-online.go.jp/pdf/hlj/20220901/hlj202209_14-15_Yabusame_Horseback_Archery_and_Samurai_Etiquette_in_Kamakura.pdf)

8. **Internal contradiction and misleading chronology.**  
   > “Sixty quiet years changed that.”

   Immediately before this, the treaty fails to stop raids, a palace burns, and scouts approach the capital. “Quiet” contradicts the account just given. The intended point appears to be recovery without sustained offensive war, which is different from sixty years of peace.

9. **An absolute defeats the chapter’s own economic evidence.**  
   > “If the state is the only seller, it can set the price, and the price becomes a tax that nobody can dodge.”

   The next discussion supplies an example of avoiding purchases: farmers returning to wooden tools. Monopoly revenue depends on purchases and enforcement; monopoly status does not make evasion or substitution impossible. This breaks the style rule against absolutes with straightforward exceptions.

10. **The argument promises impossibility, then narrates successful answers.**  
    > “For a farming state, such an enemy posed three problems, and none of them had a good answer.”  
    > “Third, he cost nothing.”

    Later, Han cavalry wins major campaigns, captures pasture and takes the corridor; crossbows provide another answer. Those answers were expensive and incomplete, which is the stronger argument already supported by the story. Likewise, an existing horse, bow and trained rider reduce a ruler’s mobilisation burden; they do not eliminate the resources or labour consumed by warfare.

11. **Reader orientation: the crucial geographical distinction is missing.**  
    > “They took a long corridor of oases that runs north-west between the mountains of Tibet and the Gobi desert.”  
    > “West of Dunhuang, the road ran from one oasis kingdom to the next around the rim of a great desert, and then over the mountains to Ferghana, Bactria and beyond.”

    A general reader may mistake the second desert for the Gobi or conflate the corridor with the oasis routes farther west. Identify the corridor as Hexi and the western desert as the Taklamakan, and establish their relationship to Dunhuang. The outline promises a map, but the draft contains none.

12. **Reader orientation: a major rewind is insufficiently anchored.**  
    > “Before the war began, the young emperor had tried something else.”

    The previous section has reached six years after Wu’s death. This jumps back to his youth, then moves through thirteen years of captivity and into later campaigns without clearly reconnecting those events to the corridor’s conquest. One chronological anchor would make the relationship between diplomacy, conquest and the horse expeditions much easier to follow.

13. **Signposting and discussion of the narrative’s own sequence.**  
    > “To see what it was doing there, we have to meet the people on the other side.”  
    > “It is worth seeing why it was made that way.”  
    > “Consider what it enclosed.”

    These announce the next explanatory move rather than making it. The first also describes what the narrative must do next, contrary to the prohibition on self-referential navigation. These are specific style violations, unlike ordinary transitions such as “Meanwhile” or “As a result.”

14. **Unnecessary discussion of a minor story’s reliability.**  
    > “The tale has surely improved in the telling.”

    This speculates about embellishment without identifying evidence. The preceding paragraph already attributes the story to Sima Qian. Under “Settle doubt before writing,” retain the attribution, establish any consequential uncertainty outside the body, or replace the anecdote if it cannot support the point.

15. **A small but checkable absolute — high confidence.**  
    > “The caterpillars that hatch from them eat the leaves of the mulberry tree and nothing else, and they eat day and night.”

    *Bombyx mori* larvae can eat some other leaves, although those foods may not support normal growth. Mulberry is the relevant food for the sericulture described; “and nothing else” adds an unnecessary false claim. [Source: experimental research in *Nature* on silkworm feeding.](https://www.nature.com/articles/182325a0)
