# Chapter 22 — Why was the Great Wall of China built?

**Line under the title:** The northern frontier of China, from about 2,200 to about 2,000 years ago.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,500 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

The wall that tourists visit is about 500 years old. The walls of the first Chinese empires were pounded earth, and in the desert they were layers of gravel and reeds. They were not built around farmland. They were built out in the pasture, around land that Chinese armies had taken from mounted herders, and later along the road to Central Asia. The herders, the Xiongnu, answered by forming an empire of their own, and for sixty years the Han paid it silk, grain, wine and brides for peace. Then one emperor chose war, and paid for it with state monopolies on salt and iron. The war won the Han a corridor to the west, a line of towers, and a trade neither side had planned. The wall could not keep riders out. What it could do was hold conquered ground, send warning, and make everyone who crossed go through a gate. The grassland and the farmland needed each other and could not govern each other.

## The story

1. **A ridge of reeds.** Answer first. Aurel Stein in the desert west of Dunhuang, 1907. The tourist wall is far later. How rammed earth is built. A wall with no farms behind it.
2. **Boys on sheep.** The Xiongnu as herders. Almost every written source is Chinese. The composite bow. Why a farming state could not beat a mounted archer.
3. **The whistling arrow.** The wall came first: the Qin take the pasture in the bend of the Yellow River. Modu makes an empire. The Han founder trapped for seven days. The treaty: silk, grain, wine and a princess, as brothers.
4. **Cocoons.** What the grassland lacked. How silk is made, from egg to reeled thread. What a Xiongnu ruler did with it, and what lies in the tombs in Mongolia. The defector's warning.
5. **Salt and iron.** Sixty years of paying and being raided anyway. The court's argument. Emperor Wu's wars, the horses lost, the monopolies, the debate held after his death.
6. **The envoy.** Zhang Qian's thirteen years. What he reported. The army sent for horses.
7. **Raked sand.** The corridor, the towers, the garrisons. The wooden strips: rations, signals, the sand strip, the archery test, the passes. What the wall was for.
8. **White camels.** Merchants calling themselves envoys. Soldiers paid in silk. The caravan routes. The Xiongnu ruler who submitted and was paid more than ever. End on the tomb and the rubbish heap.
