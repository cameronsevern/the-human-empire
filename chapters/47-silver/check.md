1. **Factual error — the ore grade is far too low.**  
   > “With this method, rock that held only a few grams of silver in a tonne was worth digging.”

   Contemporary refining thresholds were on the order of kilograms per tonne, not a few grams. Anezaki and Miyoshi report Capoche’s lower threshold as approximately 0.1%, equivalent to **1,000 grams per tonne**. Keep the explanation that amalgamation made poorer ores profitable, but remove this figure. **Confidence: high.** [Research, pp. 183–184](https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/56912/ohs_02_171.pdf).

2. **Factual error — lenders did not consistently charge more after defaults.**  
   > “Each time they came back, because nobody else in Europe had so much silver to pledge, and each time they charged more.”

   Research on the actual contracts finds no deterioration in lending terms after the 1575 default. Continued lending depended on profitable contracts, negotiated settlements and the bankers’ collective bargaining power; an automatic upward staircase of interest rates is misleading. **Confidence: high.** [Drelichman and Voth, *Contingent Debt and Excusable Defaults*](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/111205/1/econwp145.pdf).

3. **Factual error — interest was not necessarily concealed in exchange rates.**  
   > “The charge was hidden in the rate at which they changed Spanish money into Flemish money, since the church forbade open interest on loans.”

   Exchange transactions could conceal interest, but Philip’s contracts also explicitly stipulated it. One surviving asiento specifies **one per cent per month**. The passage presents one financial technique as the universal arrangement. **Confidence: high.** [Álvarez-Nogal and Chamley’s examination of the contract](https://ehes.org/2015/06/17/financing-steady-disbursements-with-a-credit-line-and-an-option-for-long-term-funded-debt-an-asiento-of-philip-ii/).

4. **Factual error and prohibited absolute — China did not use silver alone.**  
   > “A vast economy was thus short of the one metal it used for money, and silver was valued accordingly.”

   Ming China used **copper-alloy cash alongside silver bullion**. Silver became especially important for taxation and substantial transactions; copper money remained important in everyday exchange. “The one metal” gives the reader the wrong monetary system. **Confidence: high.** [Research on Ming–Qing monetary circulation](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/arcm.12617).

5. **Unsupported motive — lack of coinage does not mean lack of value.**  
   > “Atahualpa could offer a room full of them because he did not think he was giving away anything useful.”

   This invents Atahualpa’s reasoning and contradicts the preceding explanation of sacred metals. Gold and silver served religious, dynastic and political purposes; they were not useless decorations to their owners. The defensible explanation is that he offered precious objects to obtain his release. **Confidence: high that the stated motive is unsupported.** [Metropolitan Museum, *Ancient Andean Metalworking*](https://www.metmuseum.org/ko/essays/ancient-andean-metalworking).

6. **Doubtful claim — Potosí is presented as the established source of the ransom.**  
   > “Within a few years the Spaniards found the place the silver came from, and for two centuries they dug it out.”

   The later wording, “In 1545 they found it,” reinforces this identification. Potosí became the great colonial source, but that does not establish it as the source of Cajamarca’s treasure. **Porco** was a documented Inca silver mine supplying royal and sacred objects. Describe Potosí as a subsequent discovery, without assigning the ransom a provenance that has not been demonstrated. **Confidence: high on the unsupported identification; pre-Hispanic exploitation of Potosí remains debated.** [Archaeological research on Porco](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-68942010000200003&script=sci_arttext).

7. **Explanation gap — royal borrowing appears to depend almost entirely on silver.**  
   > “When a fleet was late, or a war went badly, he could not pay.”

   The surrounding account omits the domestic taxes supporting Philip’s finances. American bullion supplied a minority of royal revenue—roughly a fifth, with variation—while Castilian taxation was fundamental. Payment suspensions involved the relationship between expenditure, short-term borrowing and tax-backed debt, not simply a missing fleet. One sentence establishing the larger tax base would substantially improve the chapter’s central explanation. **Confidence: high.** [Fiscal-contract research](https://ehes.org/2015/06/17/financing-steady-disbursements-with-a-credit-line-and-an-option-for-long-term-funded-debt-an-asiento-of-philip-ii/).

8. **Disputed causal claim presented as settled.**  
   > “When the mines of Potosí faltered in the 1630s and 1640s, the shortage was felt in the markets of Canton.”

   This needs evidence specifically connecting Potosí’s output to Canton’s monetary conditions. The broader explanation of China’s seventeenth-century monetary troubles through falling foreign silver supplies is contested; Richard von Glahn challenges both its evidence and its mechanism. Global connections are established, but this particular causal chain is not equally secure. **Confidence: high that it requires qualification or replacement; medium on the underlying claim.** [Von Glahn, *Myth and Reality of China’s Seventeenth-Century Monetary Crisis*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/abs/myth-and-reality-of-chinas-seventeenthcentury-monetary-crisis/051515B7767AB41D91ED213342A6E348).

9. **Factual error — the route starts in the wrong country.**  
   > “Philip's soldiers in the Netherlands, moreover, were a thousand kilometres from Seville by a road that crossed the Alps.”

   The approximately thousand-kilometre military corridor ran from **northern Italy to the Netherlands**. Troops coming from Spain first crossed the Mediterranean. Seville is much farther away, and the sentence conflates these stages. **Confidence: high.** [Swiss National Museum on the Spanish Road](https://blog.nationalmuseum.ch/en/2022/10/the-camino-espanol-through-switzerland/).

10. **Factual error and prohibited absolute — the Dutch war did not occupy Philip’s entire reign.**  
    > “He fought the Protestant Dutch, who had rebelled against him, for the whole of his reign.”

    Philip became king in 1556; the revolt developed in the 1560s, with the war conventionally dated from **1568**. “For the last three decades of his reign” would fit. **Confidence: high.** [Royal Collection Trust chronology](https://col-militarymaps-frontend.rct.uk/eighty-years-war-dutch-war-of-independence-1568-1648).

11. **Chronological ambiguity — “the next century” points to the wrong date.**  
    > “By the middle of the next century, the textile towns of Castile had shrunk, the countryside was losing people, and the coins in a Spaniard's pocket were made of copper.”

    The immediately preceding date is **1600**, so a reader will reasonably understand this as around **1750**. The intended setting is evidently around **1650**, consistent with the chapter’s stated scope. Give that date directly. **Confidence: high.**

12. **Factual overstatement and style breach — “anything” makes the economic argument untenable.**  
    > “As a result, anything made in Spain cost more than the same thing made in France, Italy or the Netherlands, where the silver arrived later and thinner.”

    Research supports an argument that bullion inflows weakened the competitiveness of parts of Spain’s traded economy. It does not establish that **every Spanish product** became dearer than its foreign equivalent. Relative prices, wages and production costs are the relevant mechanism. Narrow the claim to affected industries, such as the cloth-making example that follows. **Confidence: high on the overstatement.** [Drelichman, *The Curse of Moctezuma*](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/eh/papers/0404/0404001.pdf).

13. **Factual overstatement — the road network was not uniformly stone-paved.**  
    > “Officials kept the accounts on knotted cords, and relays of runners carried messages along forty thousand kilometres of stone-paved road.”

    The network used different construction methods according to terrain, including unpaved surfaces. The approximate total length is defensible; applying “stone-paved” to all of it is not. **Confidence: high.** [Smithsonian explanation of road construction](https://americanindian.si.edu/inkaroad/engineering/question/how-did-inka-road-reflect-local-environment.html).

14. **Doubtful absolute — some ransom objects were kept intact.**  
    > “The Spaniards did not keep any of it as it was.”

    Accounts describe selected objects being reserved rather than melted. “Most” would preserve the point about destruction without the vulnerable “any”; the following “melted everything” has the same problem. **Confidence: medium-high; worth checking against the chapter’s preferred conquest source.** [Account in *Spanish America*, volume II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/51300/pg51300-images.html).

15. **Reader gap — the exchange-rate example establishes purchasing power in gold, not automatically in goods.**  
    > “Anyone who could carry silver to China would find it worth twice as much on arrival, and could spend it on silk and porcelain that sold in Europe and America for several times their cost.”

    The preceding six-to-one and twelve-to-one ratios show that silver bought twice as much **gold**. They do not by themselves establish twice the purchasing power in silk or porcelain. Explain that gold arbitrage and profits on imported goods are related but distinct opportunities. Also give the ratios a period rather than leaving them apparently valid throughout the story.

16. **Reader gap — the mechanism of final settlement disappears.**  
    > “No metal moved at all.”

    In the hypothetical transaction, the letter avoids shipping that particular payment from Spain to Antwerp. But the next sentence says the family settled later without explaining how its Antwerp funds were replenished. This leaves an apparent clash with the chapter’s physical movement of silver to European bankers. Specify that no chest of coins had to accompany the payment order, then briefly explain settlement.

17. **Reader gap — a new currency name appears without identification.**  
    > “A merchant in Mexico City who sent ten thousand pesos on the galleon could expect to double his money when the silk came back, or better.”

    The reader knows *reales* and *pieces of eight*, but has not been told that the silver peso here is the eight-real coin. Introduce that equivalence when the coin first appears.

18. **Continuity gap with chapter 40, rather than a direct contradiction.**  
    > “On the other side of the world there was a country that wanted silver more than anything else a foreigner could offer.”

    Chapter 40 says China “had little need of anything from across the sea.” The intervening monetary explanation can reconcile these statements, but the connection needs to be explicit: a country supplying coveted manufactures could nevertheless want imported monetary metal. Without that bridge, the reader may think the book has reversed its account of Chinese demand.
