## Factual flags

1. **Major — the new financing explanation wrongly excludes alternatives and flexibility.**

   > “The silver was the part that bankers would lend against, because it was the one large income that was not already spoken for.”
   >
   > “An arrangement like that has no slack in it.”

   These additions go beyond the earlier, now-corrected omission of domestic taxation. Royal credit contracts could draw on different revenues and permit conversion into funded debt. The documented 1595 Maluenda contract allowed repayment through sales of annuities; exercising that option released anticipated fleet revenues from their original commitments. The correct fact is that silver supported borrowing, but was neither the sole available financial resource nor part of an invariably inflexible arrangement. [Álvarez-Nogal and Chamley’s analysis of the contract](https://ehes.org/2015/06/17/financing-steady-disbursements-with-a-credit-line-and-an-option-for-long-term-funded-debt-an-asiento-of-philip-ii/).

2. **Major — a new sentence contradicts the chapter’s central explanation.**

   > “Silver in a mountain was of no use to a king in Spain until it had been shaped, counted and shipped.”

   Later the chapter says:

   > “He was borrowing against metal that was still inside a mountain in Bolivia, to be dug out by men who had not yet been drafted.”

   Anticipated silver revenue could obtain credit before the metal arrived. The correct distinction is between silver’s physical delivery and its usefulness as an expected source of repayment. The chapter itself supplies the contradiction; documented contracts likewise assigned claims on future fleets. [Source](https://ehes.org/2015/06/17/financing-steady-disbursements-with-a-credit-line-and-an-option-for-long-term-funded-debt-an-asiento-of-philip-ii/).

3. **Minor — useful vegetation did exist around Potosí.**

   > “Since nothing useful could be grown or cut nearby, everything had to be brought in.”

   The earlier sentence “Nothing grows there but coarse grass” is also too categorical. Research describes local dwarf trees and ichu grass, including grass used as camelid forage. Potosí depended heavily on imported provisions, but “nothing useful” and “everything” erase documented local resources. The correct fact is that the harsh environment forced residents to import most food and many other supplies. [Susan D. deFrance, “Diet and Animal Use in Colonial Potosí”](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0717-73562012000100002&script=sci_arttext).

4. **Major — not every Ming tax was paid in silver.**

   > “Copper coins still served for small purchases, but anything large, and every tax, was settled in silver.”

   The earlier check’s objection to silver being China’s sole monetary metal has been fixed. This is a separate remaining error: the conversion of taxes into silver was incomplete, and payments in kind continued. The correct fact is that silver became increasingly important in taxation and large transactions, while grain and other forms of tax payment survived. [Kent Deng, *Main Socio-economic Trends in Ming Qing China*, p. 17](https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/WorkingPapers/Economic-History/2023/WP364.pdf).

## Clarity flags

- **Announcement of a surprise, plus an unnecessary absolute:**
  
  > “The outcome was not what anyone would have predicted.”

  This announces the contrast supplied by the next sentence rather than developing it. “Anyone” also claims knowledge of everybody’s expectations.

- **Overextended transition:**
  
  > “When the temples had been stripped, there was nothing more to melt.”

  The intended distinction is between a finite stock of treasure and continuing mine production. “Nothing more” instead suggests that all accessible metal objects had disappeared.

- **Unclear comparison:**
  
  > “At first the work was easy.”

  Easy to dig, easy to smelt, or physically easy for the workers? The following sentences establish richer ore, not an absence of arduous labour.

- **Repetition around the miners:**
  
  > “The labour inside was divided between cutters and carriers.”
  
  This is immediately repeated by “The men who cut the ore…” and “Others carried it out.” Likewise, “a week of work meant a week underground” immediately precedes the more vivid Monday-to-Saturday description.

- **Repeated causal wording:**
  
  > “The weeks of rest were for that reason not rest at all. During his two weeks off he therefore hired himself out…”

  “For that reason” and “therefore” perform the same job in consecutive sentences. The added sentence also repeats the information the second sentence delivers.

- **An illustration becomes an ambiguous historical assertion:**
  
  > “More coins chase the same goods, and so each coin buys less. That is what happened.”

  Does “that” mean monetary inflation generally, or literally unchanged Spanish production of cloth, iron, and wheat? The new wording makes the latter reading possible without establishing it.

- **Repeated setup before the Pacific route:**
  
  > “Such a town was of little use without a reliable way to reach it from America and to get back. That problem had been solved a few years before. A sailor-monk had already worked out how to cross the Pacific in both directions…”

  The second sentence announces the answer that the third immediately supplies. “Such a town” also leaves unclear whether the claim concerns Manila generally or its role in Spanish trans-Pacific commerce.

- **Unexplained historical expression:**
  
  > “…which was the Portuguese turn of the sea on a larger ocean.”

  “Turn of the sea” is not explained for a reader without historical background. It sounds like a reference to an earlier discussion, although the wind-route explanation immediately before it can stand independently.

- **Unnecessary absolutes:**
  
  > “The profits were large enough to defeat any rule.”
  >
  > “…a coin is useful only for what it will buy.”

  The first exceeds the specific example of evaded shipping limits. The second excludes storing wealth and other uses without helping the inflation explanation.

- **Unclear period:**
  
  > “For most of this period the king was Philip II…”

  “This period” has no clear local boundaries. His stated reign, 1556–1598, is not most of the chapter’s advertised 1527–1650 span.

No hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions, or numbered chapter cross-references stood out.

## Overall

The revision preserves the existing numerical details but is not entirely fact-neutral. The main new problems are the exclusive account of royal credit, the contradiction about unmined silver’s usefulness, and the claim that Potosí had no useful local vegetation. Several other additions repeat adjacent explanations or introduce avoidable absolutes. The earlier fixes have not been re-flagged. No files were edited.
