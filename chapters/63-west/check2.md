## Factual flags

1. **Major — settlers did lose political representation during territorial government.**

   > “A family that went west therefore gave up nothing by going.”

   In this paragraph, “nothing” means political status and rights. Settlers moving from states into territories lost representation by voting members of Congress; territorial government also initially involved appointed officials. Eventual statehood did not preserve their existing political rights throughout the intervening period. The Northwest Ordinance expressly provided for a delegate who could debate but could not vote. [National Archives, Northwest Ordinance](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/northwest-ordinance).

2. **Major — the northern bison collapse is placed too early.**

   > “However, a people cannot stay in the field without food, and the victory could not bring the herds back.”

   Following Little Bighorn in 1876 and immediately preceding the 1877 surrenders, this implies that destruction of the northern herds had already occurred. Substantial northern herds survived; their final commercial destruction principally occurred in **1880–1884**. Food shortages and military pressure in 1876–1877 should not be equated with that later collapse. Hornaday’s contemporary account explicitly distinguishes the earlier southern slaughter from the later northern destruction. [William T. Hornaday, *The Extermination of the American Bison*, section “The Destruction of the Northern Herd”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17748/pg17748-images.html).

3. **Minor — two numbers do not uniquely identify the land.**

   > “A buyer needed only the numbers of a township and a section to know exactly which piece of ground was his.”

   The survey system also needs the **range**, directions, and reference meridian; subdivisions require further identification. Township and section numbers recur. The correct underlying point is that a standard survey description identified the parcel without the buyer visiting it. [Bureau of Land Management, *Specifications for Descriptions of Land*](https://www.blm.gov/sites/default/files/SpecificationsForDescriptionsOfLand.pdf).

4. **Minor — boarding schools did not follow the allotment programme chronologically.**

   > “Finally, the government reached for the children.”

   This follows the 1887 allotment law and its effects over fifty years. Yet Carlisle—the school invoked immediately afterwards—opened in **1879**, eight years before the Dawes Act. Boarding-school assimilation and allotment overlapped; the former was not the final stage after the latter. [National Park Service, Carlisle history](https://home.nps.gov/cibs/learn/historyculture/index.htm).

5. **Minor — Missouri’s admission date is wrong. Existing wording, not a new error.**

   > “In 1820 it had admitted Missouri as a slave state and drawn a line westward from its southern border across the rest of the Louisiana lands, with slavery permitted south of the line and forbidden to the north.”

   Congress passed the compromise and authorised Missouri’s state-making process in **1820**. Missouri entered the Union on **10 August 1821**. This is separate from the Missouri-exemption issue already fixed after the earlier check. [U.S. Senate, Missouri timeline](https://www.senate.gov/states/MO/timeline.htm).

6. **Minor — Congress did not charter both railway companies. Existing wording, not a new error.**

   > “The answer was a railway, and in 1862, in the middle of the civil war, Congress chartered two companies to build one, the first starting west from the Missouri river and the second starting east from California.”

   Congress incorporated the **Union Pacific**. The **Central Pacific** had already been incorporated under California law in **1861**; Congress subsequently authorised and supported its participation. “Authorised two companies to build” accurately describes the federal arrangement. [U.S. Supreme Court, *United States v. Southern Pacific Co.*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/259/214).

## Clarity flags

1. **A transition contradicts its own setup.**

   > “The plan worked too well.”

   The preceding sentence defines the plan as protecting Mexico against raiders. The following sentence demonstrates large-scale American settlement, not successful protection. Readers cannot tell which success “too well” describes.

2. **An unclear comparison interrupts the Cherokee account.**

   > “Their government went the same way as their farms.”

   “Went the same way” can suggest destruction or disappearance. Here it apparently means adopting American institutional forms. The insertion also follows the syllabary discussion rather than the farming description, weakening the connection.

3. **An addition revives an ambiguity the earlier revision had resolved.**

   > “The whole country could hear the continent being joined.”

   The preceding sentence now correctly explains the telegraph circuit. “Hear” reintroduces the suggestion of transmitted hammer sound, while “the whole country” adds an unnecessary absolute. This flags the **new sentence**, not the corrected circuit explanation.

4. **Horseback travel needs a time comparison.**

   > “On horseback a hunter could keep pace with a herd, and a war party could cover distances that nobody could cover on foot.”

   People on foot can cover great distances. The relevant advantage is speed or distance **within a given time**, not an absolute distance inaccessible to pedestrians.

5. **Several additions introduce exception-breaking absolutes.**

   > “Men who meant to fight did not bring a mother and her child.”

   > “They also did something that nobody had asked for.”

   > “Everything else these nations did, including their wars, rested on the herds.”

   Each extends a useful specific point into a universal assertion. Sacagawea’s presence reassured observers; Sequoyah devised a writing system; bison supported Plains subsistence and warfare. None requires the broader absolute.

6. **The population transition creates an unnecessarily exhaustive division.**

   > “Part of the rise was home-grown, since American families were large and food was cheap. The rest came by ship.”

   Besides excluding overland immigration, this conflicts with the chapter’s own account of approximately eighty thousand Mexicans becoming inhabitants of the enlarged United States without moving.

7. **Repeated explanations add padding rather than another step in the reasoning.**

   > “The colony was the prize, and Louisiana was to be its farm.”

   > “A farm for a colony he could not hold was of little use to him.”

   These repeat the adjacent explanation that Louisiana was intended to feed the sugar colony and that the plan collapsed.

   Similarly:

   > “The soil in question, after all, was the strip that both countries claimed.”

   The previous paragraph has just identified the disputed strip and explained Mexico’s position. The repetition delays Lincoln’s concrete challenge.

8. **One transition announces a point without supplying it.**

   > “The railways changed more than travel.”

   The next sentence begins explaining the change. This is a mild signpost of the kind the style sheet discourages.
