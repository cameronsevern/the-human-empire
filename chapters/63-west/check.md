I found the following issues, ordered by seriousness. Confidence refers to the correction, not the chapter’s overall argument. No files were edited.

1. **Doubtful factual claim — state-paid head bounties.**  
   > “Miners and state militias hunted them like animals, and the state paid bounties for their heads.”

   California financed militia campaigns against Native people, but that is different from paying per head. A specialist investigation finds no evidence of a statewide scalp- or head-bounty programme; documented local bounty offers should not be conflated with state reimbursement of militia expenses. Replace the bounty claim with the documented financing of murderous campaigns. **Confidence: high that the sentence needs correction.** [*California History*, “The California Indian Scalp Bounty Myth”](https://online.ucpress.edu/ch/article/100/2/4/196102/The-California-Indian-Scalp-Bounty-MythEvidence-of).

2. **Factual error and missing explanation — reaching the Pacific.**  
   > “In 1848 the United States reached the Pacific on paper.”

   The Oregon settlement with Britain secured American sovereignty over Pacific coastline in **1846**. The Mexican cession added California in 1848. Omitting Oregon also leaves a substantial part of the chapter’s central question unanswered: the opening’s purchases, Mexican war and Native dispossession do not explain the negotiated division with Britain. **Confidence: very high.** [Office of the Historian, Oregon Territory](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1830-1860/oregon-territory).

3. **Misleading factual compression — the Cherokee lawsuits.**  
   > “They hired lawyers and sued.”  
   > “In 1832 the Supreme Court of the United States ruled in their favour.”

   This merges two cases. The Cherokee Nation’s own suit, *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831), failed on jurisdictional grounds. The important 1832 victory was *Worcester v. Georgia*, an appeal against a missionary’s criminal conviction, whose reasoning upheld Cherokee sovereignty. The distinction needs one plain sentence. **Confidence: very high.** [National Archives, notice of the Worcester decision](https://docsteach.org/document/worcester-v-georgia/).

4. **Factual oversimplification — slavery in Mexican Texas.**  
   > “Most were from the cotton states, and they brought slaves, which Mexican law forbade.”

   Mexican restrictions changed over time, and Texas received an exemption from the 1829 abolition decree. Settlers also used legally permitted indenture arrangements to preserve slavery under another name. As written, the sentence makes all this settlement appear to have occurred under a straightforward, continuously applicable prohibition. **Confidence: very high.** [Texas State Historical Association, “Slavery”](https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/slavery).

5. **Factual error — the exception that defined the Missouri Compromise.**  
   > “In 1820 Congress had drawn a line across the Louisiana lands, with slavery permitted south of it and forbidden to the north.”

   **Missouri itself was exempt** and entered as a slave state north of the line. This is an essential exception, not incidental detail, because the paragraph explains the political balance between slave and free states. **Confidence: very high.** [Library of Congress, Missouri Compromise](https://guides.loc.gov/missouri-compromise).

6. **Factual overstatement — who could homestead.**  
   > “Under a law of 1862, any adult who settled on a quarter of a section and worked it for five years could have it for nothing, and more than a million and a half families eventually did.”

   “Any adult” is wrong: eligibility required citizenship or a declaration of intention to become a citizen, alongside other conditions. There were filing fees, and residence and improvement requirements. “Eligible settlers could claim 160 acres for a small filing fee…” would preserve the point. **Confidence: very high.** [Homestead Act text, National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/homestead-act).

7. **Internal contradiction and misleading terminology — “empty land.”**  
   > “There had always been a boundary between the settled country and the empty land beyond.”

   The chapter has already explained that the supposedly empty West contained Native territories, borders and political powers. Here the narrator adopts the settler assumption as fact. The census’s frontier was a statistical boundary of settlement density, not a boundary between inhabited and uninhabited land. Attribute that viewpoint or explain the census meaning. **Confidence: very high.** [Census Bureau, “Following the Frontier Line”](https://www.census.gov/dataviz/visualizations/001/).

8. **Factual overstatement — automatic statehood.**  
   > “A new territory would be governed from Washington only until it had sixty thousand free inhabitants.”  
   > “After that it would enter the Union as a state, on an equal footing with the original thirteen in every respect.”

   The Northwest Ordinance established this framework for the Northwest Territory, including constitutional conditions and the possibility of earlier admission. It was not a universal population trigger that automatically converted every later territory into a state. Describe a route to statehood, with admission by Congress. **Confidence: very high.** [Northwest Ordinance, National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/northwest-ordinance).

9. **Misleading factual claim — the conquest of California.**  
   > “American armies took California and New Mexico almost unopposed, landed on the Gulf coast, and marched inland by the road that Cortés had used.”

   Initial occupations encountered little resistance, but the completed conquest of California did not: Californio resistance included the substantial fighting at San Pasqual. Restrict “almost unopposed” to the initial occupations, or distinguish California from the occupation of Santa Fe. **Confidence: high.** [National Park Service, the California campaign](https://www.nps.gov/articles/mexwar.htm).

10. **Factual overstatement — reservations as unwanted land.**  
    > “They were confined to reservations, on the land that nobody else wanted, and fed on government rations.”

    Reservations could preserve portions of Native homelands that settlers subsequently coveted. The Great Sioux Reservation included the Black Hills, whose gold attracted invasion and confiscation. “Nobody else wanted” conceals precisely the continuing appetite for reservation land that the next sentence describes. **Confidence: high.** [Treaty of Fort Laramie, National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/fort-laramie-treaty).

11. **Misleading account of railway finance.**  
    > “The government paid them in land.”

    Federal support included **government-backed loans as well as land grants**. Because this paragraph explains how construction was financed, leaving out the loans materially distorts the mechanism. **Confidence: very high.** [Pacific Railway Act, National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/pacific-railway-act).

12. **Factual error — where the tanning breakthrough occurred.**  
    > “Then, in the early 1870s, tanners in the East found a way to turn bison hide into good leather, much of which went to make the driving belts for factory machines.”

    The commercially important innovation is traced to **England and Germany**, around 1871. “In the East” naturally means the eastern United States here and obscures the European demand driving the trade. **Confidence: high.** [Research paper hosted by the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/institute/working-papers/-/media/files/institute/working-papers/wp19-02.pdf?rev=0582a7f6c9fa4715844c1aeea9fb2696).

13. **Continuity problem with chapter 56 — the country’s “birth.”**  
    > “At its birth in 1783 the United States reached from the Atlantic to the Mississippi.”

    Chapter 56 distinguishes the **1776 declaration of independence** from British recognition in **1783**: “Two years later Britain recognised the United States as an independent country.” Say “When Britain recognised its independence in 1783…” to preserve that distinction. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [chapter 56](chapters/56-america/chapter.md).

14. **Chronological ambiguity against chapter 58.**  
    > “When the army he sent to that colony was destroyed by the former slaves of Haiti and by yellow fever, the plan collapsed.”

    In this position, the sentence suggests that France’s final military defeat preceded the Louisiana sale. Chapter 58 correctly puts the sale in spring 1803 and the final defeat in November. Napoleon sold after catastrophic losses had undermined his project, while fighting continued. This is an ambiguous compression rather than a wholly wrong causal argument. **Confidence: high.** [Chapter 58](chapters/58-haiti/chapter.md); [Office of the Historian, Louisiana Purchase](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1801-1829/louisiana-purchase).

15. **Overbroad “first.”**  
    > “The founder of the first such school explained that the aim was to kill the Indian in him and save the man.”

    Richard Henry Pratt founded Carlisle, the first **federally managed off-reservation** boarding school. Native boarding schools existed before it. Those qualifying words matter because “such school” currently refers simply to boarding schools for Native children. **Confidence: very high.** [Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center](https://carlisleindian.dickinson.edu/page/brief-history-school).

16. **Doubtful numerical threshold — surviving bison.**  
    > “By 1889 fewer than a thousand bison were left alive.”

    Hornaday’s contemporary estimate was **1,091**, including captive animals and Canadian herds. “About a thousand” is safer; “fewer than a thousand” needs an explicitly narrower geographical or population definition. **Confidence: high about the published estimate; lower about the actual population.** [Hornaday’s contemporary report](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/17748/17748-h/17748-h.htm).

17. **False absolute — railway timetables.**  
    > “Every town had kept its own clock by the sun, which made timetables impossible, and in 1883 the railway companies divided the country into four time zones by their own authority.”

    Timetables already existed. Different local and railway times made connections and scheduling complicated, not impossible. Railways also used shared time standards before 1883, so “every town” overstates the uniformity of the earlier system. **Confidence: very high.** [Library of Congress, history of standardized time](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2024/11/whose-time-is-it-anyway-a-brief-history-of-standardized-time-zones-in-the-united-states/).

18. **False absolute — ignorance of Louisiana.**  
    > “Nobody in Washington knew what the territory contained.”

    Jefferson and his officials had maps, published accounts and traders’ information. They lacked reliable knowledge of large areas, not all knowledge of the territory. Jefferson and Gallatin commissioned a composite map from existing sources before the expedition. **Confidence: very high.** [Library of Congress, the expedition’s preparatory map](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/treasures/tr22a.html).

19. **Unsupported absolute — immigrant ambitions.**  
    > “Every one of them wanted a farm.”

    This assigns an identical ambition to millions of people. Irish and German migrants also pursued urban trades, commerce, domestic service and industrial employment. The land-hunger argument needs “many,” not “every one.” **Confidence: very high that the universal claim is untenable.** [*Ethnic Americans*, immigration and urban settlement](https://ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu/book/dil01/dil01_02.pdf).

20. **Reader loses the constitutional mechanism.**  
    > “The states were divided into those that allowed slavery and those that had ended it, and the two groups were evenly balanced in the Senate.”

    Chapter 56 explains Congress’s two houses but does not explain that **each state receives two senators regardless of population**. Without that sentence, a general reader cannot see why admitting one state threatened the balance, or why population growth did not settle the issue.

21. **Reader loses the chronology — the Civil War appears without introduction.**  
    > “The argument over them began before the war was over, and thirteen years later it broke the country in two.”  
    > “The answer was a railway, and during the civil war Congress chartered two companies to build one, the first starting west from the Missouri river and the second starting east from California.”

    The first sentence does not explicitly identify the rupture as the Civil War; the next section moves back to 1848 and then treats “the civil war” as established knowledge. A brief identification and date would orient readers without taking over the later chapter.

22. **Unexplained historical term.**  
    > “Within a year most of the victors had come in to the agencies and given up their guns and horses.”

    “Agencies” has not been explained. Readers may imagine government departments in cities. Identify them as government posts administering reservations and distributing rations. This directly falls under the style guide’s requirement to explain historical terms on first appearance.

23. **An important visual mechanism is unclear.**  
    > “A telegraph wire had been attached to the last spike, so that the blow of the hammer was heard in every city in the country at the same moment.”

    Readers may infer that the telegraph transmitted actual sound. The wired spike and hammer were intended to complete an electrical circuit and produce signals at receiving stations. “Every city” is also an unnecessary absolute. Explain the signal rather than implying an audio broadcast. **Confidence: high.** [National Park Service, the ceremonial spikes and telegraph wiring](https://www.nps.gov/gosp/learn/historyculture/four-special-spikes.htm?nr_email_referer=1).

24. **Clear style-guide violation — announcing the explanation.**  
    > “What the United States had bought needs to be stated carefully.”

    This is a signpost about how the narrator will explain something. The following sentence begins the explanation itself. Delete the announcement and let the concrete distinction between territorial claims and actual possession carry the paragraph.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-reference, or body cross-references. Apart from the chronological points above, I found no clear contradictions with chapters 56, 58 or 60.
