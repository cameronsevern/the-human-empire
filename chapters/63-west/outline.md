# Chapter 63 — How did the United States get so big?

**Line under the title:** North America, from 1803 to 1890.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

The United States crossed a continent in less than a century by three means: it bought the claims of European powers, it took half of Mexico in a war, and it removed the Native nations who actually held the land, by treaty, by force and by destroying what they lived on. What made the expansion stick was people. The American population doubled every generation, and the government had a simple machine for turning land into farms: survey it into squares, sell it cheap or give it away, and admit each new region as a state equal to the old ones. Railways then tied the whole together. To the settlers it was the filling of an empty land promised by God. To the Cherokee, the Comanche, the Lakota and the Mexicans of the north it was a conquest. Every new territory also reopened one question: would slavery be allowed there?

## The story

1. **Opening: the river at Memphis.** Answer first. A French traveller watches a nation cross the Mississippi in December 1831.
2. **Three cents an acre.** 1803: Napoleon sells what he does not hold. What was actually bought. Two captains sent to look.
3. **The grid.** How land became property: the survey of squares. New states as equals. A population that doubled every generation.
4. **A newspaper in two languages.** The Cherokee do everything asked of them, win in the highest court, and are removed anyway.
5. **The horse nations.** The Comanche and the Lakota as powers in their own right. What Comanche raids did to northern Mexico.
6. **American blood.** Texas. The war of 1846. Half of Mexico, and gold found nine days before the treaty. The question of slavery in the new land.
7. **Seven days.** The railway across the continent, who built it, what it did to distance and to time. Free farms.
8. **Hides.** The destruction of the bison and the end of the plains nations' independence. Reservations and schools.
9. **Ending: 1890.** The census finds no frontier left. A creek in South Dakota.

## Left for later

The Civil War. Immigration and the industrial cities. The United States overseas. Native American rights in the 1900s.
