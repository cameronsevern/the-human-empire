## Factual flags

1. **Major — incorrect optical limit; existing error reinforced by the revision.**
   
   > “A beam of light cannot draw a line finer than its own waves, just as a thick brush cannot paint a thin line.”
   
   Features can be smaller than the wavelength used to print them. ASML describes printing **38-nanometre features using 193-nanometre light**. Resolution depends on wavelength, optics, and the manufacturing process. Shorter wavelengths help; wavelength is not an absolute minimum line width. [ASML’s explanation](https://www.asml.com/en/technology/lithography-principles/light-and-lasers).

2. **Major — authentication history; newly introduced.**
   
   > “Nobody had needed to prove who they were, because everybody already knew.”
   
   Early network users did use passwords to authenticate to host computers. A contemporary December 1973 report discusses weak passwords, compromised privileged credentials, and unauthorised access. The correct distinction is between **host-level authentication** and the absence of universal authentication built into the network—not the absence of any need to establish identity. [RFC 602](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc602.txt).

3. **Minor — packet switching does not eliminate idle capacity; newly introduced.**
   
   > “Pieces of many messages could share the same lines, so nothing sat idle.”
   
   Sharing allows one user’s traffic to occupy capacity another is not using. A link can still sit idle when there are no packets to send. The correct claim is that packet switching reduces wasted capacity, not that it eliminates idleness. [IETF guidance on shared capacity](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc3819).

4. **Minor — data centres are not required for every communication; newly introduced.**
   
   > “Every search, message and film draws on them.”
   
   Internet messages and video can travel directly between endpoints; they need not be stored or processed in the large facilities just described. Most familiar commercial services depend heavily on data centres, but the universal claim is false. [The IETF’s WebRTC specification describes direct endpoint communication](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8825.html#section-2.1).

5. **Minor — wrong nationality; unchanged.**
   
   > “The Englishman called the pieces packets.”
   
   Donald Davies was Welsh, born in Treorchy. “The British physicist” would also fit the chapter’s existing introduction. [Welsh Government account, printed pages 23 and 68](https://businesswales.gov.wales/sites/business-wales/files/Innovation/Welsh%20Acheivements%20-%20%20English%20-%20%20September%202013.pdf).

6. **Minor — compressed chronology; unchanged.**
   
   > “Within a year, eight of them had had enough of him and resigned together to start a rival firm, Fairchild.”
   
   Shockley’s laboratory opened in **1956**; the eight departed in **September 1957**. “Within a year” does not fit the establishment and recruitment chronology, particularly after the chapter’s preceding 1955 date. “The following year” after a 1956 establishment date would work. [Computer History Museum chronology](https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/silicon-comes-to-silicon-valley/).

7. **Minor — funding authorisation confused with payments; unchanged.**
   
   > “In 2022 the United States restricted the sale of the most advanced chips, and of the machines that make them, to China, and began paying companies to build factories on American soil.”
   
   The CHIPS Act authorised the incentives in 2022. The first preliminary agreement came in December 2023; the first final commercial fabrication award came in September 2024. For this programme, **authorised funding** is correct for 2022; **began paying companies** is not. This is separate from the export-control wording already corrected after the earlier check. [December 2023 announcement](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/DCPD-202301105/pdf/DCPD-202301105.pdf), [first final award](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDOC/bulletins/3b7ac73).
