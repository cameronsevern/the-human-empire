## Factual flags

1. **Major — new: factories confused with oil-dependent ships.**

   > “In other words, the ships and factories that Japan was so proud of ran largely on American fuel.”

   Japan’s dependence on imported petroleum does not establish that its factories predominantly used American fuel. Industrial energy came chiefly from coal and waterpower. The correct distinction is between oil-dependent naval and transport operations and the broader industrial energy supply. The US Strategic Bombing Survey explicitly identifies coal and waterpower as Japan’s principal industrial energy sources. [*Coals and Metals in Japan’s War Economy*, p. 9](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/NHC/NewPDFs/USAAF/United%20States%20Strategic%20Bombing%20Survey/USSBS%20Coals%20and%20Metals%20in%20Japan%27s%20War%20Economy.pdf).

2. **Minor — new: the northern option closes too early.**

   > “After that the north looked closed, and the generals looked for an easier direction.”

   Following the 1939 defeat directly with this sentence implies that a northern offensive ceased to be a serious option then. Japan still prepared for a possible attack on the Soviet Union in summer 1941. The army and navy decided on 9 August to concentrate on the southern advance. The 1939 defeat discouraged northern expansion; it did not settle the choice permanently. [Japanese Monograph No. 77, “The Decision to Go South”](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/Japan/Monos/pdfs/JM-77_Preparations4Manchuria%3C1943/JM-77.htm).

3. **Minor — new: freezing is presented as an unconditional spending ban.**

   > “Frozen money still belonged to Japan, but it could not be spent.”

   The funds could not be spent **without American authorization**. The freeze established a licensing system, and licensed withdrawals continued, including funds for Japanese diplomatic establishments and personnel. The distinction matters because the subsequent denial of access for oil purchases explains how the freeze became an oil embargo. [State Department instructions, 7 August 1941](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1941v04/d679).

4. **Minor — unchanged: Japanese exclusion was not literally total.**

   > “Five years later the United States passed an immigration law that shut out Japanese entirely.”

   The 1924 law barred ordinary Japanese immigration but retained specified exceptions, including returning lawful residents, qualifying students, ministers, and professors. “Entirely” is therefore false, even when restricted to immigration rather than temporary visits. [Secretary of State’s explanation to the Japanese ambassador, 1924](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1924v02/d310).

5. **Minor — unchanged: voting eligibility was not universal among men.**

   > “It had political parties, elections in which all men could vote, a lively press, and prime ministers who came from the largest party.”

   The franchise covered men aged 25 and over, with exclusions including people receiving public or private poor relief. “All men” obscures both the age threshold and substantive exclusions. [National Archives of Japan](https://www.archives.go.jp/learning/archive_collection_1/), [National Diet Library](https://www.ndl.go.jp/modern/e/cha3/description13.html).

## Clarity flags

- **Explicit signpost:** “The consequence followed step by step.” This announces the explanation rather than giving it, contrary to the stylesheet.

- **Another signpost:** “That last fact mattered most.” The next sentence supplies the reason. This sentence merely announces its importance.

- **Redundant explanation:** “The government intended to observe the forms, narrowly.” / “That is, it meant to keep to the outward rules of diplomacy, if only just.” The revision retains the obscure formulation and then translates it. One plain statement would do.

- **Repeated conclusion:** “The ships that mattered most, then, had escaped.” The preceding two sentences already establish that the carriers were absent and would decide the war.

- **Repeated conclusion:** “He meant that some risks have to be taken blind.” The immediately preceding image of shutting one’s eyes and jumping already communicates this.

- **Ambiguous transition:** “Two years later it happened again.” The preceding sentence contains both the shooting and the later death. The intended reference is clearly another prime-ministerial assassination, but “it” briefly suggests repetition of the whole sequence.

- **Unsupported universal wording:** “The lesson was not lost on anyone.” This adds no identifiable information and claims universal awareness. It is a direct example of the stylesheet’s exception-breaking absolutes.

- **Overgeneralized outcome:** “Politicians who stood in the way were shot, and the country went easy on the men who shot them.” As an introduction to the 1932 case it works, but its unrestricted wording clashes with the subsequent account of the 1936 leaders being executed.

- **Geographical unit switch:** “He moved his government a thousand miles up the river…” Elsewhere distances use kilometres and metres. A consistent system would make scale easier to follow.
