1. **Factual error affecting the central argument — high confidence.**  
   > “For eighty years after that they faced nothing worse than rebels and bandits, and against those the old weapons were good enough.”

   After 1760 the Qing fought major external campaigns in Burma, Vietnam and Nepal. The Burmese campaigns were disastrous; the Vietnamese intervention also ended in defeat. Relative security after the destruction of the Zunghars is a defensible explanation; eighty years without foreign wars is not. [Joanna Waley-Cohen, *The Culture of War in China*](https://www.battle-of-qurman.com.cn/literature/WaleyCohen-2006.pdf).

2. **Factual error and break of the rule against absolutes — high confidence.**  
   > “No enemy had ever attacked it from the sea.”

   The Qing had faced Zheng Chenggong’s maritime forces, including his enormous expedition up the Yangzi against Nanjing in 1659. China’s experience of seaborne enemies was older still. The unfamiliar threat was Britain’s particular naval capability, not attack from the sea itself. [Bruce Elleman, *A History of the Modern Chinese Navy*](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781000393217_A40919161/preview-9781000393217_A40919161.pdf).

3. **Doubtful precision in the opening — high confidence that the formulation needs changing.**  
   > “China lost because its army and navy were two hundred years out of date, and its government was too small, and too sure of itself, to see the danger in time.”

   “Two hundred years” is not a supportable measurement of the entire military establishment. Qing military innovation continued into the eighteenth century; the decisive divergence developed particularly between about 1760 and 1840. Chapter 50’s account of effective Qing artillery and logistics makes the blanket dating especially jarring, although that is a tension rather than a strict contradiction. [Tonio Andrade, *The Gunpowder Age*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691178141/the-gunpowder-age).

4. **Factual error and contradiction with chapter 50 — high confidence.**  
   > “Every danger to China, for two thousand years, had come on horseback from the north and west.”

   Chapter 50 has already described Russian enemies arriving along the Amur and the Ming exhausting their treasury fighting the Japanese in Korea. Neither fits this formulation. Zheng’s maritime campaign supplies another clear exception. “The greatest longstanding frontier threats” would preserve the argument without inventing an exclusive pattern. [Elleman’s naval history](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781000393217_A40919161/preview-9781000393217_A40919161.pdf).

5. **Misleading identification of the belligerent — high confidence.**  
   > “He had in fact started a war, and it would show that the largest and richest empire on Earth could not defend itself against a trading company's gunboats.”

   Britain fought a state-directed war using Royal Navy warships, British troops and Company forces. *Nemesis* belonged to the Company, but the expedition was not simply its gunboat operation. Following chapter 54’s account of the Company conquering India, readers are particularly likely to take this distinction literally. The destruction of the opium precipitated the crisis; Britain’s decision to intervene turned it into war. [National Army Museum, “First China War”](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/first-china-war-1839-1842).

6. **Two misleading economic claims — high confidence.**  
   > “There was one product for which China would pay silver, and the East India Company controlled the supply.”

   Chinese buyers also purchased substantial quantities of Indian raw cotton and other imports. Nor did the Company control the whole opium supply: Malwa opium from outside its production monopoly was a major competitor, alongside Turkish opium. Distinguish the Company’s Bengal monopoly from the wider trade. [MIT’s Canton trade figures](https://visualizingcultures.mit.edu/rise_fall_canton_01/cw_essay02.html); [“Poor Man’s Crop: Evading opium monopoly,” *Modern Asian Studies*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-asian-studies/article/poor-mans-crop-evading-opium-monopoly/199D0743201BF76C8ADA72F625B85FAD).

7. **Factual overstatement and prohibited absolute — high confidence.**  
   > “The court never committed itself to the effort.”

   Self-strengthening had powerful sponsors at court, notably Prince Gong and Wenxiang, and received imperial authorization. Support was divided and inconsistent, but it was not absent. That distinction matters to the explanation of why reform disappointed. [Heidelberg research on late-Qing political discourse](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/volltextserver/26142/1/Stupperich_2019_Ordering_the_Age.pdf).

8. **Chronological error — high confidence.**  
   > “After six weeks the senior British official on the spot told the merchants to give in, and promised, with no authority to do so, that the British government would pay them for their losses.”

   Charles Elliot ordered surrender on **27 March 1839**, shortly after confinement began. The detention and physical delivery of the opium extended over subsequent weeks. The sentence places his decision at the end of that process. [Elliot’s public notice, reproduced in parliamentary evidence](https://digital.soas.ac.uk/content/CB/00/00/01/90/00001/CB00000190_00001.pdf).

9. **Factual error — high confidence.**  
   > “After the second failure he collapsed, and lay in a delirium for days.”

   Hong’s visions followed his **third** examination failure, in 1837. His fourth failure came in 1843, when he reconsidered the Christian literature. [Wiley’s biographical entry](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781405198073.wbierp0715).

10. **Overcompressed territorial history; chronological tension with chapter 50 — high confidence.**  
    > “Russia, which had not fired a shot, offered its services as a mediator and came away with a million square kilometres of Qing territory along the Pacific coast, where it founded a port and named it Vladivostok, ‘ruler of the east’.”

    This combines two settlements: roughly 600,000 square kilometres north of the Amur in **1858**, and roughly 400,000 east of the Ussuri in **1860**. Much of that territory was inland. Russia also used military pressure, so “mediator” alone disguises its role. Chapter 50 correctly places the Amur acquisition in the 1850s; this passage appears to move everything to the 1860 settlement. [Encyclopedia of China, “Treaty of Aigun”](https://www.berkshirepublishing.com/ecph-china/2018/01/13/treaty-of-aigun/); [*China’s Boundary Treaties and Frontier Disputes*](https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Tibet%20and%20China/1982%20Chinas%20Boundary%20Treaties%20and%20Frontier%20Disputes%20by%20Chang%20s.pdf).

11. **Misleading treaty chronology and missing explanation — high confidence.**  
    > “The new treaties made opium legal, opened ten more ports and the whole interior to foreign merchants and missionaries, and placed foreign ambassadors in Beijing.”

    Much of this settlement originated in the **1858 Tianjin treaties and supplementary trade arrangements**, then was enforced and extended in 1860. The missing middle matters: readers never learn that an earlier agreement failed to settle the conflict, or that Qing forces defeated an Anglo-French assault at the Taku forts in 1859. A short account would explain both the renewed expedition and why Beijing became the target. [National Army Museum, “Second China War”](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/second-china-war).

12. **Doubtful generalization — medium-high confidence.**  
    > “Women were declared the equals of men.”

    The Taiping land programme explicitly allocated land without distinction between men and women. That does not establish a general declaration of equality: prescribed roles, religious teaching and leadership practices remained strongly patriarchal. State the concrete reform rather than converting it into comprehensive equality. [The Taiping economic programme, Columbia University](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/ps/cup/taiping_economic_pgm.pdf); [research on Taiping women’s status](https://qsyj.ruc.edu.cn/CN/abstract/abstract998.shtml).

13. **Doubtful causal simplification — medium-high confidence.**  
    > “From the 1820s silver began to flow out of China, for the first time since the Spanish opened their mines in the Andes.”

    A substantial **net** silver outflow beginning in the 1820s is defensible. The paragraph’s explanation of it as simply the result of opium is not settled scholarship: monetary conditions and changes in international trade also mattered. “For the first time” additionally makes an unnecessary three-century claim. Retain the net outflow and its tax consequences, but avoid implying that opium alone explains the monetary crisis. [Richard von Glahn, “The Changing Significance of Latin American Silver in the Chinese Economy”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/revista-de-historia-economica-journal-of-iberian-and-latin-american-economic-history/article/changing-significance-of-latin-american-silver-in-the-chinese-economy-16th19th-centuries/D3045EA55D654DCDCDCB3874E481A0B4).

14. **Misidentification — high confidence.**  
    > “Then the British commander, to punish the emperor for the torture and death of a party of envoys, ordered it burned.”

    The decision was Lord Elgin’s, acting as Britain’s plenipotentiary. The military commander was General Sir James Hope Grant. “British envoy” would identify the decision-maker accurately without adding another name. [National Army Museum, “Second China War”](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/second-china-war).

15. **Misleading chronology — high confidence.**  
    > “Its duties on imports were fixed at a low rate that it could not change.”

    In this position, the sentence attributes the tariff settlement to the August 1842 treaty. Article X instead required a tariff **to be fixed subsequently**; the detailed arrangements followed in 1843. [Treaty text, USC](https://china.usc.edu/node/20462); [Columbia’s explanatory notes](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/main_pop/ps/ps_china-nanjing.htm).

16. **Misleading account of the weapon — high confidence.**  
    > “The first shot she fired hit a powder store and blew a junk to pieces.”

    The celebrated explosion was caused by a **Congreve rocket**, not one of the two swivel guns just described. *Nemesis* had also participated in the bombardment of the forts before attacking the junks; “the first shot she fired” is too broad. [Peter Perdue’s account at MIT](https://visualizingcultures.mit.edu/opium_wars_01/ow1_essay03.html).

17. **Conflated ceremonies in the closing image — high confidence.**  
    > “It was handed back at midnight on 30 June 1997, in a ceremony held in pouring rain, a hundred and fifty-five years after the treaty.”

    The rain-soaked outdoor event was the earlier British farewell ceremony. The midnight handover took place indoors at the Convention and Exhibition Centre. Date the transfer unambiguously as midnight **as 1 July began**. [Contemporary report on the farewell](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/the-british-leave-a-bit-of-bad-weather-behind-them-1.86895); [Hong Kong government’s handover bulletin](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/dib/0701.htm).

18. **Reader loses the connection between economic hardship and rebellion.**  
    > “In that region lived a village schoolteacher named Hong Xiuquan.”

    “That region” could mean Canton, its former transport hinterland, or southern China generally. The preceding paragraph suggests that displaced transport workers explain Hong’s following, but the story then moves directly to his visions. Locate his home and the movement’s early recruiting grounds, and explain how religious followers became an armed movement. Chapters 50, 54 and 64 do not supply that missing link.

19. **Reader is given an unexplained change of scale.**  
    > “Two years later his followers, by then a host of half a million, fought their way to the Yangzi and took Nanjing, the old southern capital.”

    The jump from a preacher among poor hill communities to half a million followers needs one intervening step. Also clarify whether this number means fighting soldiers or a moving population including families and other followers.

20. **Reader cannot interpret the statistic.**  
    > “The trade simply went on, and doubled.”

    Doubled by when, and measured in volume or value? If the intended comparison is roughly 40,000 chests before the war against roughly 76,000 in 1865, say that it nearly doubled over the following two decades. Otherwise readers will understand an immediate consequence of the treaty. [MIT’s import series](https://visualizingcultures.mit.edu/opium_wars_01/ow1_essay01.html).

21. **Explicit signpost prohibited by style.md.**  
    > “The speed of the defeat astonished the Chinese, and needs explaining.”

    “Needs explaining” announces the explanation instead of giving it. This is precisely the prohibited signpost form; the historical observation about astonishment can stand separately.

22. **Absolutes that require correction or substantiation.**  
    > “Cities changed hands several times and were massacred each time.”

    “Each time” turns documented recurrent atrocities into an exceptionless rule. Specify that repeated captures brought repeated massacres, or give one concrete city.

    > “In China, the period that opened with Lin's trenches is known as the century of humiliation, and every schoolchild learns its dates.”

    “Every schoolchild” is an unnecessary universal claim. The substantive point is that the period occupies a prominent place in Chinese historical education.
