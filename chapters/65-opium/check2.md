## Changed facts

No existing numbers, names, dates or quotations were changed or removed. Most additions restate existing information. These changes add factual content, causal claims or materially broader wording. For insertions, “old” gives the preceding sentence where useful.

1. **Purpose of the embassy’s gifts**
   
   **Old:** “There were telescopes, clocks, a planetarium, cannon and fine pottery.”
   
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by: “The hope was that a court which had seen such things would want to buy more of them.”
   
   Adds a specific commercial intention.

2. **Britain’s dependence on tea**
   
   **Old:** “The tax on it brought in about a tenth of the government's income.”
   
   **New:** “The tax on it brought in about a tenth of the government's income, so the trade was not one that Britain could give up.”
   
   Adds a claim of necessity to the revenue figure.

3. **Scope of the opium monopoly**
   
   **Old:** “It made opium a monopoly.”
   
   **New:** “It made opium a monopoly, which means that nobody else was allowed to deal in it.”
   
   Introduces an inaccurate definition; see factual flag 2.

4. **End of the Company’s involvement**
   
   **Old:** “The Company was careful not to carry the drug to China itself, since that might cost it the right to buy tea.”
   
   **New:** “The Company was careful not to carry it there itself, since that might cost it the right to buy tea. Its own part therefore ended at the auction.”
   
   Adds a broader claim than the original distinction about shipping.

5. **A single cause of the silver drain**
   
   **Old:** No corresponding sentence.
   
   **New:** “The court now had two troubles with a single cause: a banned drug that millions were smoking, and silver draining out of the country.”
   
   Newly makes opium the exclusive explanation.

6. **Legal consequences of the 1843 agreements**
   
   **Old:** “Further agreements the next year fixed its duties on imports at a low rate, written into the treaty, and provided that a British subject accused of a crime in China would be tried by a British consul under British law.”
   
   **New:** “One fixed its duties on imports at a low rate, written into the treaty, so that China could no longer change them by itself. Another provided that a British subject accused of a crime in China would be tried by a British consul under British law, which put him out of reach of Chinese judges.”
   
   Adds explicit consequences concerning tariff autonomy and jurisdiction.

7. **What the treaty omitted**
   
   **Old:** “The treaty said nothing about the opium trade.”
   
   **New:** “On the trade that had started the war, however, the treaty was silent. It said nothing about opium.”
   
   The second sentence broadens the claim and makes it false.

8. **Military stagnation**
   
   **Old:** “For eighty years after that they fought border wars and rebels, and against those the old weapons were good enough.”
   
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by: “Nothing forced the Qing to make better ones.”
   
   Also newly added: “One side had been standing still while the other was driven forward.”
   
   These turn relative military divergence into categorical absence of pressure and change.

9. **The emperor’s letter as evidence**
   
   **Old:** “By 1840 the gap was very wide, and nobody at the Chinese court had measured it.”
   
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by: “The emperor's letter of 1793, with its scorn for ingenious objects, shows how little the court thought there was to measure.”
   
   Adds an inference about military understanding from the diplomatic letter.

10. **Hong’s interpretation**
    
    **Old:** “It explained his dream.”
    
    **New:** “To Hong, it explained his dream.”
    
    Correctly identifies this as Hong’s belief.

11. **Why provincial elites opposed the Taiping**
    
    **Old:** “The Taiping were finally worn down by new forces raised and paid for by scholar-officials in the provinces, men who defended the Confucian order because it was their whole world.”
    
    **New:** The same sentence, followed by: “The Taiping, by smashing the Confucian shrines, had made enemies of the very men who could organise the provinces against them.”
    
    Adds a specific causal connection. It is consistent with the chapter’s interpretation.

12. **Counting foreign invasions**
    
    **Old:** “The emperor fled.”
    
    **New:** “The emperor fled. For the second time, a foreign army had gone where it chose in China.”
    
    Introduces an unqualified historical count; its intended scope needs specifying.

13. **Reason the palace burning took so long**
    
    **Old:** “It took three and a half thousand men two days.”

## Factual flags

1. **Major — the treaty explicitly mentioned opium.**
   
   > “It said nothing about opium.”
   
   Article IV required China to pay six million dollars for the surrendered opium. The accurate distinction is that the treaty did not settle the future legality of the **opium trade**. The revision loses that distinction. [Treaty of Nanking, Article IV](https://archive.margaretthatcher.org/doc04/820912%20fco%20spc%20stdy%20PREM19-0792%20f147.pdf).

2. **Minor — the monopoly explanation contradicts the following account.**
   
   > “It made opium a monopoly, which means that nobody else was allowed to deal in it.”
   
   Private merchants were expressly allowed to purchase and resell Company opium—the chapter itself describes their auctions and shipments. The monopoly concerned controlled production and procurement in the Company’s eastern Indian territories, followed by its auction sales; it was not a prohibition on anyone else dealing in the product. [Bhattacharya, “India in the Rise of Britain and Europe”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0260107920907196).

3. **Minor — the Company’s financial involvement continued after auction.**
   
   > “Its own part therefore ended at the auction.”
   
   Its role in carrying the drug could end there, but its commercial involvement did not. Traders deposited proceeds in the Company’s Canton treasury and obtained bills payable in India or London; the Company used the money to purchase Chinese goods. Distinguish physical shipment from financing. [Bhattacharya](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0260107920907196).

4. **Major — the new sentence makes the silver explanation exclusive.**
   
   > “The court now had two troubles with a single cause: a banned drug that millions were smoking, and silver draining out of the country.”
   
   This is a newly introduced regression, rather than a repeat flag against the corrected net-outflow sentence. Evidence connects silver movements to currency preferences, coin availability and exchange conditions as well as merchandise trade. Opium can remain central to the chapter’s explanation, but “a single cause” excludes demonstrated monetary mechanisms. Alternatively, the sentence could accurately identify what the **court believed** caused the drain. [Irigoin, Kobayashi and Chilosi, “China inside out”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ehr.70020).

5. **Minor — crews are confused with resident merchants.**
   
   > “Their crews lived for the trading season in a row of warehouses on the waterfront, forbidden to enter the city, to bring their wives, or to learn Chinese.”
   
   The factories housed merchants and commercial staff. Ordinary sailors generally remained with their ships at Whampoa, visiting Canton for work or leave. Replace “crews” with the appropriate trading personnel. [Hong Kong Museum of History’s account of the Canton trade](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/200909/14/P200909140312.htm).

6. **Minor — the canal chronology conflates successive capitals.**
   
   > “For twelve centuries the barges on that canal had carried the rice of the south to the capital in the north.”
   >
   > “Beijing, in other words, ate what the canal brought it.”
   
   Together these imply twelve centuries of supplying Beijing as the capital. The Sui canal system served Luoyang; the direct route serving Beijing developed under the Yuan, centuries later. The canal’s long history and Beijing’s nineteenth-century dependence are both sound, but they need separating. [“Grand Canal System,” *The Geography of Transport Systems*](https://transportgeography.org/contents/chapter1/emergence-of-mechanized-transportation-systems/grand-canal-china/).

## Clarity flags

1. **An ambiguous pronoun now points toward both armies.**
   
   > “The numbers, however, hid the difference that mattered, which was in the weapons. They were armies from another age.”
   
   “They” appears to include the British force just discussed. The intended subject is the Qing armies.

2. **The buyers/suppliers distinction does not match its example.**
   
   > “He began with the buyers. He arrested Chinese dealers by the hundred and set up clinics for smokers who wanted to stop.”
   
   Dealers are also suppliers. The actual distinction here is between the domestic Chinese market and foreign importers.

3. **Unexplained specialist vocabulary.**
   
   > “In three years it brought fewer than twenty thousand men to China, a large share of them Indian sepoys.”
   
   “Sepoys” needs an immediate definition or can simply become “soldiers.” “Armed hulks” likewise needs a brief explanation that these were stationary storage ships.

4. **Planning language replaces narrative.**
   
   > “Two pressures were at work there. The first was simple crowding.”
   >
   > “The second came out of the treaty itself.”
   
   This announces and labels an explanatory list. The population pressure and disrupted transport work can supply the transitions themselves.

5. **A rhetorical question functions as a signpost.**
   
   > “How had such a power fallen so far behind?”
   
   It announces the explanation that follows. This is close to the signpost previously removed, although the wording is new.

6. **Several additions repeat what the reader has just learned.**
   
   > “The speed of the defeat astonished the Chinese, and they had reason to be astonished.”
   
   The second clause repeats the first; the following historical examples already establish the reason.
   
   > “A sect of the poor had turned into a rebellion.”
   
   Immediately repeats the preceding account of congregations becoming an army.
   
   > “The new treaties went much further than the old one.”
   
   Announces what the following concrete provisions demonstrate.

7. **The new historical count needs a boundary.**
   
   > “For the second time, a foreign army had gone where it chose in China.”
   
   Presumably this means the second Opium War. Without that boundary, it sounds like the second foreign invasion in Chinese history. “Where it chose” also sits awkwardly beside the preceding account of the allied defeat in 1859.

8. **Exceptionless formulations exceed the style sheet’s allowance.**
   
   > “Nothing forced the Qing to make better ones.”
   >
   > “Each war produced better muskets, better cannon and better ships, since any king who fell behind lost.”
   
   These turn the defensible argument about differing competitive pressures into universal rules. The problem is “nothing,” “each” and “any,” not the chapter’s confident interpretation.

9. **One comparison promises exhaustive evidence.**
   
   > “It governed four hundred million people with a few tens of thousands of officials, and it collected a smaller share of the country's wealth in tax than any state in Europe.”
   
   “Any state in Europe” requires a comprehensive comparison. The argument needs the contrast with the major European powers, not an exceptionless ranking.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or prohibited chapter cross-references.

## Overall

The revision generally improves the explanations, but it is not fact-neutral. Correct the treaty sentence and the new monopoly, financial-role and single-cause claims before accepting it. Several added summaries also slow the narrative. Previously fixed flags have been excluded except where new wording reintroduces a problem. No files were edited.
