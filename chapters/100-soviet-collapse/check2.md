## Factual flags

1. **Major — new explanation misstates the economic mechanism.**

   > “Without the old orders to bind them, factories stopped delivering to one another.”

   State orders had not disappeared. The contemporary joint international study estimated that they still covered about **75% of production in 1990**. Supply failures occurred within a deteriorating mixture of state allocation, partial enterprise autonomy, hoarding and inadequate markets. The added clause turns a damaged system into an abolished one. [IMF, *A Study of the Soviet Economy*, enterprise reform](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9789264134683/ch010.xml).

2. **Major — new description conflates reform with support for dissolution.**

   > “Gorbachev was caught between two fires: reformers who wanted the republics let go, and hardliners who wanted them held by force.”

   Reformers did not share a single programme of releasing the republics. Yeltsin himself helped formulate the April 1991 agreement for a renewed Union with substantially greater republican autonomy. The relevant distinction includes reformers seeking a looser Union and national movements seeking independence; the sentence collapses these into one position. [Library of Congress country study, hosted by the US Marine Corps](https://www.marines.mil/Portals/1/Publications/Soviet%20Union%20Study_1.pdf).

3. **Minor — new transition creates a misleading sequence.**

   > “Then East Germans took to the streets of their own cities.”

   Following the September opening of Hungary’s frontier, “Then” suggests domestic demonstrations began afterwards. Leipzig’s Monday demonstrations began on **4 September 1989**, before the Hungarian opening on 11 September. The October march represents an escalation, not the beginning. [Deutschlandmuseum chronology](https://www.deutschlandmuseum.de/en/history/calendar/1989-09-04-the-sed-hates-mondays/).

4. **Minor — new verdict overstates the alcohol campaign’s failure.**

   > “The result was the opposite of what he wanted.”

   The fiscal damage and illicit distilling were real, but the campaign also reduced alcohol consumption and mortality. Russian male life expectancy increased by more than three years during the campaign period. Failure to achieve the desired economic improvement does not make all its results the opposite of its aims. The adverse consequences can stand without this blanket conclusion. [National Research Council, “The Anti-Alcohol Campaign and Variations in Russian Mortality”](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK233403/).

5. **Major — existing statistic uses the wrong denominator.**

   > “Within a year the union, which called itself Solidarity, had ten million members, a third of all the working people in the country.”

   Ten million was approximately **two-thirds of the working population**, not one-third. The sentence substantially understates Solidarity’s penetration of the workforce. Retaining the membership total alone would also satisfy the style sheet’s preference for one number per point. [WZB historical study of opposition in Poland](https://bibliothek.wzb.eu/pdf/2008/iv08-402.pdf).

6. **Minor — existing sentence assigns the capture to the execution date.**

   > “Its dictator was booed at his own rally on live television, fled by helicopter from the roof, and was caught and shot with his wife on Christmas Day.”

   Nicolae and Elena Ceaușescu were captured on **22 December**, held at Târgoviște, and executed on **25 December**. “Caught and shot … on Christmas Day” incorrectly joins those dates. [Museum of the Romanian Revolution, account of the trial](https://www.muzeulrevolutiei.ro/pagina/procesul-ceausestilor).

These are not repetitions of the earlier check’s corrected findings.

## Clarity flags

- **Signpost sentences:**  
  > “His diagnosis was simple.”  
  > “Closer to home, he made a still bigger change.”  
  > “Their reason was a deadline.”

  Each announces an explanation supplied immediately afterwards. These fit the style sheet’s prohibition on announcing that a point is coming. The substantive sentences already provide the connection.

- **Immediate repetition:**  
  > “As a result, he satisfied neither side. He had lost the reformers, and the hardliners despised him.”

  The first sentence adds no information to the second.

  > “Even an unopposed candidate was not safe. Party bosses who ran unopposed were defeated by voters crossing out their names.”

  Repeats “unopposed” and announces the same point twice.

- **Padding:**  
  > “For Poles it was an extraordinary event.”  
  > “People got by however they could.”  
  > “The result was a new class of tycoons.”

  The surrounding sentences already establish astonishment, improvised livelihoods and extraordinary private wealth. These additions do little explanatory work.

- **Ambiguous referent:**  
  > “It was not only a joke.”

  Immediately after the nail joke, this can suggest that the nail anecdote itself is documented fact. The chandelier example demonstrates the same incentive problem; it does not establish that the nail story happened.

- **Exception-breaking absolutes:**  
  > “In such a system nobody was rewarded for making what a customer wanted, or punished for making rubbish, and the results were easy to see.”

  “Nobody” asserts a universal rule about rewards and penalties, where the argument concerns dominant incentives.

  > “For anyone who had saved, that meant ruin: the savings of a lifetime would no longer buy a pair of boots.”

  “Anyone” includes people holding foreign currency or other assets. The intended claim concerns the destruction of ordinary ruble savings.

- **Unclear republic count:**  
  > “Russians were half its population. The rest belonged to more than a hundred peoples, organised in fifteen republics…”

  This makes the fifteen republics sound like an organisation of the non-Russian half. Fifteen was the total, including Russia.

- **Unexplained terms and identities:**  
  > “Hungary, meanwhile, cut a hole in the Iron Curtain itself.”  
  > “The alliance that had been formed against the Soviet Union took in Poland, Hungary and the Czechs.”

  “Iron Curtain” is introduced without explanation. The alliance is never identified as NATO, and “the Czechs” shifts from countries to a people. Both are avoidable obstacles for the intended reader without a history background.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit cross-references.
