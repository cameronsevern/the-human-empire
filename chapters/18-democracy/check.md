I found no clear contradiction with chapters 9–11, and no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or body cross-references. The main problems are factual compression and contradictions within chapter 18. No files were edited.

1. **The ending contradicts the chapter’s own evidence.**
   > “They had not shown that the first could be done without the second, and they ran out of time to try.”

   Here “the first” means democracy and “the second” imperial domination. Yet an earlier paragraph says: “It ran for another eighty years, without the tribute, and in some ways more carefully than before.” Restored democracy operated after the empire’s defeat in 404 BCE. Athens subsequently built another naval alliance, but that does not establish that democracy required empire. The conclusion needs to distinguish their intertwined origins from a claim of necessary dependence. **Confidence: high.** Sources: the chapter’s own preceding account; [Aristotle, *Athenian Constitution*, §§41–42](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/athenian_const.2.2.html).

2. **The account wrongly makes early democracy sound restricted to armoured farmers.**
   > “At that point it was still a system for farmers who owned armour.”

   Poor citizens already participated in the assembly before Salamis. Aristotle attributes assembly and court participation for the poorest property class, the *thetes*, to Solon, before Cleisthenes. Naval service increased their political weight; it did not first admit them to political participation. That distinction matters to the chapter’s central argument. **Confidence: high on their pre-Salamis participation; less certain about the precise powers of Solon’s courts.** [Aristotle, *Athenian Constitution*, §7](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26095/pg26095-images.html).

3. **Two Persian kings have become one person.**
   > “The Great King came back ten years after Marathon, and this time he came in person, with an army too large for any Greek force to meet in the open and a fleet rowed by his subjects from Lebanon, Egypt and the Greek cities of the Turkish coast.”

   Darius ordered the Marathon expedition; his son Xerxes led the invasion ten years later. Darius died in between. “Came back” and “this time” tell an uninitiated reader that the same king returned. “His successor” would preserve the few-names approach without confusing the succession. **Confidence: high.** [Herodotus, *Histories* 7.1–4](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Herodotus/7a%2A.html).

4. **The postwar settlement skips a reversal essential to the story.**
   > “Sparta ruled the Greek cities more harshly than Athens had, and handed the cities of the Turkish coast back to the Great King as agreed.”

   This makes the surrender of the Asian Greek cities sound like an immediate, straightforward consequence of victory in 404 BCE. Sparta subsequently fought Persia over those cities, sending forces to Asia in 400/399. Persian sovereignty was decisively recognized in the King’s Peace of 387/386—about seventeen years after Athens surrendered. The following “A few years later” compounds the compression. **Confidence: high.** [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Greco-Persian Political Relations”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/greece-i/).

5. **“Never invaded” has a concrete counterexample.**
   > “Persia never invaded Greece again.”

   In 393 BCE, Pharnabazus and Conon brought a Persian-backed fleet and troops to the Greek mainland, raided the coast and occupied Cythera. The safe claim is that Persia never repeated Xerxes’ great invasion aimed at subjugating mainland Greece. **Confidence: high.** [Xenophon, *Hellenica* 4.8.7–9](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-xenophon-hellenica).

6. **Spartan decline is reduced to battlefield deaths.**
   > “It had always been short of citizens, and decades of war used them up.”

   The falling number of full citizens was not simply a count of men killed. Property concentration, impoverishment and loss of eligibility for citizenship also mattered, alongside demographic and military losses. Moreover, the following “fewer than a thousand” belongs to the mid-fourth century, rather than securely to exactly a century after 480. The present explanation turns a social and political contraction into straightforward military attrition. **Confidence: high that the explanation is incomplete; medium on the relative weight of the causes.** [Timothy Doran, *Demographic Fluctuation and Institutional Response in Sparta*](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/3pk467b6); [Aristotle, *Politics* 1270a–1271a](https://scriptura.si/works/aristotle-politics/5/).

7. **The old council did not lose its last powers.**
   > “In the thirty years after Salamis, the last powers of the old noble council were stripped away and handed to the assembly, the council of five hundred and the courts.”

   Ephialtes removed the Areopagus’s principal political supervisory powers in 462/461 BCE. It retained important jurisdiction, notably over deliberate homicide. “Last powers” is therefore wrong, and “old noble council” also arrives without enough explanation to distinguish it from the council of five hundred. **Confidence: high.** [Aristotle, *Athenian Constitution* 57.3–4](https://www.ime.gr/chronos/04/en/sources/main_soc.html).

8. **The Athenians demonstrably discussed tensions between democracy and empire.**
   > “The Athenians saw no contradiction in it.”

   This attributes a single untroubled view to an entire population. In Thucydides’ account of the very Mytilenean debate used next, Cleon explicitly questions democracy’s capacity to rule an empire and describes Athens’s rule over its allies as tyranny. That does not make him an opponent of empire, but it defeats the claim that Athenians recognized no tension. **Confidence: high.** [Thucydides, *History* 3.37](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~simon/Athens/Thucydides3/).

9. **The fleet-building vote loses its immediate political context.**
   > “They were sure that he would come back, and one of them had a plan for when he did.”

   The subsequent account presents Themistocles’ shipbuilding proposal wholly as preparation for another Persian invasion. Herodotus says he persuaded the assembly by invoking Athens’s war with nearby Aegina. Preparing for Persia may also have been his intention, but the argument citizens were offered should not disappear—especially when their foresight is then praised. **Confidence: high about the stated Aeginetan rationale; medium about Themistocles’ underlying intentions.** [Herodotus, *Histories* 7.144](https://lexundria.com/hdt/7/mcly).

10. **Naxos is given a motive the evidence does not establish.**
    > “About ten years in, the island of Naxos decided that the danger had passed and announced that it was leaving.”

    Naxos revolted and was forcibly subdued, but its specific reason is not known. “Decided that the danger had passed” supplies a convenient motive as fact. The revolt and siege carry the argument without it. **Confidence: high that this motive is unsupported.** [P. J. Rhodes, “Democracy and Empire,” *The Cambridge Companion to the Age of Pericles*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DF761B0404B840BF8B7C62AB8B4A271B/9781139001151c01_p24-45_CBO.pdf/democracy_and_empire.pdf).

11. **The military stalemate is confused with an inability to inflict damage.**
    > “For a long time neither side could hurt the other.”

    Both sides inflicted substantial damage without winning decisively. The next sentences themselves describe Sparta burning Athenian farms; later, Athenian operations encouraged helot desertion. “Neither could force the other to surrender” expresses the intended strategic point. This is also an absolute broken by the surrounding narrative. **Confidence: high.** [Thucydides, *History*, Book III](https://classics.mit.edu/Thucydides/pelopwar.3.third.html), alongside the chapter’s own examples.

12. **The war’s beginning appears to follow an event that happened during it.**
    > “As Athens grew, the cities allied with Sparta pressed it to act before the whole Greek world was paying tribute.”

    This follows Mytilene’s revolt and punishment, then introduces the outbreak of war. A general reader will naturally put those events in that order. The war began in 431 BCE; Mytilene revolted in 428 and surrendered in 427. The narrative can move backwards, but it needs a brief chronological reset. **Confidence: high.** [Thucydides, *History*, Book III](https://classics.mit.edu/Thucydides/pelopwar.3.third.html).

13. **The jury machine’s row size needs reconciliation with the ancient account.**
    > “When he had finished, the tickets formed rows across the face of the stone, with one man from each letter in every row.”

    With ten letters already introduced, this describes ten men per row. Aristotle’s account specifies two machines per tribe, five columns per machine and one white cube per five tickets. Modern reconstructions are not entirely uniform—Harvard’s teaching account also discusses ten-column arrangements—but the chapter presents its version as the routine described by one ancient writer. Identify which reconstruction is being followed and make the row size and cube calculation consistent with it. **Confidence: high about Aristotle’s description; medium that every ten-column reconstruction should be rejected.** [Aristotle §§63–64, translated in OCR’s source booklet](https://www.ocr.org.uk/Images/421546-j198-01-athens-in-the-age-of-pericles-462-429-bc-prescribed-sources-booklet.pdf); [Harvard Center for Hellenic Studies, jury allocation](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/discussion-series-athenian-law-lectures-5/).

14. **The ram was bronze-covered, not a solid bronze extension.**
    > “At the front, at the waterline, the hull ended in a beak of solid bronze.”

    The ram was a bronze sheath fitted over a substantial wooden structure connected to the hull. This matters in a passage explaining how the ship worked: the wooden structure transmitted the impact. **Confidence: high.** [Greek Ministry of Culture, “Shipbuilding”](https://2500years.culture.gov.gr/en/chapters/naupigiki/chalkino-emvolo-pliou).

15. **Sparta’s defeat and Messenia’s liberation are compressed into one apparent operation.**
    > “Another Greek city broke its army in a single afternoon, marched into the helots' country and set them free.”

    Thebes defeated Sparta at Leuctra in 371 BCE; the invasion and liberation of Messenia followed in 370/369. The sentence also risks suggesting that all Spartan helots were freed, rather than the Messenian population discussed earlier. Name Thebes, indicate the interval and specify Messenia. Those details help the reader understand who has suddenly overturned the balance of power. **Confidence: high.** [Susan Alcock and colleagues, research on Messenia’s liberation and subsequent settlement](https://www.ascsa.edu.gr/uploads/media/hesperia/25067953.pdf).

16. **An unbounded superlative breaks the rule against fragile absolutes.**
    > “By about 2,460 years ago the system was fully grown, and it asked more of its citizens than any government has asked since.”

    “More” has no defined measure: political participation, military service, taxation, labour or sacrifice. The defensible point is that Athenian democracy required unusually extensive direct participation. No ranking against every subsequent government is needed. **Confidence: high as a style finding.**

17. **An important place-name is introduced without explanation.**
    > “One sixtieth of every payment was set aside for the goddess Athena, and the amounts were carved, city by city, on a marble slab taller than two men, which stood on the Acropolis for all to read.”

    A reader with no Greek history background has not been told what the Acropolis is. A short identification as Athens’s fortified hill and sacred centre would locate both the inscription and the later temple expenditure. This falls under the requirement to explain historical terms on first appearance.

18. **This sentence announces an illustration instead of starting it.**
    > “What that meant for a subject city was shown on the island of Lesbos.”

    It is a signpost to the Mytilene story, which the next sentence already begins. Under the unusually strict “No signpost sentences” rule, start with the city and its revolt. **Confidence: medium; this is a smaller editorial issue than the factual findings.**

19. **The closing paragraph begins by summarizing the argument.**
    > “The Athenians had shown that ordinary people, chosen by chance and paid a small wage, could run a state, command a fleet and argue their way to decisions in the open.”
    >
    > “They had also shown that such a people could rule others as hard as any king.”

    These recapitulate what the chapter has already demonstrated, contrary to “Endings land on something” and “No summary of what was just said.” The northern kingdom supplies a forward-looking image afterwards, but the summary—and the contradictory conclusion flagged in item 1—weakens that landing.
