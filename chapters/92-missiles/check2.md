## Changed facts

No existing number, name, date or quotation was changed or removed. However, several additions introduce factual claims or change the implications of existing sentences. “Added” below means there was no corresponding old sentence.

1. **The submarine’s information and ability to recognise signals**
   
   **Old:** “She had been out of radio contact with Moscow for days.”
   
   **New:** “She had been out of radio contact with Moscow for days, so her officers had no idea what was happening in the world above them.”
   
   **Old:** “The message had not reached the submarine.”
   
   **New:** “However, the message had not reached the submarine. As a result, the men inside had no way of telling a signal from an attack.”
   
   These expand missing communications into categorical claims about what the officers could know or infer.

2. **Rocket propulsion and its reception**
   
   **Old:** “A rocket carries its own fuel and the oxygen to burn it, and so it can climb out of the atmosphere, coast through space and fall on its target faster than sound.”
   
   **New:** “A rocket carries its own fuel and the oxygen to burn it, so it does not need air, and so it can climb out of the atmosphere, coast through space and fall on its target faster than sound.”
   
   **Added:** “Both victors saw its value at once.”
   
   The first makes an accurate technical explanation explicit; the second adds an assertion about the timing of American and Soviet recognition.

3. **The beginning of Castro’s campaign**
   
   **Old:** “In December 1956 Castro, Guevara and eighty others sailed from Mexico in a leaking motor yacht built for twelve.”
   
   **New:** “Castro's attempt to bring that regime down began in near disaster.” The old sentence follows unchanged.
   
   The addition makes the 1956 expedition the beginning of Castro’s attempt to overthrow Batista.

4. **The cause and sequence of American sanctions**
   
   **Old:** “Washington stopped buying Cuban sugar and banned almost all trade.”
   
   **New:** “In reply, Washington stopped buying Cuban sugar and banned almost all trade.”
   
   Following the seizure of “the rest of American property,” this now explicitly makes both American measures responses to completed nationalisation.

5. **The explanation of the Bay of Pigs defeat**
   
   **Old:** “Castro, who had read the same lesson from Guatemala as Guevara, had replaced the old army with his own.”
   
   **New:** “Castro, who had read the same lesson from Guatemala as Guevara, had replaced the old army with his own, so there were no generals to lose their nerve.”
   
   The revision adds a categorical conclusion about the new army’s leadership.

6. **The identification of the missile sites**
   
   **Old:** “The film showed long canvas-covered trailers, launch stands and fuel trucks, laid out in a pattern that the analysts recognised from pictures of missile bases in the Soviet Union.”
   
   **New:** “The film showed long canvas-covered trailers, launch stands and fuel trucks. What gave them away was their arrangement: they were laid out in a pattern that the analysts recognised from pictures of missile bases in the Soviet Union.”
   
   Recognition of a pattern becomes an explicit explanation of how identification was achieved.

7. **Kennedy’s purpose in choosing the quarantine**
   
   **Old:** “He chose something slower.”
   
   **New:** “Instead he chose something slower, which would leave Khrushchev time to think.”
   
   This adds a purpose to the choice.

8. **Readiness, correspondence and military control**
   
   **Added:** “Every day brought them closer to being ready to fire.”
   
   **Added:** “Before Washington could answer, the terms changed.”
   
   **Added:** “Until then the crisis had been a contest between two governments that were, for the most part, in control of their own forces.”
   
   These add claims about missile readiness, the sequence of correspondence, and the degree of control before 27 October.

9. **The two aircraft incidents**
   
   **Old:** “At about the same hour another American spy plane, on a flight to the North Pole to sample the air for traces of nuclear tests, was blinded by the northern lights, lost its way, and wandered deep into Soviet airspace over Siberia.”
   
   **New:** “At about the same hour, thousands of kilometres away, a second accident unfolded.” The account of the lost aircraft follows.
   
   “Second accident” retrospectively classifies the deliberate shootdown over Cuba as an accident too.

10. **Public reaction in Cuba**
    
    **Old:** “Crowds in Havana chanted a rhyme calling Khrushchev a sissy who took back what he had given.”
    
    **New:** “His people shared his anger.” The old sentence follows unchanged.
    
    A reported crowd reaction becomes a claim about the Cuban population generally.

11. **The likely outcome of an invasion**
    
    **Old:** “At a meeting in Havana that year, a retired Soviet general told Kennedy's former secretary of defence about the hundred battlefield nuclear weapons, and said that the local commander had been prepared to use them on the landing beaches.”
    
    **New:** That sentence remains, followed by: “In other words, the invasion that most of Kennedy's advisers had first wanted would very likely have been met with nuclear fire.”
    
    A participant’s reported preparedness becomes the narrator’s counterfactual judgment. This is a substantive addition, but not automatically a factual error.

12. **The order of the 1963 measures**
    
    **Old:** “In 1963 a direct teleprinter line was installed between Washington and Moscow. That summer the two countries and Britain signed a treaty banning nuclear tests in the air, in space and under the sea, which put a stop to most of the fallout that had been turning up in children's milk teeth all over the world.”
    
    **New:** “In 1963 a direct teleprinter line was installed between Washington and Moscow.” Then: “A second step followed. That summer the two countries and Britain signed a treaty banning nuclear tests in the air, in space and under the sea.”
    
    “Followed” introduces an explicit chronological relationship.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — the submarine scene retains an unresolved chronology problem.**
   
   > “He argued that the Americans were signalling and not attacking, and that they should surface and ask Moscow for orders.”
   >
   > “The argument worked. The captain's temper cooled, and B-59 came up into the glare of American searchlights.”
   
   The chapter makes Arkhipov’s intervention precede and cause surfacing. Arkhipov’s own account, published with the National Security Archive’s updated reconstruction, places the critical confrontation **after B-59 surfaced to recharge its batteries**, when American aircraft activity alarmed Savitsky. Earlier accounts differ, but this staged sequence conflicts with that eyewitness evidence. The supported core is that Arkhipov helped avert escalation; the precise pre-surfacing sequence should not be treated as established. This is the unresolved portion of the earlier flag, not a repetition of the removed three-person vote. [National Security Archive, eyewitness records and reconstruction](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/russia-programs/2022-10-03/soviet-submarines-nuclear-torpedoes-cuban-missile-crisis).

2. **Minor — the new opening misdates the beginning of Castro’s campaign, while the next sentence misdates departure.**
   
   > “Castro's attempt to bring that regime down began in near disaster.”
   >
   > “In December 1956 Castro, Guevara and eighty others sailed from Mexico in a leaking motor yacht built for twelve.”
   
   Castro had already led the failed **Moncada attack on 26 July 1953**. The *Granma* expedition was a renewed attempt. It departed Mexico on **25 November 1956**, arriving in Cuba on **2 December**. The departure error predates this revision; the misleading beginning is new. [Congressional Research Service chronology](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL30386.html); [UNAM study of the expedition](https://rilzea.cialc.unam.mx/jspui/bitstream/CIALC-UNAM/CL820/5/cuba_exilio_granma_santana_adalberto.pdf).

3. **Major — “In reply” reinforces a reversed sequence of economic retaliation.**
   
   > “When the American-owned oil refineries refused to process Soviet crude, he took them over, and then the rest of American property on the island. In reply, Washington stopped buying Cuban sugar and banned almost all trade.”
   
   The sugar-quota cut occurred **between** the refinery seizures and the wider nationalisations. Refineries were seized in June–July 1960; Eisenhower cut the remaining sugar quota on **6 July**; broader nationalisations followed in August, with remaining American properties expropriated in October. Export restrictions came in October 1960, and the broader embargo in February 1962. The passage reverses an important link in its own explanation of escalating retaliation. [CRS chronology](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL30386.html); [State Department chronology](https://1997-2001.state.gov/regions/wha/cuba_chronology.html).

4. **Major — the ships’ positions and turnaround reproduce the “eyeball to eyeball” myth.**
   
   > “On the morning of the twenty-fourth the quarantine took effect, with Soviet freighters a few hours from the line.”
   >
   > “The ships nearest the line had stopped in the water, and some were turning round.”
   
   The relevant missile-carrying ships had received turnaround orders on **23 October**. By the time of Rusk’s remark on the 24th, the *Kimovsk* and *Yuri Gagarin* were already heading home, more than **500 nautical miles from the quarantine line**. Washington learned of the withdrawal that morning; the withdrawal itself was not then unfolding a few hours from interception. Distinguish the advisers’ information from the ships’ actual movements. [National Security Archive account based on shipping records, intelligence reports and Navy charts](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/29382-oct-23-eyeball-eyeball-moment-never-happened).

5. **Minor — the new transition reverses the order of the treaty signing and operational hotline.**
   
   > “A second step followed. That summer the two countries and Britain signed a treaty banning nuclear tests in the air, in space and under the sea.”
   
   The treaty was signed on **5 August 1963**; the hotline became operational on **30 August**. The hotline **agreement**, signed in June, preceded the treaty, but the chapter refers to the line’s installation. [National Archives treaty record](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/test-ban-treaty); [State Department hotline history](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v14/d217).

6. **Minor — “second accident” contradicts the preceding account of an intentional order.**
   
   > “At about the same hour, thousands of kilometres away, a second accident unfolded.”
   
   The first incident was a deliberate Soviet missile attack on the U-2, even though it was not authorised by Moscow. The second was an accidental navigational intrusion. Calling both accidents confuses unintended escalation with an unintended action. “Second incident” would preserve the distinction. [State Department crisis history](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1961-1968/cuban-missile-crisis/1000).

## Clarity flags

1. **An absolute conflicts with the scene’s own explanation.**
   
   > “As a result, the men inside had no way of telling a signal from an attack.”
   
   Shortly afterwards, Arkhipov argues that the Americans are signalling. Missing the official explanation does not mean having *no way* to infer their intentions.

2. **Repeated sentence openings and duplicated information.**
   
   > “His orders left him a dangerous amount of room. His orders allowed him…”
   
   The consecutive “His orders” is conspicuous. Similar duplication occurs in:
   
   > “On the other side, the fear was just as real. Khrushchev had been as frightened as anyone.”
   
   > “Their first instinct was to strike. Nearly all of them began by favouring an air strike…”

3. **Clear signposts prohibited by the style sheet.**
   
   > “What made the rocket different from an aircraft was simple.”
   >
   > “The name mattered, too.”
   >
   > “That silence had consequences.”
   >
   > “Its effect could be measured in an unexpected place.”
   
   Each announces the explanation or surprise delivered immediately afterwards.

4. **Padding that adds little orientation.**
   
   > “Over all of it sat a dictator.”
   >
   > “That put the president on the spot.”
   >
   > “The second part came with strict conditions.”
   
   The adjoining sentences already identify the dictator, explain Kennedy’s predicament, and state the conditions. These are weaker additions than the useful physical setup inside the submarine.

5. **An unclear historical connection.**
   
   > “The reason it happened in Cuba, however, goes back a decade, to Guatemala.”
   
   The episode explains Guevara’s outlook and the later comparison between the two invasions. It does not yet explain why Soviet missiles were placed in Cuba. The singular “The reason” promises more than this detour supplies.

6. **An ambiguous causal transition after the submarine encounter.**
   
   > “By then the president had seen how easily events could run ahead of him, and he moved to close the deal.”
   
   Placed immediately after “In the afternoon came the encounter with the submarine,” this can imply that Kennedy knew about the nuclear confrontation. The chapter elsewhere says the Americans did not know what B-59 carried. Tie the transition clearly to events Kennedy actually knew about.

7. **Absolutes or sweeping collective descriptions.**
   
   > “Meanwhile Batista's forces, which nobody wished to die for, fell apart.”
   >
   > “His people shared his anger.”
   
   “Nobody” is an exception-breaking absolute; the second expands the following evidence about crowds into a national consensus. Neither problem requires softening the book’s political interpretation.

8. **Unexplained terminology and an omitted unit.**
   
   > “The chief of staff of the flotilla…”
   >
   > “In 1963 a direct teleprinter line was installed…”
   >
   > “The temperature inside the hull was above forty-five degrees…”
   
   A general reader may need “flotilla” and “teleprinter” explained briefly. Specify Celsius.

No hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or explicit chapter cross-references stood out.

## Overall

The revision preserves the original figures and quotations but is not entirely fact-neutral: several bridges add chronology, causation or broader claims. Correct the historical sequences and the unresolved submarine staging, then trim the clearest signposts and repetitions. The chapter’s confident interpretation does not itself need correction.
