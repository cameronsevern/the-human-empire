## Factual flags

1. **Major — newly introduced: untested deaths could be counted.**

   > “A death that was never tested was never counted as Covid.”

   This is false. Death certificates could record probable or presumed Covid without laboratory confirmation, and those deaths entered mortality statistics. The correct point is that limited testing and incomplete registration caused substantial undercounting, not that every untested death was excluded. [CDC death-data guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/covid-19.htm).

2. **Major — newly introduced: the chronology of suppression and disease control is wrong.**

   > “The authorities moved against the warning before they moved against the disease.”

   The following sentence places the action against Li on 3 January. Wuhan had already issued instructions to investigate and treat the pneumonia cases on 30 December, and the market closed on 1 January. Suppression and an inadequate public-health response occurred alongside one another; suppression did not precede all action against the disease. [Investigation report, translated](https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/li-wenliang-report/); [WHO’s contemporary outbreak report](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2020-DON233).

3. **Major — newly introduced: related viruses are mistaken for a demonstrated source.**

   > “Somehow it travelled from those caves to the city.”

   “Those caves” refers to sites containing the virus’s nearest known relatives. Finding relatives there does not establish that SARS-CoV-2 itself travelled from those particular caves. The distinction matters to the chapter’s account of origins: neither the immediate ancestral virus nor its precise route into humans has been established. This is a specific geographical inference, not merely a confidently expressed interpretation. [WHO’s independent origins assessment](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/independent-assessment-of-the-origins-of-sars-cov-2-from-the-scientific-advisory-group-for-the-origins-of-novel-pathogens).

4. **Minor — newly introduced: public contact does not describe all the occupations listed.**

   > “They were also the ones who met the public every day, and they caught the virus and died at far higher rates than those at home.”

   The preceding list includes meat-packing workers. Their occupational exposure chiefly involved close contact with coworkers, shared workspaces, transport and accommodation—not daily contact with the public. The shared characteristic is working in person around other people. [CDC investigation of meat-processing outbreaks](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/wr/mm6927e2.htm).

5. **Minor — retained caption: Margaret Keenan was not the first recipient of any Covid vaccine outside a trial.**

   > “The syringe and vial used for the first Covid vaccination outside a trial, given to Margaret Keenan, aged ninety, at a hospital in Coventry, England, on 8 December 2020.”

   China began emergency-use vaccinations in July 2020. Keenan’s distinction concerned the Pfizer–BioNTech vaccine following its approval. The body’s “receive it” refers to that particular vaccine and is defensible; the caption broadens the claim incorrectly. [Chinese government’s vaccination briefing](https://english.scio.gov.cn/pressroom/2020-12/24/content_77048143_6.htm); [NHS announcement](https://www.england.nhs.uk/2020/12/landmark-moment-as-first-nhs-patient-receives-covid-19-vaccination/).

6. **Minor — retained: the mast-attack figure mixes dates and categories.**

   > “In April 2020, in Britain, people who believed that the virus was caused or spread by a new kind of mobile telephone signal set fire to more than eighty transmitter masts.”

   Contemporary BT evidence described **approaching eighty attacks since 23 March**, including arson, attempted arson and other sabotage. The reported total of **87 arson incidents** was reached by 26 May. Neither supports more than eighty masts set alight in April alone. [BT’s parliamentary evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/2896/html/); [26 May report quoting Mobile UK](https://www.belfasttelegraph.co.uk/news/uk/around-90-mast-attacks-recorded-during-coronavirus-lockdown/a/113963599.html).

7. **Minor — retained: passenger traffic is confused with cancelled flights.**

   > “Nine out of ten passenger flights were cancelled.”

   The global April figures commonly used here measure passenger traffic and capacity: passenger-kilometres fell 94.3%, while available seat-kilometres fell 87%. Neither means 90% of flights were cancelled. Flightradar24 recorded a 73.7% fall in commercial flights, a different measure again. Use a clearly identified traffic or capacity statistic. [IATA’s April analysis](https://www.iata.org/en/iata-repositorY/publications/economic-reports/air-passenger-monthly-analysis---apr-20202/); [Flightradar24’s flight counts](https://www.flightradar24.com/blog/aviation-news/aviation-business/scraping-along-the-bottom-april-air-traffic-statistics/).

8. **Minor — retained: the institutional history is too compressed to remain accurate.**

   > “The financial crashes left central banks and insurance for deposits, and the Depression left the welfare state.”

   Financial crises helped create some central banks and deposit-insurance systems, but central banking has other origins: the Bank of England was founded to finance war. Welfare states also predated the Depression; Germany introduced national sickness insurance in 1883 and old-age insurance in 1889. The Depression expanded welfare provision rather than originating it. [Bank of England history](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/about/history); [Social Security Administration’s historical account](https://www.ssa.gov/history/ottob.html).

## Clarity flags

- **Explicit signposts:**  
  > “The methods themselves fit in a few lines.”  
  > “In 2005 she and a colleague found the answer.”  
  > “There is a better measure, and it does not depend on testing.”

  These announce an explanation instead of giving it. The first also comments on the presentation of the text. All run against the stylesheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences.

- **A transition creates a misleading connection:**  
  > “An American president suggested at a press conference that doctors might look into injecting disinfectant. Millions acted on what they heard. They took a drug meant for malaria and another meant for parasitic worms…”

  The added bridge makes the drug-taking sound like a response to the disinfectant suggestion. These are separate examples and need a clear separation.

- **An unclear comparison:**  
  > “India was meant to be the exception.”

  Exception to what—rich countries securing supplies, poor countries lacking vaccines, or unequal distribution? India’s intended role as a supplier to poorer countries is clearer than the unexplained “exception.”

- **An unclear time anchor:**  
  > “Within weeks, the doubling reached Europe.”

  Weeks after the first Wuhan cases, publication of the genome, or the lockdown? “The doubling” is also an awkward substitute for rapidly rising infections.

- **Repetition rather than useful breathing room:**  
  > “The work brought her little reward.”  
  > “The speed was unheard of.”  
  > “The gamble paid off.”

  Each is immediately followed by evidence making the same point. These are stronger candidates for trimming than the useful explanations of RNA or trial design.

- **An existing signpost remains:**  
  > “Two things about it made it far harder to stop than its cousin of 2003.”

  Together with “The first was timing” and “The second was speed,” this gives the passage a numbered-explanation structure. The stylesheet explicitly prefers stating the point rather than announcing forthcoming points.

- **Absolutes that exceed the intended meaning:**  
  > “From that moment, any laboratory in the world could start work on the virus.”  
  > “It was two days before the lunar new year, when the whole of China travels…”

  Public access to a sequence does not give every laboratory the necessary capability. Nor does everyone in China travel. Both breach the explicit rule against absolutes broken by ordinary exceptions.

- **A pronoun blurs virus and disease:**  
  > “The disease that it causes was given the name Covid-19. It lodges in the nose, throat and lungs.”

  The nearest antecedent of “It” is the disease, but the intended subject is the virus.
