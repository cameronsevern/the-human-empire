I found the following issues, most serious first. I found no clear direct contradictions with chapters 67, 101 or 110, and no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-references or forbidden cross-references. No files were edited.

1. **Factual: the trust study’s findings are substantially overstated.**  
   > “It was not wealth, the number of hospital beds, or whether the country was a democracy.”  
   > “The things that counted, apart from the age of the population, were how much people trusted their government, and how much they trusted one another.”

   The study distinguished **infection rates** from **fatality among infected people**. Trust correlated with fewer infections; it was not established as the cause. Wealth did explain some variation in both outcomes, and much remained unexplained. The defensible point is that preparedness rankings performed poorly, while trust was associated with lower infection rates. **Confidence: high.** [Original Lancet study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35120592/).

2. **Factual/internal chronology: the opening puts twenty million deaths before vaccine deployment.**  
   > “Between those two facts, some twenty million people died.”

   The preceding sentence ends with vaccines manufactured within a year, meaning late 2020. Twenty million is a later, multiyear pandemic estimate. Even WHO’s estimate through **December 2021** was approximately fifteen million excess deaths. **Confidence: high.** [WHO mortality estimate](https://www.who.int/news/item/05-05-2022-14.9-million-excess-deaths-were-associated-with-the-covid-19-pandemic-in-2020-and-2021?stream=world).

3. **Factual/style: “every continent in weeks” has a clear exception.**  
   > “A virus that appeared in one Chinese city reached every continent in weeks, carried by air travellers.”

   Antarctica’s first confirmed mainland outbreak was in December 2020. “Every inhabited continent” or “six continents” preserves the point. The universal air-travel explanation also needs narrowing. **Confidence: high.** [Research on the pandemic in Antarctica](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adk4424).

4. **Factual: fiscal support is misreported as expenditure.**  
   > “Across the world, states spent about sixteen trillion dollars in two years, and central banks created the money for much of it.”

   The IMF’s $16 trillion figure covered **announced measures through March 2021**: roughly $10 trillion in additional spending and forgone revenue, plus $6 trillion in loans, guarantees and capital injections. It was not $16 trillion actually spent over two years. Central-bank asset purchases also require a separate explanation; that total does not establish how much spending was financed by money creation. **Confidence: high.** [IMF Fiscal Monitor, April 2021](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/fiscal-monitor/2021/april/english/ch1.pdf).

5. **Factual: adoption of the pandemic agreement is confused with signature.**  
   > “In 2025 most of the world's states signed an agreement to share information and vaccines more fairly next time.”

   The World Health Assembly **adopted** the agreement in May 2025. Opening it for signature was conditional on adoption of its pathogen-access and benefit-sharing annex. **Confidence: high.** [WHO’s adoption announcement](https://www.who.int/news/item/20-05-2025-world-health-assembly-adopts-historic-pandemic-agreement-to-make-the-world-more-equitable-and-safer-from-future-pandemics).

6. **Factual/style: the intelligence agencies did not have “no confidence.”**  
   > “The intelligence agencies of the United States examined the question and split, some leaning one way and some the other, none with any confidence.”

   Assessments included **low confidence** and **moderate confidence**; the latter applied to a laboratory-associated origin assessment. Neither means no confidence. “None with high confidence” would convey the uncertainty accurately for the period discussed. **Confidence: high.** [Declassified intelligence assessment](https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Declassified-Assessment-on-COVID-19-Origins.pdf).

7. **Factual: the border-testing account combines different policies and dates.**  
   > “Taiwan and New Zealand closed their borders early and tested everyone who arrived.”

   Taiwan initially used risk- and symptom-based testing alongside quarantine, not universal testing of arrivals. New Zealand introduced routine testing during managed isolation in June 2020, after its initial border closure and domestic lockdown. **Confidence: high.** [Taiwan’s April 2020 rules](https://www.cdc.gov.tw/En/Bulletin/Detail/6emsMaYdnl2HLYCy_8t9IQ?typeid=158); [New Zealand’s June announcement](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-covid-19-testing-strategy-keep-new-zealand-safe).

8. **Factual: the conventional-vaccine explanation mistakes manufacturing time for development time.**  
   > “The usual way to make one is to grow the virus itself in eggs or vats of cells and then kill or weaken it, which takes years.”

   Growing and processing virus does not inherently take years: conventional influenza vaccines can reach initial approved supplies in about five to six months. Developing and testing a new vaccine can take years. Also, killed or weakened whole-virus vaccines are only some established vaccine types. **Confidence: high.** [WHO’s manufacturing timeline](https://www.who.int/news/item/06-08-2009-pandemic-influenza-vaccine-manufacturing-process-and-timeline).

9. **Factual/style: the vaccine-speed record needs a defined comparison.**  
   > “The fastest that any vaccine had been developed before was four years.”

   Four years is the familiar **mumps-vaccine** benchmark, but “any vaccine” includes counterexamples: vaccines against the newly identified 2009 H1N1 strain were administered that October. Distinguish developing a vaccine against a new disease from adapting an established vaccine platform to a new strain. The opening’s “which had never been done before” needs the same precision. **Confidence: high on the exception; medium on the appropriate record wording.** [CDC’s H1N1 chronology](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/h1n1flu/cdcresponse.htm).

10. **Factual: ivermectin is misleadingly presented as an animal-only medicine.**  
    > “Millions took a drug meant for malaria and another meant for worming horses.”

    Ivermectin has long-established human antiparasitic uses as well as veterinary formulations. Some people took animal products, but describing the drug generally as horse wormer misrepresents what others took. The accurate criticism is its unsupported use against COVID-19. **Confidence: high.** [FDA explanation](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/ivermectin-and-covid-19).

11. **Factual: thirty years of neglect erases substantial research and investment.**  
    > “The vaccine that the two companies designed in a weekend rested on an idea that had been ignored for thirty years.”

    Karikó faced serious rejection, but mRNA technology was not ignored throughout those decades. Companies dedicated to it existed well before 2020, and vaccine research and trials preceded the pandemic. “Struggled for acceptance” would fit the history better than “ignored.” **Confidence: high.** [Nobel Assembly’s scientific background](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2023/10/advanced-medicinprize2023-3.pdf).

12. **Factual: the claim that governments funded “most” research lacks a defined denominator.**  
    > “Governments had paid for most of the research and had ordered hundreds of millions of doses before anyone knew whether they would work.”

    Public funding was crucial, but “most” requires specifying which vaccines, which research stages and which costs. Studies establishing large public investment do not automatically establish a majority of all research expenditure; purchasing doses is also distinct from funding research. **Confidence: high that the wording needs substantiation; medium on any replacement percentage.** [Study of US public investment](https://www.bmj.com/content/380/bmj-2022-073747).

13. **Factual/reader clarity: excess mortality needs attribution and an explanation of indirect deaths.**  
    > “On that reckoning the pandemic had killed about fifteen million people by the end of 2021, and well over twenty million by the time the World Health Organization declared the emergency over, in May 2023.”

    The first estimate is recognisably WHO’s; the second needs its own named model and date. Excess mortality includes deaths indirectly associated with the pandemic, such as disrupted treatment, and is not simply a count of undiagnosed COVID deaths. That distinction matters because the paragraph introduces it chiefly as a correction for inadequate testing. **Confidence: high on the distinction; the May 2023 total needs a specified source.** [WHO’s definition and estimate](https://www.who.int/news/item/05-05-2022-14.9-million-excess-deaths-were-associated-with-the-covid-19-pandemic-in-2020-and-2021?stream=world).

14. **Factual: Zoom participants are counted as unique users.**  
    > “Meetings moved to video calls, and a company that provided them went from ten million daily users to three hundred million in four months.”

    These were **daily meeting participants**. Someone joining several meetings could be counted several times. Zoom publicly corrected precisely this confusion. **Confidence: high.** [Zoom’s SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1585521/000119312521006730/d919929d424b5.htm); [report of the correction](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/zoom-admits-does-not-300-092721036.html).

15. **Factual: the school-closure denominator is wrong.**  
    > “At the peak, schools were closed to one and a half billion pupils, nine tenths of all the children in the world.”

    The figure referred to roughly nine tenths of **enrolled learners**, including university students—not all children worldwide. **Confidence: high.** [UNESCO’s account](https://courier.unesco.org/en/articles/education-unprecedented-crisis).

16. **Factual: the remote-work statistic describes the wider workforce.**  
    > “A quarter of the working days of American office staff are now spent at home, five times as many as before.”

    The widely cited estimate concerns the share of **all paid workdays** worked from home, not specifically office staff. Those populations are not interchangeable. Anchor “now” to a year as well. **Confidence: high.** [Researchers’ 2023 presentation](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e2ea3a8097ed30c779bd707/t/641b9ed4b67db14c6df78b23/1679531743927/Big%2BShift%2Bto%2BRemote%2BWork%2C%2BIMF%2C%2B29%2BMarch%2B2023%2C%2BPart%2B1.pdf).

17. **Factual/reader clarity: “New York” shifts silently from city to state.**  
    > “In New York eight hundred people were dying daily, and refrigerated lorries were parked outside the hospitals to hold the bodies.”

    The familiar approximately 800 daily-deaths figure was **statewide**; the preceding sentence and the lorries suggest New York City. Specify the state and that this was around the first wave’s peak. **Confidence: high.** [Contemporaneous state death series](https://covidtracking.com/data/state/new-york/outcomes/).

18. **Factual/style: South Korea’s “never” is too broad.**  
    > “South Korea, which had been badly frightened by an outbreak of a related virus five years before, set up test stations that people could drive through, traced contacts using the records of mobile telephones and bank cards, and never shut its shops.”

    South Korea avoided a general national lockdown, but closed categories of businesses, including bars, clubs, gyms and internet cafés. Describe the absence of a general shutdown rather than implying uninterrupted commercial opening. **Confidence: high.** [Contemporaneous university notice reproducing the restrictions](https://eng.snu.ac.kr/communication/notice/notice?bbsidx=1109&md=v).

19. **Reader clarity: anonymity makes the vaccine story hard to follow.**  
    > “The first was tested on forty-three thousand volunteers, half of whom were given the vaccine and half salt water, without knowing which.”

    “The first” is ambiguous because the American company appeared first in the narrative, but this is the Pfizer–BioNTech trial. Name Moderna and BioNTech when introduced, then identify which vaccine is being tested. These names meet the style guide’s exception for names needed to follow the story.

20. **Reader clarity: the political comparison conceals its own finding.**  
    > “In the United States the question of whether to be vaccinated, or to wear a mask, became a badge of political loyalty, and after the vaccines arrived, death rates in the districts that voted for one party pulled steadily ahead of those in the districts that voted for the other.”

    A general reader cannot tell which party had higher death rates. Name the parties and identify whether the underlying evidence concerns counties, electoral districts or individually registered voters. The current circumlocution withholds information essential to understanding the point.

21. **Style: an absolute outruns the concrete example already given.**  
    > “Governments have learned that they can pay a whole population's wages in an emergency, and what it costs afterwards.”

    The chapter describes wage support for eligible workers and other forms of household assistance, not payment of an entire population’s wages. “Support incomes on an enormous scale” would match the evidence without weakening the argument.

22. **Style/reader orientation: the disease itself is never named.**  
    > “The new virus belonged to a family named for the crown of spikes on its surface.”

    Neither COVID-19 nor SARS-CoV-2 appears anywhere in the chapter. Naming the disease once would orient readers, and naming the family “coronaviruses” here would connect the crown explanation to an actual term. Earlier chapters supply useful background about germs and vaccination, but cannot supply these missing identities.
