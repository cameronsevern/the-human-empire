## Changed facts

No names, dates, numerical figures or quotations were changed or removed. Most additions make existing claims explicit. These passages add substantive claims or change their scope:

1. **Chinese writing and movable type**
   
   **Old:** “Chinese writing, however, uses thousands of different characters, which makes a set of movable pieces enormous.”
   
   **New:** “Movable pieces were a poor fit for Chinese writing, however. It uses thousands of different characters, which makes a set of movable pieces enormous.”
   
   The revision adds a judgment about technological suitability to the existing statement about the size of the character set.

2. **Alphabetic type**
   
   **Old:** “An alphabet needs only a couple of dozen letters, used over and over.”
   
   **New:** “An alphabet, by contrast, needs only a couple of dozen letters, used over and over. A printer with an alphabet therefore needs a great many copies of a very few shapes.”
   
   Adds an accurate explanation of the need for multiple copies of each character.

3. **The casting mechanism**
   
   **Old:** “He hammered it into a small bar of soft copper, which left a hollow in the shape of the letter.”
   
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by “That hollow was his mould.”
   
   Adds a technical identification that confuses the matrix with the complete mould.

   **Old:** “A workman could cast them one after another all day, all identical.”
   
   **New:** “A workman could cast them one after another all day, and because they all came out of the same hollow, they were all identical.”
   
   Adds a causal explanation. The matrix reproduces the letter face; the complete mould also determines the type’s body.

4. **Luther’s position on indulgences**
   
   **Old:** “If this was true, the men preaching the indulgence were doing real harm, because they taught people to put their faith in a receipt.”
   
   **New:** “The certificates from across the border struck at the centre of that belief. If Luther was right, an indulgence was not merely useless. The men preaching it were doing real harm, because they taught people to put their faith in a receipt.”
   
   Changes criticism of the preaching into rejection of indulgences themselves. This reintroduces an error previously identified in `check.md`.

5. **Frederick’s intention**
   
   **Old:** “He was also proud of his university and tired of seeing German silver carried over the Alps.”
   
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by “He did not intend to give up his most famous professor.”
   
   Adds an explicit attribution of intention. It is consistent with the protection described immediately afterwards.

6. **The peasants’ scriptural test**
   
   **Old:** “The list ended by offering to withdraw any demand that could be shown to be against scripture.”
   
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by “That was Luther’s own test, put to a new use.”
   
   Adds an explicit connection between the peasants’ demands and Luther’s appeal to scriptural authority. This is a defensible interpretation.

Other additions—including the bankers protecting their money, Charles’s limited authority, Henry removing papal jurisdiction, multiple religious camps and neighbouring calendars—restate facts already present rather than introduce new historical information.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — the revision restores the error about Luther’s initial position.**
   
   > “If Luther was right, an indulgence was not merely useless.”
   
   In its position before the ninety-five theses, this presents Luther as already rejecting indulgences categorically. In 1517 he still distinguished legitimate papal indulgences from the false assurances of their sellers. Theses 38 and 71 explicitly retain a legitimate role for them. The old sentence’s criticism of the preachers avoided this problem.
   
   **Correct fact:** Luther initially attacked abuses and misleading claims about indulgences; his rejection developed further afterwards. [Oxford’s edition of the ninety-five theses](https://editions.mml.ox.ac.uk/editions/luther-95-theses/).

2. **Minor — the new explanation misidentifies the mould.**
   
   > “That hollow was his mould.”
   
   The impression in copper formed the **matrix**, which shaped the letter’s face. It fitted into the hand mould, which shaped the body of the metal type. Calling the hollow the mould obscures the function of the “little hand-held box” in the next sentence.
   
   **Correct fact:** The copper impression formed the letter-shaped bottom of the mould, not the whole mould. [ASME’s account of Gutenberg’s casting system](https://www.asme.org/getmedia/42c4bfde-c3f8-4c84-ac32-df7cb070f5e3/278-gutenberg-and-mass-production.pdf).

3. **Minor — the existing alloy description omits a functional ingredient.**
   
   > “He then clamped the copper into the bottom of a little hand-held box and poured in molten metal, a mixture of lead and tin that melts on a kitchen fire and sets in a second.”
   
   The standard account of Gutenberg’s type metal includes antimony, which helped produce durable, sharply cast type. Naming only lead and tin gives an incomplete recipe in a passage specifically explaining how the technology worked.
   
   **Correct fact:** The alloy contained lead, tin and antimony. [University of New England’s Museum of Antiquities](https://www.une.edu.au/info-for/visitors/museums/museum-of-antiquities/exhibitions-and-programs/codebreaker-challenge/printing).

4. **Minor — one cardinal is made to cover two administrations.**
   
   > “Instead, for the second half of the war, this cardinal paid the Protestant armies and then sent French troops to fight beside them, because the emperor was a rival of France and a weak, divided Germany suited him.”
   
   “This cardinal” means Richelieu, but he died in December 1642, six years before the war ended. Mazarin continued French policy. The explanation of France’s strategic interests remains sound; the singular attribution across the second half of the war does not.
   
   **Correct fact:** Richelieu initiated the policy, and Mazarin continued it after his death. [Bibliothèque nationale de France’s catalogue of the royal letter announcing Richelieu’s death and Mazarin’s entry into the council](https://ccfr.bnf.fr/portailccfr/jsp/index_view_direct_anonymous.jsp?record=eadcgm%3AEADC%3ACHD016263).

## Clarity flags

- **Explicit signposting:** “The practical conclusion was not hard to draw.” This announces the conclusion immediately before giving it, contrary to the style sheet.

- **Unclear antecedent and scope:** “Luther’s teaching took away the reasons for it.” “It” could mean the church, its wealth, its independent jurisdiction or its allegiance to Rome. The following sentences concern monasteries and the papacy specifically.

- **Repeated explanation:** “Charles held many crowns, but in Germany his power was thinner than his title.” The next three sentences explain his titles and limited authority again. The later “It was those rulers who gathered to hear the case” also repeats information supplied by “Standing in front of the assembled princes.” This sequence has more setup than the reader needs.

- **Redundant callback:** “That is what the gaps in the printed sheet were for.” The opening already explains that a clerk filled the gaps with a name and date, and the immediately preceding sentence again says the clerk wrote the name.

- **A count that does not resolve:** “The pope was prepared to overlook all three difficulties for a very large fee.” The preceding sentence supplies two distinct obstacles: Albrecht’s age and holding multiple offices. Already being an archbishop and being forbidden to hold two archbishoprics describe the same obstacle.

- **An unsupported inference in the caption:** “Its size suggests how much money the sales brought in.” A chest’s capacity does not establish how much money passed through it, especially when its attribution to Tetzel is itself uncertain.

- **An unnecessary absolute:** “The pope denounced the treaty, and nobody took any notice.” The intended point is that his denunciation did not prevent implementation. “Nobody” makes a much broader claim about every response to it.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or body cross-references. The useful transitions generally comply with the style sheet’s instruction to give readers room.
