## Changed facts

No names, dates, numbers or quotations were changed or removed. However, several additions introduce or strengthen factual claims. For inserted sentences, there is no old counterpart; where relevant, I quote the old sentence they expand.

1. **Everything came from elsewhere.**  
   Old: “Almost nothing in the building came from Agra.”  
   New: “Almost nothing in the building came from Agra. Its materials, its craftsmen and its ideas were all brought in from far away.”  
   This strengthens “almost nothing” into “all” and extends the claim explicitly to workers and ideas. It introduces an error; see below.

2. **Relative transport distances.**  
   Old: “Jade came from China, turquoise from Tibet, and blue lapis lazuli from Afghanistan.”  
   New preceding sentence: “The coloured stones travelled further still.”  
   This explicitly compares their journeys with the marble’s journey. It follows from the three examples, though “the coloured stones” could encompass every stone used.

3. **The carts’ military purpose.**  
   Old: “On the gunners' advice he tied seven hundred carts together with ropes of rawhide to make a wall, with gaps for his cavalry, and set the guns and musketeers behind it.”  
   New insertion: “The carts were there to stop a charge for long enough to let the guns do their work. That is what happened.”  
   This adds an explicit explanation of the defensive arrangement.

4. **Gun costs caused political centralisation.**  
   Old: “Guns had a political effect as well as a military one.”  
   New: “Guns had a political effect as well as a military one, because they were expensive.”  
   Additional new sentence: “Power therefore drained away from the chiefs and towards the ruler at the centre.”  
   The revision makes the causal interpretation explicit. That is a substantive assertion, although it develops the existing argument.

5. **Recruits had exclusively royal loyalties.**  
   Old: “A man taken from a Christian village at twelve arrived at court with no relatives and no party of his own.”  
   New: “By contrast, a man taken from a Christian village at twelve arrived at court with no relatives and no party of his own. He had nobody to be loyal to except the sultan.”  
   The addition moves from circumstances on arrival to an absolute claim about loyalty. It reintroduces the problem identified in the earlier check.

6. **Ismail’s inherited position explains his command.**  
   Old: “He was the hereditary leader of a religious brotherhood, and his followers, who wore red caps, believed him to be something close to divine and went into battle without armour.”  
   New: “He was the hereditary leader of a religious brotherhood, which is how a boy came to command an army. His followers, who wore red caps, believed him to be something close to divine and went into battle without armour.”  
   No underlying detail changes; the causal explanation is new.

7. **Akbar’s partnership began with marriage.**  
   Old: “Akbar decided to make them partners.”  
   New: “Akbar decided to make them partners. He began with a wedding.”  
   This adds an explicit claim about the starting point, rather than simply proceeding to the marriage example.

8. **Tax assessment gave farmers advance knowledge.**  
   Old: “From these they worked out an average, and the state claimed a third of it.”  
   New following sentence: “A farmer therefore knew in advance roughly what he would owe.”  
   The claim about the farmer’s knowledge is new, although the old passage already described the system as predictable.

9. **The coin supply was foreign.**  
   Old: “A large part of the silver dug from the mountain at Potosí thus came to rest in India, was minted into rupees, and paid the land tax.”  
   New introductory sentence: “The silver for those coins came from abroad.”  
   The old sentence remains. The addition broadens the stated provenance from a substantial Potosí contribution to the silver supply generally.

Other additions principally restate existing information: forced conversion in Iran, Armenian privileges, religious taxation, earlier religious mixing, the longstanding Sikh–Mughal conflict, and the duration of Aurangzeb’s southern war. They introduce no new named event or numerical detail.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — the Taj’s materials did not all come from far away.**

   > “Almost nothing in the building came from Agra. Its materials, its craftsmen and its ideas were all brought in from far away.”

   The marble exterior obscures a substantial brick structure. Archnet specifically records that the bricks were baked on the outskirts of Agra. The new absolute is therefore false, and the preceding “almost nothing” is misleading about the building’s fabric. The correct distinction is between locally produced construction materials and marble, decorative stones and specialist contributions drawn from elsewhere. Source: [Archnet, “Taj Mahal Complex”](https://next.archnet.org/sites/1559).

2. **Major — the revision reintroduces the exclusive-loyalty error.**

   > “He had nobody to be loyal to except the sultan.”

   Recruitment did not erase family loyalties or prevent recruits from developing other allegiances. The chapter immediately supplies a counterexample: Mehmed remembered his origins and helped a relative become head of the Serbian church. Historical evidence also documents his extensive family patronage.

   The correct fact is that recruitment was intended to produce servants dependent on the sultan; it did not leave them incapable of other loyalties. This is a newly reintroduced problem, rather than a repetition of a successfully fixed flag. Source: [*Encyclopaedia of Islam*, “Sokollu Mehmed Pasha”](https://isamveri.org/pdfdkm/18/DKM181197.pdf).

3. **Major — the three-empires geography is dated too early.**

   > “By the middle of the 1500s, three empires covered the whole stretch of country from the Danube to the Bay of Bengal.”

   Bengal was still outside Mughal rule in the middle of the century. Akbar conquered its Afghan kingdom in **1576**, with consolidation continuing afterward. The statement anticipates the eastern extent of Mughal territory by decades. The accompanying map is explicitly dated to the middle of the **1600s**, so it cannot illustrate the stated mid-sixteenth-century situation.

   The correct chronology places this broad territorial configuration later than the middle of the 1500s. Source: [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Akbar I”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/akbar-i-mughal-india/).

4. **Minor — the three Jesuits did not all stay three years.**

   > “Later he invited Hindu priests, Jain monks, Zoroastrians from Iran and, in 1580, three Jesuit priests from the Portuguese colony at Goa, who arrived hoping to convert him and left after three years of courteous disappointment.”

   This assigns the same departure date to all three missionaries. Their departures were staggered: Henriquez left earlier, probably in 1581; Monserrate returned in 1582; Acquaviva remained until 1583. The correct summary is that the **first mission lasted from 1580 to 1583**, not that each participant stayed three years. Source: [H. Hosten, *Jesuit Missionaries in Northern India*](https://www.fondazioneintorcetta.info/pdf/biblioteca-virtuale/documenti/NorthernIndia.pdf).

## Clarity flags

1. **Padding in the battle explanation.**

   > “That is what happened.”

   The next sentence immediately describes what happened. The explanation of the carts is useful; this extra confirmation adds neither information nor orientation.

2. **An empty transition before an actual geographical statement.**

   > “The result was a new map.”

   The following sentence describes the territorial result, and an actual map follows. This sentence merely announces that information.

3. **Unclear viewpoint.**

   > “The change also made Iran an enemy next door.”

   An enemy next door to whom? The Ottoman viewpoint appears only in the next sentence. Naming the Ottomans here would make the transition immediately intelligible.

4. **Repeated wording.**

   > “One of them was a boy from a village in the hills of Bosnia, the son of Serbian Orthodox farmers, who was taken as a boy.”

   “A boy … as a boy” repeats the same information. The preceding paragraph has already explained childhood recruitment.

5. **A loose transition after the paragraph split.**

   > “The silver for those coins came from abroad. India's weavers, meanwhile, made the best cotton cloth in the world, and merchants from every country came to buy it.”

   The cloth exports explain how silver arrived, but “meanwhile” presents them as a separate concurrent development. The new paragraph opening makes that mismatch more noticeable.

6. **An unnecessary exception-breaking absolute.**

   > “India's weavers, meanwhile, made the best cotton cloth in the world, and merchants from every country came to buy it.”

   “Every country” makes a literal worldwide claim the argument does not need. “Merchants from many countries” preserves the point about international demand.

The “all” and “nobody” claims in factual flags 1–2 also violate the style sheet’s rule against exception-breaking absolutes. I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-reference or explicit body cross-references. Most added setup sentences fit the sheet’s instruction to give readers room; they should not be removed merely for being transitions.

## Overall

The revision largely preserves the factual content, but it is not entirely fact-neutral: two additions introduce or restore clear errors. Two older chronology problems also remain. Most transitions help; a few add repetition or vague framing. No files were edited.
