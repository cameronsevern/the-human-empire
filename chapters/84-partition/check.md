1. **The proposed union is presented as an agreement it never quite was.** [Lines 43–45](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:43): “The provinces would run everything else, and would be free to form groups, so that the Muslim-majority provinces of the north-west and the east could govern themselves together.” “Both parties accepted.”

   The central dispute was precisely whether grouping was optional. The British required provinces to enter the initial sections, with withdrawal from subsequently formed groups possible after the first elections under the new constitution. Congress asserted an earlier right to opt out; the League relied on compulsory initial grouping. Explain these incompatible interpretations before Nehru’s press conference, or the reader gets a misleading account of why the settlement failed. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [Cripps’s explanation to Parliament](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1946/jul/18/india-cabinet-mission), [Congress’s resolution of 24 May](https://nehruarchive.in/documents/congress-resolution-on-the-cabinet-mission-statement-24-may-1946-lp7o7).

2. **The opening’s dates are wrong.** [Line 13](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:13): “India and Pakistan became independent two days after that.” “The lines were not published until two days later still.”

   “That” refers to 12 August, making independence 14 August and publication 16 August. The legal independence date was **15 August**, and the awards were published **17 August**; Pakistan’s independence celebrations are observed on 14 August. This also clashes with chapter 76’s placement of India’s independence at midnight ending 14 August. Use explicit dates here. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [Indian Independence Act, section 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo6/10-11/30/1998-11-19), [the published boundary awards](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Radcliffe_Award).

3. **Urdu is incorrectly made the language of Jinnah’s followers generally.** [Line 31](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:31): “He drank whisky, rarely set foot in a mosque, and spoke English far better than Urdu, the language of the people he came to lead.”

   Urdu was important to Muslim political culture, but most Indian Muslims—and most Pakistanis—had other mother tongues. Pakistan’s 1951 census recorded about 2.5 million Urdu mother-tongue speakers against about 41 million Bengali speakers. Calling Urdu “the language” also makes the later Bengali dispute harder to understand. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Pakistan’s 1951 census, language tables](https://censusindia.gov.in/nada/index.php/catalog/31888/download/35069/1368_1951_REP.pdf).

4. **Congress’s eventual ministries are confused with outright election victories.** [Line 25](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:25): “In 1937, when the provinces held elections for their own governments, Congress won eight of the eleven.” “It had the votes to rule alone, and it did, refusing to share office with the Muslim League, the main party of the Muslims.”

   Congress won outright majorities in **five** provinces and initially formed governments in **seven**; an eighth ministry followed in Assam in 1938. It did not possess an outright majority across all eight. The failed coalition negotiations with the League should be located particularly in the United Provinces, rather than presented as one uniform arrangement everywhere. **Confidence: high on the electoral correction.** Sources: [Congress’s own chronology](https://inc.in/brief-history-of-congress/1935-1945), [1937 election results and ministry formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1937_Indian_provincial_elections).

5. **The migration total has been squeezed into an unsupported timespan.** [Line 73](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:73): “Within a few months about fifteen million people had crossed the borders, which is the largest movement of people in so short a time that has been recorded.”

   The widely used estimate of roughly 14.5 million migrants comes from the **1951 censuses**, not a count confined to the first few months. Punjab’s displacement was exceptionally rapid, but substantial migration across Bengal continued afterwards. “One of the largest forced migrations” is supportable; this precise speed record needs evidence and breaches the style rule against fragile absolutes. **Confidence: high that the timespan needs correction; moderate on any worldwide ranking.** Sources: [Bharadwaj, Khwaja and Mian, *The Big March*](https://projects.iq.harvard.edu/files/epod/files/khwaja_a_-_the_big_march_epw_publish08.pdf), [Partition Museum’s Bengal account](https://www.partitionmuseum.org/partition-of-india).

6. **The transition into the December war omits a crucial event.** [Line 103](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:103): “In December India invaded.”

   India did invade East Pakistan, but this sentence makes that sound like the uncomplicated beginning of the interstate war. Indian involvement and border fighting preceded December; Pakistan’s air attacks on Indian airfields on **3 December** marked the opening of full-scale war. A brief chronological bridge is needed. **Confidence: high.** Source: [contemporary diplomatic records and editorial chronology](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v11/d215).

7. **The surrender figure incorrectly counts everyone as a soldier.** [Line 103](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:103): “The war lasted thirteen days, and ended with ninety thousand Pakistani soldiers laying down their arms.”

   Roughly 90,000–93,000 Pakistanis entered Indian custody, but the total included civilian internees as well as military and other uniformed personnel. It is not a count of soldiers laying down weapons. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [ICRC repatriation figures distinguishing prisoners of war and civilians](https://international-review.icrc.org/sites/default/files/S0020860400017472a.pdf), [account of the camps and their civilian inhabitants](https://indianexpress.com/article/cities/chandigarh/military-digest-pakistani-pow-1971-surrender-9749817/).

8. **Gandhi’s fast and Mountbatten’s tribute are put in the wrong chronological relationship.** [Line 79](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:79): “When the violence began again, he announced that he would eat nothing until it stopped.” “The viceroy wrote that in Punjab he had fifty-five thousand soldiers and large-scale rioting, and in Bengal he had one man and peace.”

   The paragraph begins in August and never marks the move to the **1–4 September fast**. Mountbatten’s tribute was written on **26 August**, before renewed violence and the fast; it was not a verdict on the fast’s success. He was also governor-general by then, rather than viceroy. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [Gandhi Smriti chronology](https://www.gandhismriti.gov.in/last-144-days), [Gandhi’s correspondence identifying the letter’s date](https://www.gandhipedia150.in/static/data/highlighted_pdfs_output/Delhi_volume89_book_142.pdf).

9. **The Punjab commission’s religious composition is wrong.** [Line 11](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:11): “He was given a bungalow, four Indian judges for each province, two Hindu and two Muslim, who disagreed on every point, and a set of maps and census tables that were years out of date.”

   Punjab’s four judges included **one Hindu, one Sikh and two Muslims**. Teja Singh was Sikh. “Disagreed on every point” is also unnecessarily absolute: failure to agree on a boundary does not establish disagreement on every issue. **Confidence: high on membership.** Sources: [Punjab commission membership](https://eos.learnpunjabi.org/PUNJAB%20BOUNDARY%20COMMISSION.html), [the commission’s own report](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AReport_of_the_Bengal_Boundary_Commission_%28Radcliffe_Award%29.pdf/8).

10. **The first-election turnout needs reconciliation.** [Line 109](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:109): “Six voters in ten turned out.”

    The Election Commission’s current historical series gives **45.67%**, approximately five in eleven. Some older ECI publications give **61.16%**, so this is a genuine source discrepancy rather than a number to correct silently without checking its basis. Use the current series or establish why the older calculation is appropriate. **Confidence: high that the sources conflict; moderate on declaring the draft’s number simply false.** Sources: [current ECI table](https://www.eci.gov.in/about-eci/), [government publication giving 45.7%](https://www.publicationsdivision.nic.in/journals/Journalarchives/Yojana/Yojana-English/2014/July/Yojana_2014_July_pdf.pdf).

11. **The largest princely states were much smaller than France.** [Line 89](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:89): “It belonged to more than five hundred princes, whose states ranged from a few villages to kingdoms the size of France.”

    Hyderabad and Jammu and Kashmir were each roughly **200,000–220,000 square kilometres**, around two-fifths the area of metropolitan France. “About the size of Great Britain” would be a much closer comparison. **Confidence: high.** Source: [princely-state sizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princely_state).

12. **“The whole Congress leadership” creates a false absolute and loses chapter 76’s qualification.** [Line 41](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:41): “In 1942 Gandhi launched a campaign to drive the British out at once, and the whole Congress leadership spent the next three years in prison.”

    Gandhi was released on **6 May 1944**. Chapter 76 more accurately says the British “held most of them for the rest of the war.” Retain that distinction. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [contemporary report of Gandhi’s release](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v05/d239), [chapter 76](chapters/76-gandhi/chapter.md:99).

13. **Karachi’s Hindu population did not disappear completely.** [Line 73](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:73): “Three years later its Hindus had gone, and its population had doubled with Muslims from India.”

    Most Hindus left, but the 1951 census still recorded a Hindu population of roughly **2%**. “Most of its Hindus had gone” preserves the point without violating the rule against absolutes defeated by exceptions. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Sarah Ansari’s analysis of the 1951 census](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/files/26821739/SarahAnsari_PK_April30_SA_11_May_2016_FINAL_ACCEPTED_VERSON_.pdf).

14. **The Kashmir vote appears without the conditions that explain its failure.** [Line 93](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:93): “India promised that the people would be asked by vote which country they wished to join.” “The vote has never been held.”

    These statements need one explanatory step. The UN framework linked a plebiscite to withdrawal of outside fighters and reduction of Indian forces; the necessary demilitarisation was never achieved. Without this, readers cannot understand what prevented the vote or how the dispute became entrenched. **Confidence: high on the conditions; this is an explanatory omission, not a denial of India’s promise.** Source: [UN Security Council Resolution 47](https://mandates.un.org/mandate/S/RES/47%281948%29?end_year=1948&start_year=1948).

15. **The constitution-making assembly has not been introduced.** [Line 47](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:47): “In July he told a press conference that Congress would enter the new assembly free to change whatever it wished, including the groups.”

    A general reader has encountered provincial elections and proposed governments, but no explanation of this assembly. Identify it as the body that would write independent India’s constitution. Otherwise the reason Nehru’s statement threatened the agreement remains obscure.

16. **Military rule appears without explanation.** [Line 101](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:101): “The generals in the west refused to let it take office.”

    The preceding account has Jinnah founding a country, then suddenly generals deciding whether an elected majority may govern. Add a short bridge explaining that Pakistan had come under military rule. This is a missing causal step, not a request for a survey of Pakistan’s politics.

17. **An already-known person is unnecessarily concealed.** [Line 107](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:107): “About thirty-five million Muslims had stayed, and the new constitution, which was written by a committee under a lawyer born into the lowest rank of the caste system, declared that the state had no religion and that every adult had a vote.”

    Chapter 76 has already introduced **Ambedkar**, his background and his conflict with Gandhi. Naming him here would reward the reader’s memory and connect that struggle to constitution-making. This is a continuity and clarity problem, rather than a contradiction. See [chapter 76’s introduction of Ambedkar](chapters/76-gandhi/chapter.md:89).

18. **A historical possibility is turned into an unprovable absolute.** [Line 45](chapters/84-partition/chapter.md:45): “It was the last moment at which India could have stayed whole.”

    Calling this the last major negotiated opportunity is defensible. Claiming that unity became impossible at that exact moment goes further than the evidence can establish. This is the specific style.md problem of an absolute, not an objection to a confident interpretation.
