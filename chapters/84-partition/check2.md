## Changed facts

No existing date, numerical estimate or quotation was replaced or removed. However, several additions change what the chapter asserts, rather than merely connecting sentences.

1. **The judges acquire a different role.**  
   **Old:** “He was given a bungalow, four Indian judges for each province, two Muslims and two who were not, who could agree on nothing that mattered, and a set of maps and census tables that were years out of date.”  
   **New:** “He was also given four Indian judges for each province, two Muslims and two who were not, to advise him.”  
   “To advise him” newly characterises their institutional role.

2. **Radcliffe’s lack of visits becomes the cause of particular boundary decisions.**  
   **Old:** “He drew his lines through districts, villages and, in places, through single houses, and finished on 12 August.”  
   **New:** “As a result, he drew his lines through districts, villages and, in places, through single houses, and finished on 12 August.”  
   The preceding sentence concerns his inability to visit. The revision therefore introduces a causal explanation.

3. **Congress receives an explicit motive for refusing a coalition.**  
   **Old:** “In the largest province of the north, where it had the votes to rule alone, it refused to share office with the Muslim League, the main party of the Muslims.”  
   **New:** That sentence remains, followed by “Congress saw no reason to share what it had won.”  
   The refusal is unchanged; the categorical account of Congress’s reasoning is new.

4. **The migration receives an implied endpoint.**  
   **Old:** “Within four years about fifteen million people had crossed the borders, most of them in the first few months, in one of the largest forced migrations ever recorded.”  
   **New:** The same sentence is now introduced by “When the movement was over, its size was hard to take in.”  
   This makes the four-year count sound like a completed migration.

5. **Gandhi’s peace work receives a new starting point.**  
   **Old:** “Gandhi had opposed partition to the end, and called it vivisection, the cutting up of a living body.”  
   **New:** That sentence remains, followed by “Once it came, however, he set himself to stop the killing that came with it.”  
   This places his turn towards stopping communal violence after partition.

6. **Gandhi’s January fast becomes an explicitly numbered programme.**  
   **Old:** “In January 1948 Gandhi fasted again, in Delhi.” / “He demanded that the city's Muslims be left in safety, and that the Indian government hand over Pakistan's share of the treasury, which it had been holding back.”  
   **New:** “In January 1948 Gandhi fasted again, in Delhi, and this time he made two demands.” / “First, he demanded that the city's Muslims be left in safety.” / “Second, he demanded that the Indian government hand over Pakistan's share of the treasury, which it had been holding back.”  
   The old passage named two concerns; the new one presents them as the fast’s two defined demands.

7. **The Kashmir withdrawal sequence gains a circular dependency.**  
   **Old:** “The United Nations ruled that Pakistan's fighters must first withdraw and India's army be reduced.” / “Neither side would move first, and the vote has never been held.”  
   **New:** The revision inserts “Each step, in other words, waited on the other side.”  
   That is a new description of how the conditions worked.

8. **Export earnings become a claim about net contributions.**  
   **Old:** “For twenty years the east supplied most of the country's export earnings and received the smaller share of its spending.”  
   **New:** That sentence remains, followed by “The eastern wing, in other words, paid in more than it got back.”  
   Export earnings and government spending shares do not themselves establish the fiscal balance implied by “paid in”.

9. **Pakistan’s population loss becomes explicit.**  
   **Old:** “The eastern wing became a new country, Bangladesh.”  
   **New:** “The eastern wing became a new country, Bangladesh, and Pakistan lost more than half its people.”  
   This is consistent with the chapter’s earlier statement that the east held more than half the population.

10. **A previously implicit name is supplied.**  
    **Old:** “It did not use the name.”  
    **New:** “It did not use the name Pakistan.”  
    This is a clarification, without a change of historical meaning.

The added explanations of majority rule, Jinnah’s bargaining position and Nehru’s preference for a strong centre generally develop interpretations already present.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — The new Kashmir explanation contradicts the stated sequence.**  
   > “Each step, in other words, waited on the other side.”

   Resolution 47 did not require both sides to wait for the other to act first. Pakistan was to secure the withdrawal of tribesmen and Pakistani nationals; once the commission established that withdrawal was occurring and arrangements to stop fighting were effective, India was to begin reducing its forces. The preceding sentence’s “must first withdraw” already contradicts the new gloss. Describe an agreed sequence that was not implemented, rather than a formally circular requirement. [UN Security Council Resolution 47](https://mandates.un.org/mandate/S/RES/47%281948%29)

2. **Major — Gandhi’s fast is misleadingly presented as a two-demand ultimatum.**  
   > “In January 1948 Gandhi fasted again, in Delhi, and this time he made two demands.”

   Gandhi pressed for payment to Pakistan, and his fast influenced the cabinet’s decision. But payment was not one of two formally specified conditions whose fulfilment ended the fast. He ended it after community representatives gave a seven-point assurance concerning communal peace and Muslim security. Preserve the connection between his intervention and payment, while distinguishing it from the conditions for ending the fast. [Gandhi Smriti chronology, 17–18 January](https://www.gandhismriti.gov.in/last-144-days); [Nehru’s contemporary explanation of the payment decision](https://nehruarchive.in/documents/cable-to-vijayalakshmi-pandit-15-january-1948-go2xq1)

3. **Minor — Gandhi was already working to stop the violence before partition.**  
   > “Once it came, however, he set himself to stop the killing that came with it.”

   His Calcutta peace intervention preceded independence: he agreed to work with Suhrawardy on 11 August and moved into the disturbed neighbourhood on 13 August. His earlier work in Noakhali and Bihar preceded it by months. The accurate transition is that he **continued** working to stop communal killing as partition arrived. [Gandhi Smriti chronology](https://www.gandhismriti.gov.in/last-144-days)

4. **Minor — The new migration introduction falsely suggests completion.**  
   > “When the movement was over, its size was hard to take in.”

   The four-year total that follows is a census-based measurement, not an endpoint. Migration across Bengal continued after 1951, including substantial renewed movements in 1964–65. The corrected four-year figure should remain; the newly added completion claim should not. [Partition Museum’s account of Bengal migration](https://www.partitionmuseum.org/partition-of-india)

5. **Minor — The judges were commissioners, not merely advisers.**  
   > “He was also given four Indian judges for each province, two Muslims and two who were not, to advise him.”

   The four judges were members of each boundary commission, with Radcliffe as chairman. His sole decision became the award because the commission could not reach agreement. Identify them as fellow commissioners; their religious composition has already been corrected and is not the issue here. [Punjab Boundary Commission’s own report](https://statelesshistories.org/assets/uploads/documents/Radcliffe-Report_of_the_Punjab_Boundary_Commission-1947.pdf)

6. **Minor — Limited elections preceded 1909.**  
   > “When they first allowed Indians to elect a few members to the councils that advised the government, they gave Muslims a separate list of voters and seats reserved for them.”

   Limited indirect electoral selection existed under the 1892 arrangements, formally channelled through nomination. The 1909 reforms expanded electoral representation and introduced separate Muslim electorates; they were not the first appearance of electoral selection. [UK Parliament’s history of the councils](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/parliament-and-empire/parliament-and-the-american-colonies-before-1765/government-of-the-raj-1858-1914/)

7. **Major — Separate electorates did not make every electorate religiously exclusive.**  
   > “A Muslim politician from then on needed only Muslim votes, and a Hindu only Hindu ones, so neither had much reason to speak to the other side.”

   Muslim voters could also participate in general electorates. Reserved Muslim constituencies therefore did not produce the symmetrical Hindu-only/Muslim-only system described here. The correct distinction is between separate Muslim seats and general or other constituencies. This matters because the new causal conclusion rests on the inaccurate symmetry. [Ambedkar’s account of the 1909 arrangements, government edition](https://www.mea.gov.in/Images/CPV/Volume8.pdf)

8. **Major — Jinnah did explicitly demand sovereignty.**  
   > “He never said exactly where the borders of Pakistan would run or whether it would be wholly separate.”

   Exact boundaries remained negotiable, but the second half is contradicted by his public positions. In the 1944 Gandhi–Jinnah correspondence he identified six provinces, subject to territorial adjustments, and called for an independent sovereign Muslim state. Historians can still interpret that demand as bargaining; that interpretation does not establish that he never stated it. [*Foundations of Pakistan*, volume II, reproducing and discussing the correspondence](https://www.nihcr.edu.pk/Downloads/PDF%20Books/Foundations_of_Pakistan_Vol.II.pdf)

## Clarity flags

- **An explanation that conflicts with the opening’s political account:**  
  > “The lines were needed because of a plain fact of numbers.”

  This makes partition sound mathematically necessary. The opening and Cabinet Mission passage instead explain why political choices defeated a possible union. The demographic fact explains the fear, not by itself the necessity of borders.

- **An unsupported causal connector:**  
  > “As a result, he drew his lines through districts, villages and, in places, through single houses, and finished on 12 August.”

  “As a result” makes both the boundary’s route and the completion date consequences of not visiting. The logical connection is stronger than the information supplied.

- **A signpost that announces a coming effect:**  
  > “It also had a second effect, which lasted far longer.”

  This delays the explanation and leaves “far longer” without a clear comparison. It is the kind of announcement the style sheet prohibits.

- **A second announcement-style sentence:**  
  > “The officials found practical answers to each problem.”

  The following sentences already show the solutions through symbols and ballot-box transport. This sentence announces rather than adds.

- **Padding through immediate restatement:**  
  > “Power, in short, sat in one wing, and most of the citizens lived in the other.”

  The preceding two sentences have just said that the west held the institutions and the east held most people.

- **Repetition that also blurs the meaning:**  
  > “Each side answered the other's dead with dead of its own.”

  The previous sentence already describes reprisal attacks. “Dead of its own” can sound as though each side killed its own people.

- **A financial explanation that substitutes unlike measures:**  
  > “The eastern wing, in other words, paid in more than it got back.”

  A general reader will understand a tax-and-spending comparison. The evidence supplied concerns export earnings and spending shares. This is not an equivalent restatement.

- **New fragile absolutes:**  
  > “Each massacre was reported, with embellishments, in the next place, so that every killing helped to cause the next.”  
  > “The threat was plain to everyone: if the killing went on, the old man would die, and the killers would carry the blame.”

  “Every killing” and “everyone” add unnecessary universal claims. The causal argument does not need either.

- **Unclear scale:**  
  > “Everywhere else they were a minority, scattered among Hindus.”

  The preceding sentence describes broad regions; this one sounds like a claim about every locality. “Scattered” also obscures concentrated Muslim communities outside those regions.

There are no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit cross-references. Many of the new transitions appropriately follow the style sheet’s instruction to give readers room; those are not defects merely because they add words.

## Overall

The revision preserves the headline facts but is not entirely fact-neutral. Its main risks are new causal explanations, the numbered account of Gandhi’s fast, and the circular account of Kashmir demilitarisation. The earlier check’s repaired points have not been repeated. No files were edited.
