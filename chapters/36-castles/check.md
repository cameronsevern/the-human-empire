I found the following issues in [chapter 36](chapters/36-castles/chapter.md), ranked by seriousness. Confidence refers to the correction, not the chapter’s claim. No files were edited.

1. **The central explanation overstates the disappearance of government.**  
   > “Europe is full of castles because, for about three hundred years, most of it had no government larger than a district.”

   **Factual; high confidence.** Fragmentation encouraged castle-building in parts of Europe, but castles also served effective royal governments. William’s English castles—the opening example—helped impose centralized conquest, rather than replace an absent government. The argument needs to distinguish these two uses. [English Heritage describes royal castle-building under Henry II and Edward I.](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/story-of-england/medieval/war/)

   The related sentence, “Against enemies like these, a king was nearly useless,” also **contradicts chapter 35**, which describes Alfred defeating the Danes and subsequently says: “The English had fortified their towns and built a fleet.” Royal organization was part of the successful response. [Alfred’s coordinated defensive network](https://www.royal.uk/alfred-great-r-871-899) supplies a concrete counterexample.

2. **Land grants were not the only way to support knights.**  
   > “Somebody else therefore had to feed him, and there was hardly any coin in circulation to pay him with.”  
   > “The lords of Europe solved the problem in the one way open to them.”

   **Factual, continuity and absolute; high confidence.** Knights could receive land, wages or maintenance in a lord’s household. The correction is that land grants were an important arrangement, not the sole available one. [The Metropolitan Museum explicitly lists household maintenance and wages alongside landholding.](https://www.metmuseum.org/es/essays/feudalism-and-knights-in-medieval-europe)

   The blanket coin shortage also clashes with chapter 35’s substantial English coin payments around 1000. Chapter 37 subsequently opens with knights raising large sums of money in 1096. Regional and chronological limits are needed.

3. **The Europe-versus-everywhere-else conclusion is untenable.**  
   > “Rulers in other parts of the world did not have to hold such conversations.”

   **Factual and absolute; high confidence.** Bargaining with powerful subjects was not uniquely European. Mongol rulers, for example, depended on assemblies and elite agreement in choosing a great khan. That was not parliamentary taxation, but it breaks the broad assertion that other rulers did not need such negotiations. The defensible comparison concerns particular institutions and powers, not the existence of bargaining. [OpenStax’s account of the kurultai](https://openstax.org/books/world-history-volume-1/pages/14-2-chinggis-khan-and-the-early-mongol-empire) explains this constraint.

4. **Magna Carta is made to establish a broader, more immediate tax rule than it did.**  
   > “A king who wanted money beyond his regular dues had to call his great men together and ask.”

   **Factual; high confidence.** The 1215 provisions concerned specified feudal levies—scutage and aid—with exceptions. They did not immediately establish parliamentary control over every extraordinary tax. Wider control developed through later disputes and legislation. [The charter’s clauses 12 and 14](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/%20education/resources/magna-carta/british-library-magna-carta-1215-runnymede/) and [Parliament’s constitutional timeline](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/originsofparliament/birthofparliament/keydates/1215to1399/) distinguish these stages.

5. **The distinction between serf and slave rests on a false prohibition.**  
   > “A serf was not a slave: he could not be sold away from his village, and custom fixed what he owed.”

   **Factual and absolute; high confidence.** Serfs were usually transferred with estates, but individual sales occurred. An English document from 1170 records the sale of Aluric, with his descendants, to Bury St Edmunds. Serfdom and slavery can be distinguished without claiming that separate sale was impossible. [Legal historian Sara Butler discusses the document and its implications.](https://legalhistorymiscellany.com/2019/08/15/how-to-tell-a-serf-from-a-slave-in-medieval-england/)

6. **Magna Carta did explicitly consider serfs.**  
   > “The barons were not thinking of the serfs, who were not free men and made up about half the country.”

   **Factual; high confidence.** The protection quoted immediately before this applies to free men, but clause 20 expressly protects a villein’s means of cultivation against excessive royal financial penalties. The accurate distinction is limited protection versus equal rights, not complete exclusion. [The Magna Carta Project’s commentary on clause 20](https://magnacarta.cmp.uea.ac.uk/read/magna_carta_1215/Clause_20?com=%21all) explains its scope.

7. **The Domesday land-transfer claim confuses the secular aristocracy with all English land.**  
   > “The book shows that in twenty years nearly all the land of England had passed from several thousand English landowners to about two hundred Norman lords.”

   **Factual; high confidence.** The English secular landed elite was almost completely replaced, but those Norman magnates did not possess nearly all English land. The Crown and Church held substantial shares; the king’s principal tenants acquired about half the country’s landed wealth. [The Royal Household’s account of William’s reign](https://www.royal.uk/william-the-conqueror) gives that distinction.

8. **The university passage dates the organized guild too early.**  
   > “About 1100, in the Italian city of Bologna, students who had come from all over Europe to hear lectures on law formed a guild of their own to bargain with their teachers and landlords.”

   **Factual; high confidence that the stages are conflated.** Bologna’s teaching tradition dates from the late eleventh century, but the developed student associations and *universitates* belong to a later institutional history. Bologna’s own account places increasingly stable associations after 1183 and their consolidation into *universitates* in the early thirteenth century. Separate the beginning of teaching from the creation of the governing guild. [University of Bologna history](https://www.unibo.it/en/university/who-we-are/our-history/nine-centuries-of-history/the-students2019-universities)

9. **The account of 1265 hides who summoned the representatives.**  
   > “In England, in 1265, two knights were called from every county and two citizens from each of the main towns.”

   **Misleading context; high confidence.** Following the explanation of kings asking for money, the passive construction makes this sound like another royal initiative. Simon de Montfort controlled the government and held Henry III captive; the assembly helped sustain his regime. One sentence identifying the organizer and circumstances would prevent the wrong inference. [UK government historical account](https://history.blog.gov.uk/2015/01/20/simon-de-montforts-1265-parliament/)

   The preceding León example also needs a chronological bridge: **1188 precedes 1215**, so it cannot read as an outcome of Magna Carta.

10. **Domesday did not cover every county.**  
    > “Twenty years after his conquest he wanted to know exactly what he had won, and he sent teams of officials into every county of England.”

    **Factual and absolute; high confidence.** The survey omitted Northumberland and Durham, much of the northwest, and important cities including London and Winchester. “Most of England” would preserve the point. [National Archives guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/domesday-book/)

11. **The farming account turns one pattern into a universal history.**  
    > “Farmers had always rested half their land each year so that the soil could recover.”

    **Factual and absolute; high confidence.** Two-field cultivation was one arrangement among several. Neither its earlier universality nor its wholesale replacement by three fields is justified. Archaeological research finds substantial variation in medieval rotation and seasonal sowing. [Oxford research on crop rotation](https://doi.org/10.1093/9780191988905.003.0004)

    There is also a **reader-comprehension gap** in: “One field was sown in autumn with wheat or rye, the second in spring with oats, peas or beans, and only the third lay idle.” Explain that the fields exchanged roles in successive years; otherwise this describes permanent assignments rather than rotation.

12. **Northern Europe is given a single soil type.**  
    > “The soil of the north is heavy, wet clay.”

    **Factual; high confidence.** Northern Europe contains varied soils. The relevant claim is that the heavy plough helped exploit its **areas of fertile clay soil**. This matters to the causal argument: research tests differing benefits according to how much suitable clay a region possessed. [Andersen, Jensen and Skovsgaard’s study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387815000978)

13. **The cathedral explanation takes reduced structural dependence too far.**  
    > “After that the walls were holding up nothing.”

    **Factual and absolute; high confidence.** Ribs, piers and buttresses enabled much larger openings; they did not make the remaining masonry structurally irrelevant. Walls and infill still had weight and structural interactions. Explain that much of the vault load was concentrated at supports, allowing larger windows. [Structural engineer Jacques Heyman’s explanation](https://ingenia.org.uk/articles/why-ancient-cathedrals-stand-up-the-structural-design-of-masonry) distinguishes the loads carried by the different elements.

14. **Eight oxen becomes an invariable mechanical requirement.**  
    > “It was so heavy that it took eight oxen to pull, which was more than any one peasant owned.”

    **Factual and absolute; high confidence on variable teams.** Eight oxen was a standard Domesday accounting team, but actual teams varied. The evidence does not justify either a universal eight-animal requirement or “any one peasant.” The *Agricultural History Review* specifically cautions against assuming all medieval teams comprised eight or more oxen. [Volume 4, discussion of plough teams](https://www.bahs.org.uk/AGHR/VOLUMES/AGHR4.pdf)

15. **John’s opponents become all his barons.**  
    > “A king who pushed them too hard could find himself facing all of them together, and in 1215 that happened to King John of England.”

    **Factual and style; high confidence.** John faced a substantial rebel faction, not every baron. The chapter itself soon supplies the more accurate wording: “a large party of barons.” The negotiations were between royal and rebel parties. [Parliament’s account](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/originsofparliament/birthofparliament/overview/magnacarta/magnacartahow/)

16. **An important new social label goes unexplained.**  
    > “John had lost Normandy to the king of France, and he had spent ten years squeezing his barons for money to win it back.”

    **Reader clarity / “plain words.”** “Barons” has just appeared without an explanation of how these men relate to the lords, counts, dukes and knights already introduced. Briefly identify them as the king’s great landholders. Otherwise the reader must infer whether this is another rank or a collective name for familiar people.

17. **This is an explicit signpost sentence.**  
    > “Buried in the middle, however, are two sentences of a different kind.”

    **Style.** It announces the importance of the quotations before giving them, rather than stating the substantive difference. This falls directly under style.md’s prohibition on announcing that a point or surprise is coming.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit chapter cross-references. Apart from the chapter 35 conflicts identified above, I found no clear contradiction with chapter 27; chapter 37 mainly reinforces the need to qualify the money-shortage claim.
