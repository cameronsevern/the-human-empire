# Chapter 2 — How did humans end up everywhere?

**Line under the title:** The whole world, from about 70,000 years ago to about 700 years ago.
**Length:** 5,000 to 7,000 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples.

The movements are the story, not section headings. Facts get one lookup pass after drafting.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Humans reached every habitable part of the planet in about 50,000 years, on foot and in small boats. They did it without changing their bodies. A polar bear is built for ice and would die in a desert; a human changes clothes, tools and food instead. Wherever these people arrived, two things followed: the other kinds of human disappeared, and so did most of the largest animals. And the journey left humanity scattered across continents and islands that would lose touch with each other for thousands of years.

## The story

1. **They walked.** Answer the question at once. No wings, no fur, no great speed: a few kilometres a generation is enough to cross a continent in a few thousand years. Set a human beside other animals: each is fitted to one kind of country, and humans live in all of them with the same body. What changed from place to place was what people knew.

2. **Not the first to leave.** Upright apes had been walking out of Africa for nearly two million years, and their descendants were living across Europe and Asia: Neanderthals, Denisovans, the small people of Flores. Use the passage in `chapters/held/the-others.md`. They were human too, and each had settled into one region.

3. **The crossing.** Around 60,000 to 70,000 years ago, some bands of our own kind left north-east Africa. They were few. Everyone alive today whose ancestors lived outside Africa descends from them, which is why there is more genetic variety inside Africa than in the rest of the world put together. The ice age had locked up water and lowered the seas, so coasts ran further out and some straits were narrow.

4. **To the ends of the Earth.** Follow the routes and what each required:
   - Along the southern coast of Asia, living off shellfish and fish.
   - Australia, by at least 50,000 years ago. There was never a land bridge, so this needed boats and a crossing out of sight of land. The first sea voyage.
   - Europe, about 45,000 years ago.
   - The far north: Siberia in an ice age, made possible by the bone needle, fitted clothing, and houses built of mammoth bones.
   - The Americas, across the land that joined Siberia to Alaska, and then from Alaska to the southern tip of South America in a couple of thousand years.

5. **Meeting the others.** In Europe, Neanderthals had lived for hundreds of thousands of years. Within a few thousand years of our arrival they were gone. We know we met, because we had children together: most people outside Africa carry a little Neanderthal DNA, and people in New Guinea and Australia carry Denisovan DNA too. Tibetans owe their ability to live at altitude to a Denisovan gene. Why did the others vanish? There is little sign of massacre. They were few, and scattered in small groups. We arrived in larger numbers with wider webs of contact, and the larger web knows more (chapter 1's last idea, restated without a cross-reference). They were outnumbered, out-competed for the same game, and partly absorbed.

6. **The quiet continents.** Wherever people arrived for the first time, the largest animals died out soon after. Australia lost its giant kangaroos, a wombat the size of a car and a lizard longer than a crocodile. The Americas lost mammoths, ground sloths as big as elephants, camels and native horses. Africa, where animals had lived beside humans for millions of years and learned to fear them, lost the fewest. The ice age was also ending, and climate has been blamed. Islands settle the argument, because people reached them late and in a steady climate: in New Zealand, the giant flightless moa were gone within about two centuries of the first canoes. The narrator's view, stated plainly: we did most of it, with spears, fire and appetite, and often without noticing, since no one hunter ever saw more than a slight thinning of the herds.

7. **The last places.** Antarctica aside, the last habitable lands were the islands of the Pacific. Their settlers crossed thousands of kilometres of open ocean in double-hulled canoes, steering by stars, swells and birds, carrying pigs, chickens and seedlings. Hawaii, Easter Island and New Zealand were reached only about 700 to 1,000 years ago, later than the building of the great cathedrals. So the walk that began in Africa ended in a canoe, within recorded history.

8. **One species, scattered.** Close on what the journey left. First, everyone on Earth is closely related: the differences between peoples, such as skin colour, are recent adjustments to sunlight and are skin deep. Second, the seas rose when the ice melted and cut the land bridges. People in the Americas, in Australia and on the far islands were now on their own, and would stay so for ten thousand years or more, building their own worlds with no knowledge of the others. Third, the big game was thinning, and people in several places were starting to lean harder on plants. End on that image, without announcing what comes next.
