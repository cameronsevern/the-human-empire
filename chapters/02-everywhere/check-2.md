## Factual flags

1. **Major — “That takes many thousands of generations, and what comes out at the end is a different animal.”**  
   Climatic adaptation does not require this timescale or produce a different species. Researchers have documented changes in cold tolerance and genetic composition in green anoles following a single severe winter. The correct distinction is that cumulative culture greatly expands human adaptability, not that biological adaptation necessarily takes thousands of generations. [Campbell-Staton et al., *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28774927/)

2. **Major — “They had tools and fire as well, but each of these peoples kept to the kind of country it knew.”**  
   The surrounding contrast implies that archaic humans remained confined to one environmental type. Neanderthals occupied environments ranging from tundra to closed forests, including Mediterranean woodland. Greater ecological flexibility in *Homo sapiens* is defensible; environmental confinement in Neanderthals is not. [“Iberian Neanderthals in forests and savannahs”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jqs.3339)

3. **Minor — “It belonged to a teenage girl who died ninety thousand years ago.”**  
   “Teenage” still gives an upper age limit the evidence does not establish. The bone’s thickness indicates that Denisova 11 was **at least thirteen**, potentially older than nineteen. The earlier age flag therefore remains partly unresolved. [Slon et al., *Nature*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6130845/)

4. **Major — “A few thousand years later, about three quarters of North America’s kinds of large animal had vanished, the horses among them.”**  
   “Later” refers to the first hunters’ arrival, but the chapter itself places humans in New Mexico more than twenty thousand years ago. White Sands dates indicate roughly 23,000–21,000 years ago; the youngest occurrences of many extinct North American species fall around 13,000–11,000 years ago. That is roughly ten millennia of overlap, not a few thousand years. This corrects the chronology without rejecting human responsibility. [USGS footprint chronology](https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2025/3046/fs20253046.pdf); [megafaunal extinction chronology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4739530/)

5. **Minor — “The ice age was also ending at about this time, and for many years the warming climate was blamed instead.”**  
   This works for the American losses but not the Australian losses discussed in the same passage. Major Australian extinctions occurred tens of thousands of years before the last ice age ended—for example, approximately 45,000–43,000 years ago in southwestern Australia. The sentence needs a clear American referent. [Van der Kaars et al., *Nature Communications*](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms14142)

6. **Major — “So the pattern holds wherever it can be checked. The animals lived until people came, and died soon after.”**  
   The chapter’s own Wrangel Island example does not establish this sequence. Its mammoths disappeared around 4,000 years ago; the earliest archaeological evidence of humans follows their disappearance by several centuries. Their extinction has no established human cause. Human arrival followed by extinction is a strong pattern in many places, but “wherever” is contradicted by the evidence just invoked. [Finnish Museum of Natural History’s research account](https://www.helsinki.fi/en/luomus/news/last-mammoths-died-remote-island)

7. **Minor — “As a result, a single troop of chimpanzees in West Africa holds more genetic variety than the whole human species.”**  
   The supporting finding concerns **mitochondrial DNA variation**, not an unrestricted comparison of whole genomes. Genome-wide research places western chimpanzees among the less genetically diverse great-ape populations. Also, the preceding short dispersal timeline does not by itself explain human diversity: population history before that dispersal matters. Preserve the striking comparison by specifying mitochondrial variation and removing the simple causal link. [Gagneux et al., *PNAS*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10220421/); [Prado-Martinez et al., *Nature*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3822165/)

8. **Minor — “By the time the ice melted, about twelve thousand years ago, the Earth had become a different place.”**  
   The last glacial period ended approximately then, but the great ice sheets had not finished melting. Major deglaciation and the associated sea-level rise continued for thousands of years; one reconstruction places the main phase between about 16,500 and 8,200 years ago. “As the ice retreated” would preserve the intended time setting. [Lambeck et al., *PNAS*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1411762111)

## Clarity flags

- **“The answer had to be made, and the most important part of it was very small.”** This announces a coming reveal rather than stating it, contrary to the style sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences.

- **“Two results of that journey shaped everything that came after.”** Another explicit signpost. Together with “The first…” and “The second…”, it turns the closing section into a summary organised around numbered points, which the style sheet discourages.

- **“So humans ended up everywhere by walking, and where the land stopped, by paddling.”** “Paddling” jars immediately after the account of sailing canoes. “By boat” would encompass both methods.

- **“All of them were human.”** “Human” now includes several species, whereas earlier passages use it for our own species. A brief distinction would help a reader with no background.

- **Repeated explanation markers:** “In other words” appears six times, often after an explanation that is already clear. The repetition makes the narrator’s rhythm conspicuous.

- **“Each branch went on inventing and built its own world, and the civilisations that later arose on either side of the oceans knew nothing of each other.”** The referents are unclear after discussing both Australia and the Americas. It also appears to undo the earlier account of Polynesian–American contact. Specify which societies and period the sentence means.

The revision fixes many earlier problems and sustains a clear, engaging narrative. The main remaining corrections concern adaptation, extinction chronology, and the scope of the genetic comparison.
