## Factual flags

1. **“Every other animal is fitted to one kind of country … Animals do spread into new climates, but they do it by changing their bodies. That takes hundreds of thousands of years.”**  
   Many animals occupy several climates without becoming different species: wolves, foxes and ravens are examples. Behaviour, learning and physiological flexibility also help animals colonise new environments, and genetic adaptation need not take hundreds of thousands of years. Human cumulative culture is exceptional, but the contrast presented here is false.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

2. **“The only other animals that have managed to live everywhere are the ones we brought with us: dogs, rats and lice.”**  
   Neither humans nor these animals literally live everywhere, and numerous animals have naturally extensive distributions across continents and climates. This repeats the misleading suggestion that broad ecological adaptability is exclusive to humans and their companions.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

3. **“They are called the Denisovans … and nobody yet knows what their faces looked like.”**  
   This is outdated. In 2025, molecular evidence linked the substantial Harbin skull to Denisovans, providing direct evidence of facial anatomy, although their full range of variation remains unknown.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

4. **“These older peoples had one thing in common … each was at home in one kind of country.”**  
   Neanderthals occupied varied environments and used technological and behavioural adaptations themselves. The proposed division between archaic humans adapting through bodies and modern humans adapting through knowledge does not hold. The accompanying explanation that Flores humans necessarily descended from larger island arrivals also presents a debated evolutionary reconstruction as settled.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

5. **“Those early wanderers … died out or went back. They have no descendants today.”**  
   We cannot establish that the particular early populations represented by the Israeli fossils left no descendants. The principal ancestry of living non-African populations comes from a later expansion, but that does not prove the complete genealogical extinction of earlier dispersals.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

6. **“The sea stood far lower than it does now, by the height of a twenty-storey building at its lowest.”**  
   At the last glacial minimum, global sea level was roughly 120 metres lower, substantially more than an ordinary twenty-storey building. If the comparison refers specifically to 70,000–50,000 years ago, it needs to distinguish that interval from the ice age’s lowest sea level.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

7. **“One small population that walked out of it. It probably numbered a few thousand people.”**  
   Genetic bottleneck estimates do not directly count the people who emigrated. An inferred *effective population size* is different from a census population, and the evidence does not establish one discrete emigrating band of a few thousand. The closing description of “one small band of emigrants” makes that unsupported reconstruction even firmer.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

8. **“They were following a coast … Following the coast, the descendants of those first bands passed around Arabia, India and south-east Asia within a few thousand years.”**  
   A rapid southern coastal dispersal is a hypothesis, not an established itinerary. The relative importance of coastal and inland routes, the exit route from Africa and the timing of movement across Asia remain debated.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

9. **“It was the first sea voyage that we know of.”**  
   Much earlier hominin occupation of islands such as Flores requires sea crossings. The settlement of Australia is exceptional evidence of substantial maritime dispersal, but cannot simply be called the first known sea voyage; the intentionality and means of earlier crossings are unresolved.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

10. **“They were in Europe by about forty-five thousand years ago. There they met real winters for the first time.”**  
    Modern humans could encounter severe winters in inland and northern Asia, and evidence places them in some such environments before this date. Europe was not demonstrably their first encounter with winter.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

11. **“With needles, with fire, and with houses built from the bones and hides of mammoths, people were living north of the Arctic Circle more than thirty thousand years ago.”**  
    Arctic occupation more than 30,000 years ago is supported, but the sentence attaches a specific housing reconstruction to it without adequate evidence. The best-known substantial mammoth-bone dwellings are much later and considerably farther south. Also, fitted clothing can be made with awls and other sewing methods; an eyed needle was not its necessary starting point.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

12. **“Hunters followed herds across it … with their dogs trotting beside them.”**  
    Dogs accompanying the earliest people into the Americas is not established. Secure American dog remains are much younger than the chapter’s more-than-20,000-year-old human footprints, and proposed dates for dog domestication and arrival remain debated.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

13. **“Nobody caught a wolf and tamed it. More likely, the wolves started it.”**  
    The categorical first sentence rules out a live hypothesis. Human adoption and rearing of wolf pups remains a proposed route to domestication, alongside scavenging and other forms of association; “more likely” in the next sentence does not undo that exclusion.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

14. **“The gene that allows it came from the Denisovans.”**  
    Tibetans carry a Denisovan-related variant near **EPAS1** that contributes substantially to high-altitude adaptation. It is not a single gene that enables life and reproduction at altitude: multiple genetic and physiological factors contribute, and people without that variant can also live and have children there.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

15. **“A girl of about thirteen who died ninety thousand years ago.”**  
    The Neanderthal–Denisovan individual’s bone indicates an age of **at least about thirteen**, not an estimated age centred on thirteen. She could have been older.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

16. **“Numbers explain it better … In each valley the newcomers raised a few more children … They were outnumbered and absorbed.”**  
    This turns a plausible demographic explanation for Neanderthal disappearance into an established account. Competition, population size, interbreeding, climatic disruption and other processes remain under investigation; we do not know that a consistent reproductive advantage operated valley by valley. Interbreeding is established, but the extent to which absorption explains their disappearance is not.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

17. **“Within a few thousand years of those landings, every one of them was gone.”**  
    Australian arrival and megafaunal extinction dates do not support this precise, universal sequence. Depending on the arrival chronology, coexistence may have lasted considerably longer, and the last appearances of different animals are unevenly known.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

18. **“The difference is experience … Fire did the rest … Islands settle the argument … The animals lived until people came, and died soon after.”**  
    Human hunting and environmental changes were important causes of many extinctions, and the evidence for human destruction of moa is strong. But the chapter presents human causation, prey naïveté and fire as a settled global explanation, dismissing genuine debate about the contribution of climate and ecological change in particular regions. Island cases do not settle every continental extinction, and Wrangel Island mammoths themselves disappeared without a securely established human cause.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

19. **“A giant sloth had nothing to fear from anything.”**  
    Giant ground sloths were not universally free of predators; large American carnivores could threaten them, especially juveniles. Large size and formidable defences do not establish either invulnerability or an absence of fear.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

20. **“No raft would drift to them by luck.”**  
    Accidental drift to Pacific islands is physically possible. Deliberate navigation and provisioning explain successful settlement far better, but that does not make accidental landfall impossible.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

21. **“Some of them even reached South America and came back, because the sweet potato … was growing in Polynesian gardens.”**  
    Sweet potatoes support pre-European connections, and genetic evidence independently supports contact between Native Americans and Polynesians. Neither establishes this particular direction and round-trip itinerary: Polynesian voyages to America are one explanation, but American voyagers reaching Polynesia are another.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

22. **“The people of the Americas were now on their own … So were the people of Australia … none knew that the others existed.”**  
    Neither region was completely isolated after sea levels rose. The Americas experienced later migrations and contacts through the Arctic, as well as Norse contact; Australia received dingoes and maintained external connections, including through Torres Strait and later with visitors from Indonesia. Separation did not mean ten millennia without migration, exchange or knowledge of outsiders.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

23. **“There was no empty valley left beyond the next ridge.”**  
    If “empty” means uninhabited, this is false. Reaching every inhabited continent did not fill its habitable territory, especially with a global population of only a few million; substantial unoccupied areas remained.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

## Clarity flags

- **“Seventy thousand years ago, nearly all of them lived in Africa.”** The antecedent of “them” is “our ancestors,” but the next section introduces other ancestral human populations already outside Africa. Specify that this refers to *Homo sapiens*.

- **“Others lived in the Philippines, and another small-brained kind had been living in southern Africa.”** Unlike the Neanderthals and Denisovans, these groups receive neither names nor clear dates. Readers cannot tell whether the southern African group belongs among the humans alive 70,000 years ago.

- **“All of them were human.”** Explain briefly that “human” here includes several species or lineages within our evolutionary family, whereas “our own species” means *Homo sapiens*.

- **“The people who crossed were few.”** The preceding paragraphs describe two possible exits, then concentrate on the Red Sea. It is unclear whether “crossed” means crossed that strait or left Africa by either route.

- **“The Americas … filled quickly.”** “Filled” could mean people reached the length of the continents or occupied them densely. The evidence described supports geographical spread.

- **“About three quarters of the continent’s large animals had vanished.”** Specify that the proportion concerns species or genera, rather than individual animals, and whether it refers to North America or both Americas.

- **“A few million people now lived on it … gathering the seeds of wild grasses.”** The narrative has just reached New Zealand’s settlement roughly 700 years ago, but this ending returns to the period before agriculture. Mark that backward jump explicitly.

## Overall

The chapter’s central explanation—cumulative knowledge enabled an extraordinary human expansion—is defensible. Its largest factual weakness is presenting contested reconstructions of migration, Neanderthal disappearance and megafaunal extinction as observed sequences. The comparison with other animals also understates their behavioural flexibility and archaic humans’ cultural adaptations. The ending needs both a correction to its claims of continental isolation and a clear transition back to the period before agriculture.
