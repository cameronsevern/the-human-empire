Read the requested files; no files edited. Findings are ranked by seriousness. I found no clear direct contradiction with chapters 25, 27, or 29, and no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions, or explicit cross-references.

1. **Factual overclaim: Muslim ignorance becomes the explanation for survival.**
   > “They marched east to help an emperor who had asked for something much smaller, against enemies who did not understand for decades what the war was about.”
   > “The idea that tens of thousands of people had walked across a continent for the sake of a tomb did not occur to them.”

   Muslim responses varied. By 1105, al-Sulami explicitly understood the invasion as a Christian religious war aimed at Jerusalem and connected it with Christian expansion elsewhere. Failure to organise a united response does not establish failure to understand the crusaders’ motives. **Confidence: high** that these blanket claims need narrowing. Sources: [Alex Mallett, *Muslims and Crusaders*](https://dokumen.pub/muslims-and-crusaders-christianitys-wars-in-the-middle-east-10951382-from-the-islamic-sources-seminar-studies-2nbsped-1138543101-9781138543102.html), [Carole Hillenbrand, *The Crusades: Islamic Perspectives*](https://courses.lsa.umich.edu/israel-palestine/wp-content/uploads/sites/142/2013/12/083.-Hillenbrand-89-112.pdf).

2. **Factual error: monetary weights become individual coins.**
   > “He handed it over to the bishop as security for a loan of thirteen hundred pieces of silver and three of gold.”
   > “They signed a contract with Venice to build a fleet to carry thirty-three thousand men, for eighty-five thousand pieces of silver.”
   > “He promised that if the crusaders put his father back on the throne, he would pay them two hundred thousand pieces of silver, enough to clear the debt and finance the whole war.”

   These sums are recorded in **marks**, units of weight/account, not individual “pieces.” The substitution badly distorts the scale of the financing. Godfrey’s reported sum is 1,300 marks of silver and three marks of gold; the Venetian contract and imperial promise are 85,000 and 200,000 silver marks. Explain the unit or translate it into an appropriately rounded weight. **Confidence: very high.** Sources: [Alan V. Murray on Godfrey’s army](https://www.academia.edu/4420073/Alan_V_Murray_The_army_of_Godfrey_of_Bouillon_1096_1099_Structure_and_dynamics_of_a_contingent_on_the_First_Crusade), [Villehardouin’s account](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/villehardouin.asp).

3. **Factual error: Manzikert did not destroy the main Roman army.**
   > “In 1071 a Turkish army met the main Roman army in the far east of what is now Turkey, destroyed it, and captured the emperor.”

   The emperor was captured, but substantial forces escaped or had not participated in the decisive fighting. The subsequent civil wars and Turkish involvement in them turned defeat into the loss of Anatolia. “Defeated” is secure; “destroyed” gives the wrong causal explanation. **Confidence: high**, although casualty estimates remain disputed. Source: [Georgios Theotokis, *The Campaign and Battle of Manzikert, 1071*, introduction](https://openaccess.ihu.edu.tr/server/api/core/bitstreams/cded6b09-0910-4640-94fb-cf37487c5bd5/content).

4. **Doubtful claim: Alexios’s intentions are presented as documented quantities.**
   > “What he had in mind was a few hundred knights, perhaps a few thousand, who would take his pay, swear him an oath, and serve under his generals.”

   His appeal for military assistance is established. Its intended scale and the extent of his involvement in planning a larger expedition are disputed. The numerical range is not a securely documented specification. This matters because the supposedly accidental arrival of an unwanted mass army is a principal argument here. **Confidence: high that the certainty is unwarranted; moderate about the best reconstruction of his intentions.** Source: [Jonathan Harris, *Byzantium and the Crusades*](https://elearn.uoc.gr/pluginfile.php/303927/mod_resource/content/0/Jonathan%20Harris%20-%20Byzantium%20and%20the%20Crusades.pdf).

5. **Factual/internal inconsistency: tens of thousands of knights.**
   > “Because a pope offered the knights of western Europe a way to save their souls by doing the one thing they were trained to do, and tens of thousands of them took him at his word.”

   Grammatically, “them” means knights. Later the chapter gives approximately seven thousand knights among sixty thousand people. Tens of thousands participated, but they were not all knights. **Confidence: very high.** The chapter’s own later estimate exposes the mismatch; [Murray’s study](https://www.academia.edu/4420073/Alan_V_Murray_The_army_of_Godfrey_of_Bouillon_1096_1099_Structure_and_dynamics_of_a_contingent_on_the_First_Crusade) also distinguishes mounted knights from other participants.

6. **Factual overclaim and prohibited absolute: effortless Muslim victory.**
   > “The Muslim world could have crushed those states at any time in the next half century.”

   There was no unified “Muslim world” capable of acting as one army. The following paragraphs themselves explain the divisions. Greater aggregate resources do not establish that victory was available “at any time”; Muslim armies also attempted campaigns and suffered defeats. State the potential advantage of coordinated opposition rather than an assured counterfactual outcome. **Confidence: high** that the claim exceeds the evidence. Source: [Hillenbrand’s discussion of fragmented Muslim responses](https://courses.lsa.umich.edu/israel-palestine/wp-content/uploads/sites/142/2013/12/083.-Hillenbrand-89-112.pdf).

7. **Factual errors: the four states’ geography and chronology.**
   > “Those who stayed held four small states strung along the coast of Syria and Palestine, with a few hundred knights among them.”

   Edessa was inland, extending into northern Mesopotamia. Moreover, the immediate aftermath of 1099 did not yet contain the completed four-state arrangement: the county of Tripoli developed subsequently, and Tripoli itself fell in 1109. Mark the passage of time and avoid putting all four on the coast. **Confidence: very high.** Sources: [Routledge’s crusading chronology](https://routledgetextbooks.com/textbooks/medievalportal/9780415359689.php), [OpenStax’s map of the states](https://openstax.org/books/world-history-volume-1/pages/13-4-the-crusading-movement).

8. **Factual error and prohibited absolute: Italian participants excluded from crusading.**
   > “The other people who made the conquest last were not crusaders at all.”

   This introduces the Italian maritime cities, whose expeditions included people who took the cross. Commercial interests and crusading commitments could coexist. Caffaro reports Genoese taking the cross before the 1097 expedition. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Christopher J. Marshall, “The Crusading Motivation of the Italian City Republics”](https://deremilitari.org/2014/06/the-crusading-motivation-of-the-italian-city-republics-in-the-latin-east-c-1096-1104/).

9. **Factual error and prohibited absolute: invincible cavalry.**
   > “A warrior in a mail coat, braced in his saddle by stirrups and carrying a heavy lance, could ride down anything on foot.”

   Formed infantry could resist mounted attacks. At Hastings, within this generation, Norman cavalry could not simply break the English shield wall. Armour and stirrups did not confer the universal advantage claimed. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [English Heritage’s account of Hastings](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/1066-battle-of-hastings-abbey-and-battlefield/history-and-stories/what-happened-battle-hastings/).

10. **Factual overclaim: Urban invented armed pilgrimage.**
    > “Urban had invented the armed pilgrimage.”

    Armed pilgrimages preceded 1095, including the German pilgrimage of 1064–65. Urban’s innovation concerned the combination of an authorised military expedition, pilgrimage and penitential reward—not the first pilgrims carrying weapons. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Christopher Tyerman, *The Crusades: A Very Short Introduction*, “Holy war”](https://erenow.org/postclassical/the-crusades-a-very-short-introduction/7.php).

11. **Doubtful “first”: Christian violence against Jews.**
    > “The gospel story of the crowd that called down Christ's blood on itself and its children was by then a thousand years old, and in 1096, for the first time, large numbers of Christians acted on it with swords.”

    The unprecedented scale and significance of 1096 are defensible; the first occurrence of collective Christian anti-Jewish killing is not. Earlier attacks are recorded, including violence associated with troops travelling towards Spain in the eleventh century. Nor can the first violent application of this particular verse be securely dated. **Confidence: high that “for the first time” needs removing; moderate on comparisons of scale.** Source: [*Jewish Encyclopedia*, “France,” on persecution before 1096](https://jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/11591-normandy). Chapter 25’s discussion of the verse supplies background, but does not itself contradict this sentence.

12. **Factual error: Godfrey’s title.**
    > “He owned a castle on a rock above a river, called Bouillon, which was the seat of his family and the source of his title.”

    Bouillon supplied his territorial designation, but his ducal title was **Duke of Lower Lorraine/Lotharingia**, not Duke of Bouillon. Following “a duke named Godfrey,” the sentence invites the wrong inference. **Confidence: very high.** Source: [*Catholic Encyclopedia*, “Godfrey of Bouillon”](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06624b.htm).

13. **Reader orientation: the change from the First to later Crusades is nearly invisible.**
    > “The loss of the city brought the kings of western Europe east in person.”
    > “The crusade that followed did the most lasting damage of all, and it never reached a Muslim country.”

    Richard’s expedition is never identified as the Third Crusade, while the Second has disappeared entirely. A novice can reasonably count the expedition of 1204 as the third. One brief bridge about the failed Second Crusade, followed by identification of Richard’s expedition, would prevent this confusion. The omitted expedition took place in 1147–49. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Routledge chronology](https://routledgetextbooks.com/textbooks/medievalportal/9780415359689.php).

14. **Reader orientation: “the Temple” appears to have returned.**
    > “A priest who was with the army wrote that in the Temple men rode in blood up to their knees and the bridles of their horses.”

    Chapter 25 has explained that the Jewish Temple was destroyed; chapter 29 has introduced the later Islamic shrine on its platform. Here “the Temple” needs identifying as the crusader chronicler’s terminology for the sanctuary area/buildings, rather than a rebuilt Jewish Temple. This is a continuity ambiguity, not an actual contradiction. See [chapter 25](chapters/25-christianity/chapter.md:67) and [chapter 29](chapters/29-conquests/chapter.md).

15. **Reader orientation: anonymity obscures a recurring character.**
    > “One of their leaders had spent years trying to conquer her father's empire and now presented himself as its ally.”
    > “The crusaders considered that this released them from their oath, and the Norman leader kept Antioch for himself.”

    These refer to Bohemond, but the reader has no reliable way to connect them. Naming him at the first appearance would satisfy—not violate—the style guide’s exception for people readers meet again.

16. **Reader orientation and unexplained terminology: the military orders.**
    > “Those knights belonged to a new kind of organisation that the crusade had produced.”
    > “The orders of these soldier monks, with houses all over Europe sending them money and recruits, became the standing army of the crusader states and the builders of their enormous castles.”

    Identify Usama’s friends as the Templars, and explain “order” as a religious organisation. “Houses” also needs clarifying as establishments or estates. As written, the passage moves from anonymous guards to several unnamed international organisations without giving a novice a stable subject to follow.

17. **Style: explicit signposting.**
    > “They were starving and trapped, and what happened next shows the state of mind that carried them through.”

    “What happened next shows” announces the illustrative point before the lance story supplies it. This directly breaks the prohibition on signpost sentences. The immediate predicament already gives the reader sufficient preparation.

18. **Style: another announced explanation.**
    > “It did not, and the reason lies in how the invasion looked from the other side.”

    This promises an explanation instead of giving it. The next sentence—about Syria’s competing Turkish princes—can begin that explanation directly. This objection is to the signposting, separately from the factual overclaim in the preceding sentence.
