# 35. Who was the richest person in history?

*West Africa and the Sahara, from about 800 to 1591*

The usual answer is Mansa Musa, who ruled the empire of Mali in West Africa seven hundred years ago, and it is as good an answer as any. He owned no gold mines. He controlled the markets where the gold of West Africa was exchanged for the salt of the Sahara, and he taxed everything that passed through them.

In 1375, a Jewish mapmaker on the island of Majorca finished an atlas for the king of Aragon. It was painted on sheets of vellum mounted on wooden boards, and it showed everything that the merchants and sailors of the western Mediterranean knew or had heard about the world, from the Atlantic to China.

Most of Africa on that map is empty. South of the Atlas mountains the painter has drawn a few oases and a man in a veil riding a camel. Below them, in the middle of the blank, sits a Black king on a throne. He wears a heavy gold crown and holds a gold sceptre in one hand. In the other, he lifts up a lump of gold the size of an orange, as if offering it to the rider. A caption beside him says that this lord is called Musa of Mali, that he is the ruler of the Black people of Guinea, and that he is the richest and noblest lord in all that region, because of the abundance of gold that is gathered in his land.

The mapmaker had never been within three thousand kilometres of Mali, and Musa had by then been dead for forty years. All that reached Majorca was a name, a place and a reputation. The reputation was deserved. In the fourteenth century, a large share of all the gold in circulation in Europe, North Africa and the Middle East had come from West Africa. The first gold coins struck in western Europe since Roman times, the florins of Florence and the ducats of Venice, were minted largely from metal that had crossed the Sahara on the back of a camel.

## Houses of salt

The Sahara is about the size of the United States. For most of history it cut Africa in two as effectively as an ocean. What made it crossable was the camel, which was brought into North Africa in Roman times and became common there over the following centuries. After the Arab conquest of North Africa, Muslim merchants in the oasis towns on the desert's northern edge began to organise regular crossings.

A caravan might have a thousand camels or more. It left in the cooler months and took about two months to reach the far side, travelling from well to well under the guidance of the veiled desert herders who knew where the wells were. Losing the way meant death. So did finding a well dry.

The best description of the crossing comes from a Moroccan named Ibn Battuta, the greatest traveller of the Middle Ages, who went south with a caravan in 1352. Twenty-five days out, he came to a place in the middle of the desert called Taghaza, which he thought the most miserable village he had ever seen. It had no trees and no soil. Its houses and its mosque were built of blocks of rock salt, roofed with camel skins. The only people who lived there were the slaves of the salt merchants, who dug slabs of salt out of the ground, and they lived on dates brought from the north and camel meat. The water was brackish and the place swarmed with flies. Despite all this, Ibn Battuta notes that enormous sums in gold changed hands there.

South of the salt mine lay the worst stretch, ten days without water. The custom, he explains, was to hire a man of the desert people to ride ahead alone to the first town on the southern side. There he arranged for people to come out four days into the desert with water to meet the caravan. If the messenger died on the way, nobody came, and most of the caravan died too. Ibn Battuta's messenger got through.

The salt was worth the journey because of a simple fact of geography. The savanna and forest of West Africa have hardly any salt. People who work in tropical heat lose salt in their sweat every day and have to replace it or sicken, and their cattle need it as well. The Sahara, by contrast, has beds of pure rock salt lying just under the sand, left by lakes that dried up thousands of years ago. A slab weighed thirty or forty kilograms, and a camel carried two. Arab writers reported that in the far south, where the gold came from, salt was so precious that it was exchanged for gold weight for weight. That was a traveller's tale, but the fact behind it was real. The price of a slab multiplied several times between the mine and the southern markets.

## Nuggets for the king

The gold lay further south again, in the country around the upper reaches of the Senegal and Niger rivers, some weeks' journey beyond the desert's edge. It was found as dust and small grains in the gravel of old stream beds. Farmers dug for it in the dry season, when there was no work in the fields. They sank narrow shafts a few metres deep, hauled the gravel up in baskets, and washed it in gourds.

Those farmers answered to nobody but their own village chiefs. No king ever managed to take over the gold fields, and the miners took care that no outsider learned exactly where they dug. Arab geographers repeated a story that the gold was obtained by silent barter. The merchants laid out their salt on a riverbank and withdrew, the miners came and set a heap of gold beside each pile and withdrew in turn, and so on until both were satisfied, without the two sides ever meeting.

Between the miners in the south and the caravans from the north lay a belt of grassland, and whoever ruled that belt held the meeting point. The first state to do so was called Ghana. It lay in what is now the south of Mauritania and the west of Mali, a long way from the modern country that has borrowed its name, and it was at its height from about 800 to 1100.

Almost everything written about it comes from a geographer in Muslim Spain who never left home and who collected the reports of merchants. He wrote in 1068. The capital, he says, consisted of two towns about ten kilometres apart. One was the merchants' town, with stone houses and twelve mosques, where the traders from the north lived. The other was the king's town, with a sacred grove where the priests of the old religion kept their shrines. The king himself was no Muslim, though his treasurer and most of his ministers were.

When the king gave audience, the geographer goes on, he sat in a domed pavilion. Around it stood ten horses in cloths of gold. Behind him stood ten pages holding shields and swords mounted in gold, and on his right the sons of his vassal kings, with gold plaited into their hair. The door was guarded by dogs wearing collars of gold and silver.

The geographer also explains where the gold came from, and his account is the key to the whole system. The king took one gold coin in tax on every donkey load of salt that entered his country, and two on every load that left. He was, in other words, a customs officer on a grand scale. There was a further rule. Any nugget found in the mines of his realm belonged to the king, and only the dust was left to the people who dug it. If that were not done, the geographer says, gold would become so plentiful that it would lose its value.

## The lion's song

About the year 1100, Ghana broke up. The trade routes had shifted east, and the peoples it had ruled fell to fighting each other. Out of those wars, around 1235, came a larger empire, founded further south among the Mande people of the upper Niger.

The story of its founding was never written down by those who lived it. It has been carried for nearly eight hundred years by a class of hereditary singers, who serve as the keepers of family histories and the advisers of chiefs, and who still perform it, to the music of a harp with twenty-one strings, over several evenings. It tells of a prince named Sunjata, born so crippled that he could not walk, whose mother was mocked for it by a rival queen. In the song, he pulls himself upright on an iron rod, which bends under his weight into the shape of a bow. He grows up in exile, returns to free his people from a sorcerer king, and wins the battle that founds the empire of Mali. His name means "the lion prince".

A sung epic makes a poor record of dates. It preserves very well what a people thinks is important, and Arab writers of the next century confirm that Mali's founder was a real king of that name.

His successors, who bore the title *mansa*, pushed their rule west to the Atlantic and east along the great bend of the Niger. That river was the making of the empire. It rises near the gold fields, flows north-east to the edge of the desert, and then curves south again, and for most of its length it can carry large canoes. Goods unloaded from the camels at the desert's edge could be floated a thousand kilometres into the interior. At the top of the bend, where the camel met the canoe, stood a trading post called Timbuktu.

## Twelve years of cheap gold

Musa came to the throne of Mali about 1312. According to the account that he gave of it later, his predecessor had become convinced that the ocean to the west must have a far shore. The king fitted out a fleet of canoes with water and provisions, sailed off into the Atlantic at the head of it, and was not seen again.

Musa was a devout Muslim, as the rulers of Mali had been for several generations. In 1324 he set out to perform the pilgrimage to Mecca, a journey of more than six thousand kilometres each way. He travelled as a king, and he took with him enough gold to pay his way in a style that nobody along the route would forget.

The chroniclers of Timbuktu, writing three centuries later, say that sixty thousand people marched with him, and that five hundred slaves walked ahead of his horse, each carrying a staff of gold. Those numbers have grown in the telling. The sober evidence comes from Cairo, where the caravan rested for three months. An official of the Egyptian government, who arrived in the city twelve years afterwards, found that people were still talking about the visit, and wrote down what they told him.

Musa, they said, had flooded the city with his generosity. There was not an officer of the court or a holder of any post who had not received a load of gold from him. His followers bought cloth, slaves and singing girls in the markets and paid whatever was asked. So much gold was spent and given away that its price in Cairo fell, and twelve years later it had not recovered. Cairo was then the largest city in the Muslim world and the money market of the Mediterranean, and a visitor from beyond the Sahara had upset its currency.

The same informants preserved two other details. When Musa was brought before the sultan of Egypt, he was told that he must kiss the ground. He refused, saying that he bowed down to God alone, and the matter was smoothed over only when he agreed to bow, declaring as he did so that he was bowing to God. On the way home, moreover, he ran out of money. He had to borrow from Cairo merchants at ruinous interest, and they did very well out of it.

In Cairo, Musa also explained how his gold was obtained, and what he said confirms the Spanish geographer. The gold country, he said, lay to the south of his kingdom, among people who were not Muslims. They paid him tribute in gold. However, earlier kings had found that whenever they conquered one of the gold towns and introduced Islam there, the output of gold fell away to nothing. They had therefore left the miners to their own religion and their own rulers.

It would be easy to see the pilgrimage as vanity. It is better understood as an investment. Mali lay at the far edge of the Muslim world, and until 1324 few people in the centres of that world could have said where it was. After the pilgrimage they all knew. Egyptian merchants began to travel to Mali. The sultans of Egypt and Morocco exchanged ambassadors with its kings. Musa brought scholars, jurists and books back with him, along with a poet and architect from Muslim Spain, who built him a great mosque at Timbuktu and a palace with a domed audience hall. His gold bought him and his kingdom a place among the Muslim states, and with it the trust of the merchants who came south.

## Books at a profit

In the two centuries after Musa, Timbuktu grew from a trading post into a city of perhaps fifty thousand people and one of the centres of Islamic learning. Around its mosques gathered teachers of law, grammar, astronomy and medicine, each with a circle of students who lodged in his house. A traveller who visited in the early 1500s noted that manuscripts imported from North Africa were sold there, and that more profit was made from books than from any other merchandise. The city's leading families built up private libraries, which their descendants still keep. Some hundreds of thousands of manuscripts survive in and around Timbuktu, most of them in Arabic and some in the languages of West Africa written in Arabic letters.

Islam in West Africa was the religion of the court and the trading towns. A Muslim merchant from Cairo or Morocco could count on finding, in any town on the Niger, a mosque, a judge who knew the law of contract, and people who could read a letter of credit. The farmers who made up most of the population kept to the gods and ancestors of their villages, as the gold miners did. The kings, who needed both the merchants and the farmers, stood with one foot in each world.

When Ibn Battuta reached the capital of Mali, after his crossing of the desert, he recorded what pleased him and what did not, with his usual frankness. He admired the people's hatred of injustice and the complete safety of the roads, on which neither a traveller nor a resident had anything to fear from robbers. He admired the crowds at Friday prayers, and the way parents chained up their sons until they had learned the Qur'an by heart. On the other hand, he was shocked that women went about unveiled, and that the daughters of the king himself appeared in public without clothes. He also thought the king's welcome gift, which consisted of three loaves, a piece of fried beef and a gourd of sour milk, insultingly small.

His book mentions, in passing, the other cargo of the desert road. When he went home to Morocco, he travelled with a caravan that included six hundred enslaved women. For a thousand years, the caravans that carried gold north also carried people, who had been captured in raids on the villages of the savanna and were sold in the markets of North Africa and the Middle East as servants, concubines and soldiers. The numbers in any one year were in the thousands. Added up over the centuries, they come to several million.

## Cattle against guns

In the 1400s, Mali weakened, and its place was taken by a state further down the river. The Songhai were a people of boatmen and fishermen from the eastern side of the Niger bend. In the 1460s their king, a ruthless soldier with a fleet of war canoes, captured Timbuktu and the other river ports one after another. His successors ruled the largest empire that West Africa had yet seen, about the size of western Europe, with a standing army and a salaried administration. They drew their wealth from the same source as the kings of Ghana and Mali before them.

Their neighbours on the far side of the desert had watched that wealth for a long time. In 1590 the sultan of Morocco, who had just beaten off a Portuguese invasion and had an army with nothing to do, decided to take the gold trade at its source. His advisers told him that no army could cross the Sahara. He replied that if merchants could cross it, so could soldiers, and that the people of the south fought with spears and swords and had never faced firearms.

He sent about four thousand men, under a commander who had been born a Christian in Spain. Roughly half of them carried muskets, and many of those were Spaniards and other Europeans in Moroccan service. Eight thousand camels carried their powder, their shot and several small cannon. The crossing took about five months, and a large part of the force died on the way.

In March 1591, the survivors met the army of Songhai on the bank of the Niger. It outnumbered them several times over and included thousands of armoured horsemen. The Songhai commanders had heard of guns and had devised a plan. They drove a herd of a thousand cattle ahead of their line, to break up the Moroccan ranks and soak up the bullets. The first volleys, however, terrified the animals. The herd turned and stampeded back through the Songhai army. The battle was over within hours, and the empire did not survive it.

The victory gained Morocco very little. The sultan's men held Timbuktu and the river towns, but beyond them the empire dissolved into a patchwork of small states and the roads became unsafe. The gold fields, as ever, remained out of reach, and less gold came north after the conquest than before it.

There was a further reason, which lay on the coast. For a century, Portuguese ships had been anchoring off the shore of what is now Ghana and buying gold from African traders who had brought it down from the forest. A ship could carry in one voyage what a caravan carried in a year, and it did not have to cross a desert. The gold of West Africa had begun to flow towards the sea.

Among the Moroccans' prisoners was the most learned man in Timbuktu, a jurist named Ahmad Baba, who had spoken against the occupation. He was marched across the Sahara in chains and held in Morocco for fourteen years. His collection of sixteen hundred books was seized by the soldiers. He remarked afterwards that of all his relatives, he had owned the smallest library.
