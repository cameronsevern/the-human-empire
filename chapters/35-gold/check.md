1. **Gold ownership and political control are conflated — high confidence.**  
   > “Those farmers answered to nobody but their own village chiefs.”  
   > “No king ever managed to take over the gold fields, and the miners took care that no outsider learned exactly where they dug.”

   Local management of mining did not mean complete political independence. Al-Umari describes gold producers paying tribute to Mali and attempts at conquest followed by withdrawal. The chapter itself subsequently reports both tribute and royal entitlement to nuggets. Distinguish direct operation of mines from overlordship and tribute; the absolute “No king ever” also breaks the style rule. See Michael Gomez, *African Dominion*, pp. 108–109, discussing and quoting al-Umari. [Source](https://ebooks.umu.ac.ug/librarian/books-file/African%20Dominion%20A%20New%20History%20-%20Gomez%2C%20Michael%20A_.pdf)

2. **The first European gold coins claim is wrong — very high confidence.**  
   > “The first gold coins struck in western Europe since Roman times, the florins of Florence and the ducats of Venice, were minted largely from metal that had crossed the Sahara on the back of a camel.”

   Post-Roman western kingdoms minted gold centuries before Florence and Venice: Merovingian tremisses provide a straightforward counterexample. Describe the Italian coins as part of a revival of large-scale gold coinage. The separate assertion “largely” also needs evidence specific to those mints. [Deutsche Bundesbank’s catalogue of ancient gold coins](https://www.bundesbank.de/resource/blob/607732/99668609e0d13564c8ec9920f63c40fd/mL/ancient-gold-coins-data.pdf)

3. **The explanation of salt demand is too sweeping — high confidence.**  
   > “The savanna and forest of West Africa have hardly any salt.”

   Salt scarcity varied geographically. West Africa had coastal salt production and inland distribution networks, as well as vegetable salts. The defensible point is that many inland communities lacked convenient supplies of mineral salt, making Saharan salt valuable—not that the whole savanna-and-forest region effectively lacked it. [John Alexander, “Towards an Understanding of the Salt Industries of West Africa”](https://www.panafprehistory.org/images/uploads/TOWARDS_AN_UNDERSTANDING_OF_THE_SALT_INDUSTRIES_OF_WEST_AFRICA_John_Alexander.pdf)

4. **The Moroccan invasion’s immediate cause is misdated — very high confidence.**  
   > “In 1590 the sultan of Morocco, who had just beaten off a Portuguese invasion and had an army with nothing to do, decided to take the gold trade at its source.”

   The Portuguese defeat was in **1578**, twelve years earlier. “Just” creates a false immediate sequence, while “an army with nothing to do” substitutes an unsupported motive for Morocco’s continuing strategic concerns, including Ottoman pressure and Portuguese coastal positions. [John Hunwick, introduction to *Timbuktu and the Songhay Empire*](https://modules.oc4d.org/BookBridge/books/Ghana%20History/Timbuktu-and-the-Songhay-Empire-Al-Sa-Dis-Ta-Rikh-Al-Sudan-John-O-Hunwick-2003-Brill-Academic-Publishers.pdf)

5. **Ghana’s collapse is placed too early and made too definite — high confidence.**  
   > “About the year 1100, Ghana broke up.”

   Ghana’s decline was prolonged. Al-Idrisi still described a populous, commercially important Ghana in 1154, and archaeological evidence indicates continuing prosperity during the twelfth century. Its eventual subordination to Mali belongs in the thirteenth century. A gradual loss of dominance is safer than a breakup around 1100. [David Conrad, *Empires of Medieval West Africa*](https://sahistory.org.za/sites/default/files/archive-files3/david_c._conrad_empires_of_medieval_west_africabook4me.org_.pdf)

6. **The mosque’s attribution is doubtful — high confidence that it needs correction.**  
   > “Musa brought scholars, jurists and books back with him, along with a poet and architect from Muslim Spain, who built him a great mosque at Timbuktu and a palace with a domed audience hall.”

   Al-Sahili’s association with a decorated royal audience chamber is much better supported than his supposed construction of Timbuktu’s great mosque. His designation as an architect and the architectural achievements attributed to him have been challenged in specialist scholarship. Retain the supported audience-chamber episode without presenting the mosque attribution as established. [Suzan Aradeon, “Al-Sahili: the historian’s myth of architectural technology transfer from North Africa”](https://doi.org/10.3406/jafr.1989.2279)

7. **The ship–caravan comparison is unsupported and does not explain the gold trade — high confidence about the problem; the ratio is unverified.**  
   > “A ship could carry in one voyage what a caravan carried in a year, and it did not have to cross a desert.”

   Neither vessel size, caravan size nor cargo is specified. For compact, valuable gold, carrying capacity alone cannot explain the direction of trade. Portuguese traders also encountered difficulty diverting gold towards the coast; the change was not an automatic victory of ships over caravans. Explain access to coastal markets and competing trading networks, or supply evidence for a defined comparison. [Ivor Wilks, “Wangara, Akan and Portuguese … II. The Struggle for Trade”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/wangara-akan-and-portuguese-in-the-fifteenth-and-sixteenth-centuries-ii-the-struggle-for-trade/5B8BF887F7EC2CE4CFB4D4EB3DD3A686)

8. **The return to Ibn Battuta leaves the reader with the wrong king in mind.**  
   > “When Ibn Battuta reached the capital of Mali, after his crossing of the desert, he recorded what pleased him and what did not, with his usual frankness.”

   After the long account of Musa, subsequent references to “the king” naturally sound like Musa. Ibn Battuta visited in 1352–53, under **Mansa Sulayman**. Briefly identify him as Musa’s successor or brother before describing the supposedly stingy welcome. **Very high confidence.** [Ibn Battuta’s account](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp)

9. **The desert journey merges two distinct stages — high confidence.**  
   > “South of the salt mine lay the worst stretch, ten days without water.”  
   > “The custom, he explains, was to hire a man of the desert people to ride ahead alone to the first town on the southern side.”

   Ibn Battuta describes the ten-night stretch after Taghaza, then a stop at **Tisarahla**, where the caravan replenished its water and dispatched the messenger towards Walata. The chapter makes the messenger arrangement appear to belong to the preceding waterless stage. One sentence establishing the intervening watering stop would preserve the geography. [Ibn Battuta’s account](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp)

10. **The ending blurs imprisonment and compulsory exile — high confidence.**  
    > “He was marched across the Sahara in chains and held in Morocco for fourteen years.”

    The fourteen-year absence is defensible, but the surrounding prisoner imagery suggests fourteen years in custody. Ahmad Baba was deported in 1594, released from imprisonment in 1596, and required to remain in Morocco until his return in 1608. Distinguish imprisonment from enforced residence, and mark that his deportation occurred several years after the conquest. [*Arabic Literature of Africa*, biographical entry](https://www.rahs-open-lid.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Arabic-Literature-of-Africa_-The-Writings-of-Western-Sudanic-Africa-Handbook-of-Oriental-Studies-Handbuch-Der-Orientalistik-PDFDrive-.pdf)

11. **The monetary mechanism needs one explanatory sentence.**  
    > “So much gold was spent and given away that its price in Cairo fell, and twelve years later it had not recovered.”

    Readers may reasonably wonder how money can become cheaper. Explain that gold bought less silver: al-Umari expresses the change in silver dirhams per gold mithqal. This also clarifies what the reported price decline measures. **High confidence; the underlying twelve-year report is supported.** [Al-Umari, translated extract](https://1619education.org/sites/default/files/2022-04/Mansa%20Musa%20Primary%20Sources.pdf)

12. **An unexplained financial term is combined with an unwarranted universal.**  
    > “A Muslim merchant from Cairo or Morocco could count on finding, in any town on the Niger, a mosque, a judge who knew the law of contract, and people who could read a letter of credit.”

    “Any town” extends the claim beyond the major Muslim trading centres actually being discussed. “Letter of credit” also needs a plain explanation: a written arrangement allowing a merchant to obtain payment elsewhere without carrying the money. The earlier chapters do not supply that explanation. This breaks both the plain-words rule and the rule against vulnerable absolutes.

13. **These absolutes add claims the story does not establish.**  
    > “It was painted on sheets of vellum mounted on wooden boards, and it showed everything that the merchants and sailors of the western Mediterranean knew or had heard about the world, from the Atlantic to China.”  
    > “All that reached Majorca was a name, a place and a reputation.”  
    > “After the pilgrimage they all knew.”

    “Everything,” “all that” and “they all” turn effective descriptions into exhaustive assertions. The atlas contains a selection of geographical knowledge; the chapter cannot establish the limits of information reaching Majorca or universal awareness after Musa’s visit. These are specific violations of the absolute-claims rule, not objections to a confident voice.

14. **A minor source dispute is left on the page.**  
    > “Those numbers have grown in the telling.”

    This follows the enormous caravan figures and introduces a comparison with “sober evidence.” Under “Settle doubt before writing” and “Fix a weak example by replacing it,” the better course is to omit the doubtful figures and proceed directly to the Cairo testimony. The size of the caravan is not developed into a historical disagreement that the story needs.

15. **A signpost announces an explanation instead of giving it.**  
    > “There was a further reason, which lay on the coast.”

    This is the clearest breach of “No signpost sentences.” The following sentence about Portuguese ships can introduce the coastal development itself.
