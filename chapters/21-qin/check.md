I read the style guide and outline first, then chapter 21 and chapters 9–11. No files were changed. Findings below are ordered by seriousness; confidence refers to the correction.

1. **Factual error affecting the explanation of Qin’s collapse — [line 155](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:155):** “After the conquest there were no more enemy heads to take.”

   Qin continued fighting after 221 BCE, campaigning against the Xiongnu in the north and conquering territory in the south. The disappearance of opportunities for military rewards therefore cannot carry the explanation given here. The defensible distinction is between conquering the six kingdoms and the continuing demands of imperial expansion and construction. **Confidence: high.** Source: Charles Sanft, “The Qin dynasty,” in the [*Routledge Handbook of Imperial Chinese History*, pp. 20–21](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781317538233_A35095488/preview-9781317538233_A35095488.pdf).

2. **Factual error affecting the explanation of writing — [line 85](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:85):** “Nevertheless, because a Chinese sign stands for a word and not for a sound, they could all read the same written order, each pronouncing it in his own way.”

   Chinese characters represent meaningful units of language, normally associated with syllables; sound is integral to the system, and many characters contain a pronunciation component. Shared writing worked through learning a common written language, not through symbols that bypassed language. “They could all read” also needs to mean trained readers, not the population generally. The same misconception appears in “Each sign stands for a word, not for a sound” at line 27. **Confidence: high.** Source: the discussion of Chinese writing in [*Exploring Written Artefacts*, published by the University of Chicago’s Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures](https://isac-idb-static.uchicago.edu/multimedia/460954/isacs14.pdf).

3. **Misleading account of what the Han retained — [line 161](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:161):** “They kept the counties and the appointed officials, the registers, the ranks, the labour service, the round coin, the script and the measures.”

   Much of this is correct, but the omission matters because the chapter makes rejecting hereditary kingdoms Qin’s decisive institutional choice. Early Han restored substantial hereditary kingdoms alongside centrally governed commanderies. Its settlement combined Qin administration with a major retreat from Qin centralisation. A sentence acknowledging this is needed before saying that principally “the weight and the story” changed. **Confidence: high.** Source: [“Government of the Han dynasty,” section on kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Han_dynasty).

4. **Wrong inference from the legal evidence — [line 105](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:105):** “Fines are reckoned in shields and suits of armour, so that every careless official helped to equip the army.”

   Those were denominations of fines; they do not establish that offenders supplied military equipment. Qin administrative records show payment in cash equivalents. Keep the striking terminology, but remove the inference about equipping the army. **Confidence: high.** Source: Maxim Korolkov, [“Between Command and Market: Credit, Labour, and Accounting in the Qin Empire”](https://www.academia.edu/64835632/Between_Command_and_Market_Credit_Labour_and_Accounting_in_the_Qin_Empire_221_207_BCE_).

5. **Misleading separation of Sima Qian from the later legend — [line 139](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:139):** “The detail that they were scholars of the old books, and that they were buried alive, grew in the telling under the Han.”

   This follows a reconstruction presented as Sima Qian’s simpler account, but his account already includes Fusu identifying the victims with Confucius’s teachings. The execution term’s interpretation as *live burial* is a separate question. The paragraph needs to distinguish the victims’ identity, the method of execution, and later embellishment; it currently makes them sound like one demonstrable later addition. **Confidence: high on what Sima Qian reports; moderate on reconstructing the event.** Sources: [Sima Qian’s account](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dkjordan/chin/Symaa/Symaa31-wimp.html) and Ulrich Neininger’s [“Burying the Scholars Alive”](https://ulrichneininger.de/?p=461).

6. **Overstated completion of script reform — [line 83](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:83):** “The emperor's ministers issued a list of approved signs in the Qin style, and the others were dropped.”

   The last clause turns a programme of standardisation into an accomplished elimination of alternatives. Manuscript evidence shows continuing variation; scholars dispute the traditional picture of an immediately uniform Qin script. Describe the promotion of Qin forms without claiming that other forms simply disappeared. **Confidence: high that the present wording is too categorical; the extent of unification remains debated.** Source: Imre Galambos, [“The Myth of the Qin Unification of Writing in Han Sources”](https://shahon.org/wp-content/uploads/2010/02/Galambos-2004-The-myth-of-the-Qin-unification-of-writing-in-Han-sources.pdf).

7. **Wrong unit in the opening object — [line 9](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:9):** “Sixteen and one fifth cubic inches, it says, make one measure.”

   These are cubic *cun*, ancient Chinese units, not modern cubic inches. The distinction matters: 16.2 modern cubic inches is about 265 millilitres, whereas the museum gives this vessel’s capacity as 202.15 millilitres, consistent with your preceding paragraph. Explain “Chinese inches” or retain the original unit with a short gloss. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [the inscription’s unit](https://biblia.com/api/plugins/embeddedpreview?historybuttons=false&layout=minimal&navigationbox=false&resourceName=LLS%3A9781921816468&sharebutton=false) and the [Shanghai Museum’s measurements](https://www.shanghaimuseum.net/mu/frontend/pg/m/article/id/CI00004222).

8. **Incorrect sequence in oracle-bone divination — [line 25](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:25):** “A question was cut into the shoulder blade of an ox or the under-shell of a turtle: whether the harvest would be good, whether the queen's child would be born safely, whether the king should go to war.”

   The following sentence makes heating and cracking happen afterwards. In the usual documented sequence, the inscription recorded a divination already performed, sometimes considerably later. It was not normally a written question submitted before making the crack. **Confidence: high.** Source: Martin Kern, [“Early Chinese literature, beginnings through Western Han”](https://mkern.scholar.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf2716/files/mkern/files/kern_cambridge_history_of_chinese_literature.pdf).

9. **Misleading description of Qin’s justification of rule — [line 163](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:163):** “Above all, they stopped saying that they ruled because they were strong.”

   This implies that Qin publicly justified itself through strength alone. Qin’s imperial inscriptions also proclaimed sagehood, benefits to the people and orderly government. The Han changed the account of legitimate rule, but did not introduce moral claims into previously naked boasting about force. **Confidence: high.** Source: the Qin inscriptions discussed and translated in Sanft’s [“The Qin dynasty,” p. 19](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781317538233_A35095488/preview-9781317538233_A35095488.pdf).

10. **Continuity problem with chapter 11 — [line 39](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:39):** “War had become a matter of bookkeeping.”

    This sounds like a new development, but [chapter 11, line 97](chapters/11-bronze/chapter.md:97) already explains armies sustained by clerks recording horses, grain, bronze and chariot wheels. The new development here is the scale and reach of registration and mass mobilisation. This is a continuity problem rather than a direct factual contradiction: specify what changed in the bookkeeping.

11. **Missing bridge from chapter 11 — [line 121](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:121):** “In their hands they carried real weapons of bronze: swords, spears, and crossbows with bronze triggers.”

    Readers last saw bronze giving way to iron at the end of chapter 11. Finding this much later, exceptionally powerful army equipped with bronze may make them think the chronology has gone wrong. The sentence itself is sound; it needs a brief reminder that metalworking followed different regional histories and that bronze weapons remained important in Qin China.

12. **Unexplained administrative hierarchy — [line 89](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:89):** “Orders come down in writing, from the capital to the district, from the district to the county, and from the county to the village.”

    Counties were introduced as Shang Yang’s divisions; later, the whole country was divided into thirty-six districts. Until this sentence, the reader has not been told whether districts replaced counties or contained them. Explain that districts were the larger units containing several counties when introducing the thirty-six.

13. **Style: an absolute that the narrative itself undermines — [line 57](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:57):** “The new ruler kept every one of his laws.”

    “Every one” asserts unchanged retention of the entire legal corpus, much more than the point requires. The meaningful claim is that Shang Yang’s reforms survived his execution. This breaches the style guide’s rule against exception-sensitive absolutes; it is not an objection to confidently describing that continuity.

14. **Style: unsupported worldwide superlative — [line 67](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:67):** “So the soldiers of the most organised army on earth had to write home for their shirts, and what they asked about first, after their mother's health, was their rank.”

    “The most organised army on earth” requires a comparison the chapter does not establish. The joke works with Qin’s highly organised army; the worldwide ranking contributes no explanation and creates an unnecessary factual burden.

15. **Style: forward signpost — [line 27](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:27):** “That fact about the script would later matter a great deal.”

    This announces future significance instead of stating it, directly breaching “No signpost sentences.” The later writing passage can supply the significance when the story reaches it.

16. **Style: signpost and unnecessary certainty claim — [line 151](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:151):** “What cannot be doubted is what happened next.”

    This is precisely the prohibited announcement that an answer or decisive point is coming. Move directly to the revolt. The preceding distinction between labour service and frontier duty is substantive uncertainty, however, and should **not** be treated as a hedging stack.

I found no direct factual contradiction with chapters 9–11, no explicit chapter self-reference, and no prohibited numbered cross-reference. The continuity issues above are narrower than contradictions.
