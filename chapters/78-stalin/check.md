Ranked by seriousness. No files edited.

1. **Fact / misleading economic explanation — high confidence.**  
   > “The only resource it had in quantity was grain.”

   The USSR also possessed substantial oil, timber, coal and mineral resources. Oil, timber and furs were important exports; in 1927–28, each earned more than grain products in the published European-frontier trade figures. Grain mattered especially because it fed the towns and because the state wanted control over its supply—not because nothing else could finance imports. This also breaks the rule against exception-fragile absolutes. Source: [Soviet Union Information Bureau, contemporary foreign-trade statistics](https://www.marxists.info/history/ussr/government/1928/sufds/ch12.htm).

2. **Fact / false scholarly unanimity — high confidence.**  
   > “Nobody who has studied the evidence doubts that the famine was man-made, or that it could have been stopped by a decision in Moscow.”

   Scholars disagree about the relative contributions of policy and harvest failure, and about what relief was feasible at particular stages. Mark Tauger explicitly argues for a major environmental explanation. His argument need not be accepted to invalidate “Nobody.” State the evidence-supported conclusion about government responsibility directly, without claiming universal agreement or making prevention sound like a single, unspecified decision. Source: [Tauger, *Natural Disaster and Human Actions in the Soviet Famine of 1931–1933*](https://carlbeckpapers.pitt.edu/ojs/index.php/cbp/article/download/89/90).

3. **Fact / conflated procedures — high confidence.**  
   > “Former kulaks, priests, people with relatives abroad, people who had been heard to grumble in a queue, and members of suspect nationalities such as Poles and Germans were arrested at night, sentenced in minutes by a panel of three officials, and shot before morning.”

   This turns several operations into one universal overnight process. Three-member panels handled the principal quota operation, but national operations also used different approval procedures, including two-member bodies. Investigations and imprisonment could precede sentencing; arrest-to-execution was not routinely completed that same night. The brief review of a file is being confused with the prisoner’s entire passage through the system. Source: [François-Xavier Nérard, “The Butovo Shooting Range”](https://www.sciencespo.fr/mass-violence-war-massacre-resistance/en/document/butovo-shooting-range.html).

4. **Fact / chronology — high confidence.**  
   > “It was done in a single winter, by force.”

   Mass collectivisation **began** in the winter of 1929–30; it was not completed then. About half of households were enrolled by early 1930, many subsequently withdrew, and the proportion reached roughly 90% in 1936. Your later account of retreat, renewed pressure and the 1936 figure already contradicts this sentence. Source: [Mark Harrison, *Foundations of the Soviet Command Economy*, p. 21](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/research/centres/cage/manage/publications/283-2016_harrison.pdf).

5. **Fact / mismatched populations — high confidence.**  
   > “About a million and a half died in Kazakhstan, where the collectivisers had destroyed the herds of a people who had lived for centuries as herdsmen.”  
   > “That was more than a third of all the Kazakhs.”

   The first number includes Kazakhstan’s non-Kazakh inhabitants; the second sentence treats it as an ethnic Kazakh total. Sarah Cameron’s estimate distinguishes approximately **1.5 million deaths overall from 1.3 million ethnic Kazakh deaths**. The loss of more than a third of ethnic Kazakhs is defensible, but its numerator must be identified correctly. Source: [Cameron’s research, summarised by the Wilson Center](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/publication/the-kazakh-famine-1930-33-and-the-politics-history-the-post-soviet-space).

6. **Fact / unsupported breadth — medium-high confidence.**  
   > “The army that faced them had lost most of its experienced commanders three years before, and it was very nearly destroyed in the first six months.”

   The devastation of the **senior command** is well established. “Most of its experienced commanders” extends that claim across an undefined, much larger population. Specify senior commanders, or say that the purges had removed many experienced officers. Research distinguishing ranks finds exceptionally heavy losses among general-grade officers, rather than establishing this claim about experienced commanders generally. Source: [Zakharov and Sonin, *The Anatomy of the Great Terror*](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/BFI_WP_2024-154.pdf).

7. **Fact / wrongly dated industrial comparison — medium-high confidence.**  
   > “A country that had imported its tractors was making more of them than any other.”

   In this paragraph, readers will understand this as a late-1930s comparison. The Soviet overtaking of American tractor output belongs to the Depression trough: Lewis Siegelbaum reports approximately 50,250 Soviet versus 50,000 American tractors in 1932. American production subsequently recovered sharply, reaching about 238,000 in 1937. Give the year of the comparison or remove the world-leading claim. Sources: [Siegelbaum, *Cars for Comrades*](https://dokumen.pub/cars-for-comrades-the-life-of-the-soviet-automobile-0801446384-9780801446382.html); [US Department of Agriculture, *Agricultural Outlook, 1940*](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/324870/files/1940.pdf).

8. **Contradiction with chapter 77 — high confidence.**  
   > “The West was in the depths of the Depression, and its engineers were glad of the work.”

   Together with the grain exports, imported machinery and American contractors, this conflicts with chapter 77’s statement: “The Soviet Union had cut itself off from the world economy…” The problem is principally chapter 77’s wording. State-controlled foreign trade and insulation from international financial markets did not mean economic isolation. The American industrial contracts are documented in [Harvard’s account of Soviet planning and Albert Kahn](https://www.wcfia.harvard.edu/publications/centerpiece/spring2015/feature-crawford).

9. **Fact / “from nothing” — high confidence.**  
   > “Coal and steel output were to double or treble, and whole new industries, making tractors, aircraft, chemicals and machine tools, were to be created from nothing.”

   Several of these industries already existed. Aircraft manufacture, for example, existed under the tsars and continued through the 1920s. The plans greatly expanded and reorganised production; they did not create every listed industry from nothing. One counterexample defeats the sentence’s absolute. Source: [Andrei Sokolov, “Before Stalinism: the Defense Industry…”](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics-staging/staff-backup/mharrison/archive/noticeboard/bergson/sokolov.pdf).

10. **Earlier-chapter continuity / misleading starting point — high confidence.**  
    > “It began with a murder.”

    The antecedent appears to be Stalin’s assault on “everybody else.” Chapter 73 has already described political policing, hostage shootings and repression outside the peasantry under Lenin. Stalin’s urban deportation campaigns also preceded Kirov’s murder. Identify the particular escalation that followed December 1934, rather than making this the beginning of political terror against non-peasants. Sources: chapter 73, “Reds and Whites” and “The glass case”; [Nicolas Werth’s chronology of Stalinist repression](https://www.sciencespo.fr/mass-violence-war-massacre-resistance/en/document/mass-crimes-under-stalin-1930-1953.html).

11. **Fact / missing qualification within the chapter’s dates — high confidence.**  
    > “Schools, technical colleges and universities were opened by the thousand and were free.”

    This needs a time boundary. In 1940, before the chapter ends, fees were introduced for senior secondary and higher education. The earlier expansion of free education remains a valid point. Source: [Blumenthal and Benson, *Educational Reform in the Soviet Union*](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/363441468768731106/pdf/multi0page.pdf).

12. **Fact / unspecified statistical denominator — high confidence.**  
    > “In 1926 barely half the population could, and by 1939 four people in five could.”

    Literacy statistics exclude younger children and vary with the age range used. “The population” conceals that denominator. A standard comparable series for **ages 9–49** gives 56.6% in 1926 and 87.4% in 1939—roughly six in ten and nine in ten. If retaining the present approximations, identify and source the age group they describe. Source: [*Educational Reform in the Soviet Union*, literacy table](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/363441468768731106/pdf/multi0page.pdf).

13. **Fact / absolute contradicted by documented resistance — high confidence.**  
    > “The peasants who remained had one means of protest left.”

    Slaughtering livestock was one response. Peasants also withdrew from collectives, fled, demonstrated and mounted violent resistance. “One means” falsely closes those possibilities and breaks the style rule on absolutes. Source: [archival research on the OGPU and collectivisation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1134/S1019331622090131).

14. **Reader orientation / unexplained change of timeframe.**  
    > “About eighteen million people passed through them in Stalin’s time, and well over a million and a half died there.”

    The narrative and subtitle stop in 1941, but “Stalin’s time” silently carries the reader into the postwar years. Give the period covered by these totals explicitly. Otherwise readers can reasonably mistake them for the pre-invasion toll.

15. **Reader comprehension / a name promised but withheld.**  
    > “Between three and a half and four million people died in Ukraine, where the event is remembered by a name that means ‘death by hunger’.”

    The sentence explains a name without supplying it: **Holodomor**. This is a useful historical term the reader needs, and giving it would satisfy the style guide’s instruction to explain terms on introduction.

16. **Style / absolute inconsistent with the chapter’s own evidence.**  
    > “Then there was fear, and the absence of any other source of information.”

    The preceding paragraphs themselves show people learning through personal experience, including witnessing starving children and losing relatives to the police. The defensible point concerns the suppression of independent public reporting, not the absence of *any* other information.

17. **Style / signpost sentence.**  
    > “Then he gave them a figure.”

    This announces the numerical statement that immediately follows without adding substantive information. Under the unusually strict “No signpost sentences” rule, go straight to Stalin’s words.

18. **Style / stacked statistics.**  
    > “Three of its five marshals were shot, and thirteen of its fifteen senior generals, and tens of thousands of officers were dismissed or arrested.”

    Three numerical comparisons illustrate the same point in one sentence. This directly breaks “One number per point.” Keep the most telling figure and describe the wider losses in words.
