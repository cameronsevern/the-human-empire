1. **Factual error — high confidence.** “It closed the port of Boston until the tea was paid for, cancelled the colony's charter, and put Massachusetts under a general.” Also: “Men in Virginia and Pennsylvania who had thought the Bostonians hot-headed now saw a government abolishing an elected assembly by decree.”

   The Massachusetts Government Act altered the charter rather than cancelling it wholesale. It replaced the elected council with royal appointees, strengthened the governor and restricted town meetings; it did not itself abolish the elected House of Representatives. The later confrontation over convening the assembly is a separate event. [Source: text of the Massachusetts Government Act](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/mass_gov_act.asp).

2. **Misleading tax comparison — high confidence on the distinction; moderate on the exact ratio.** “The British government collected duties on their trade, and beyond that the average colonist paid about one fiftieth of the tax that a person in Britain paid.”

   The one-fiftieth estimate concerns **Crown taxes**, not the colonist’s total tax burden. Colonists also paid provincial and local taxes. “Beyond that” makes the comparison particularly confusing: the reader cannot tell which taxes the figure includes. Identify the recipient and basis of comparison, or omit the ratio. [Source for the Crown-tax estimate](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2025/11/king-george-iii-british-monarchy-american-revolution/684309/); [Alvin Rabushka, *Taxation in Colonial America*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt7sz5w).

3. **Factual error — high confidence.** “In one state, New Jersey, the law said ‘inhabitants’ and for thirty years women who owned property voted too, until the legislature noticed and stopped it.”

   The legislature knew. Election laws in 1790 and 1797 explicitly used “he or she,” and parties courted women’s votes. Disenfranchisement in 1807 followed political conflict and allegations of electoral abuse, not discovery of an unnoticed loophole. [Sources: Museum of the American Revolution on enfranchisement](https://www.amrevmuseum.org/virtualexhibits/when-women-lost-the-vote-a-revolutionary-story/pages/how-did-the-vote-expand-new-jersey-s-revolutionary-decade) and [disenfranchisement](https://www.amrevmuseum.org/virtualexhibits/when-women-lost-the-vote-a-revolutionary-story/pages/how-did-women-lose-the-vote-the-backlash).

4. **Factual error and exception-breaking absolute — high confidence.** “Each colony had a governor sent from London, and each had an assembly elected by its own landowners.”

   Connecticut and Rhode Island elected their governors; proprietary colonies had another arrangement again. Connecticut alone breaks “each.” Voting qualifications also varied and were not uniformly limited to landowners. [Source: Connecticut’s 1662 charter, providing for election of its governor](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/17th_century/ct03.asp).

5. **Conflated events — high confidence.** “When the war ended, Washington was the most powerful man in America, at the head of an unpaid and angry army, and some of his officers hinted that he should make himself king.” Also: “He called them together, rebuked them, and a few months later rode to where Congress was sitting, handed back his commission, and went home to his farm.”

   These sentences merge Lewis Nicola’s monarchy proposal in May 1782, which Washington rejected by letter, with the Newburgh crisis of March 1783, when officers threatened action over unpaid compensation. Washington’s meeting addressed the latter. His resignation followed in December 1783. [Sources: Mount Vernon on Nicola](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/lewis-nicola) and [Newburgh](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/newburgh-conspiracy).

6. **Contradiction with chapter 55 — high confidence.** “What was new was that a government was being founded on them.”

   “Them” includes government by consent and the people’s right to remove rulers. Yet chapter 55 already says: “Parliament abolished the monarchy and the House of Lords, and declared that the people were the source of all just power.” The American combination and implementation can be distinctive without claiming that founding government on these principles was unprecedented. [Earlier passage: chapter 55, “No Christmas”](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md).

7. **Contradictory voting figures — high confidence.** “Parliament's answer was that it represented every subject of the Crown, whether he voted or not, as it represented the nine tenths of Englishmen who had no vote either.”

   Chapter 55 gives approximately one man in five the vote. Later, chapter 56 says two-thirds of white American men qualified, “three times the share in England,” implying roughly one Englishman in five again. The ten-percent figure needs a different denominator or correction. Parliament’s historical account gives adult-male enfranchisement declining from 23 percent in 1722 to 17 percent in 1790. [Source: UK Parliament](https://committees.parliament.uk/committee/326/petitions-committee/news/99136/growing-the-political-nation-petitioning-in-the-eighteenth-century/).

8. **Factual error and exception-breaking absolute — high confidence.** “Every soldier, musket and barrel of flour had to cross three thousand miles of ocean, and an order from London took two months to arrive and was out of date when it did.”

   Britain recruited Loyalist soldiers in America and obtained provisions locally through purchasing, requisitioning and foraging. Dependence on Atlantic supply lines was a serious constraint, but “every” invents a total dependence. Nor was every arriving order necessarily obsolete. [Source: American Battlefield Trust on British logistics and local provisioning](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/conflicting-british-strategies-executing-american-revolution).

9. **Misleading constitutional scope — high confidence.** “The slave trade was protected for twenty years.”

   The Constitution prevented **Congress** from prohibiting overseas importation into existing states before 1808. It did not prevent individual states from banning importation, and the domestic slave trade did not lose constitutional protection automatically in 1808. Specify the trade and the government being restrained. [Source: Constitution, Article I, Section 9](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript).

10. **Misleading constitutional scope — high confidence.** “There was to be no king, no nobility and no official church.”

    “No official church” needs “national.” The federal settlement did not abolish state religious establishments; Massachusetts retained tax-supported religion until 1833. The explicit federal prohibition on establishment came with the First Amendment in 1791. [Source: Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/loc/lcib/9805/religion.html).

11. **Misleading account of deaths — high confidence.** “He kept it together through defeats, desertion, and a winter in which two thousand of his men died of cold and disease in their huts while Congress failed to send shoes.”

    The Valley Forge estimate is nearly two thousand deaths **from disease during the encampment**. Two-thirds occurred in March, April and May, when supplies were improving. The sentence turns this into a winter freezing scene and locates the deaths in huts without support. [Source: National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/vafo/learn/historyculture/valley-forge-history-and-significance.htm).

12. **Factual error and exception-breaking absolute — high confidence.** “Within months, crowds had burned the stamp distributors in effigy and pulled down their houses, and not one of them dared to take up his post.”

    Georgia’s distributor, George Angus, arrived in January 1766 and distributed stamps under protection; about sixty vessels cleared using stamped papers. He subsequently withdrew under pressure. “Not one” is false. [Source: Kenneth Coleman, *The American Revolution in Georgia*, University of Georgia Press](https://ugapress.manifoldapp.org/read/726350a2-1e1f-410e-ac7a-5b2fce37b1cf/section/41e9262a-0c6a-4969-8c21-f0bae74ef4ef).

13. **Factual error plus weak anecdote contrary to style.md — high confidence on the chronology; high confidence that the tune is unverified.** “After three weeks of bombardment the British general surrendered, and his bandsmen are said to have played a tune called ‘The World Turned Upside Down’.”

    The siege lasted about three weeks, but the main allied bombardment began on 9 October; Cornwallis sought a ceasefire on 17 October and surrendered on 19 October. The tune is a later, poorly supported tradition. “Are said to have” is not a hedging stack, but retaining the anecdote this way conflicts with the instruction to replace or cut weak examples. [Sources: NPS siege chronology](https://home.nps.gov/york/learn/historyculture/siegetimeline.htm); [Mount Vernon’s examination of the musical claim](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/colonial-music-institute/essays/a-march-allegedly-played-at-yorktown).

14. **Doubtful “first” — moderate confidence.** “The shots in the forest grew into the first war to be fought around the whole world.”

    Calling the Seven Years’ War the first global war is a familiar interpretation, not an uncontested factual distinction. Earlier wars, including the War of the Austrian Succession, already joined European fighting to campaigns in America and India. “A war fought across the world” preserves the point without an unstable priority claim. Chapter 54 has already introduced fighting near Madras in 1746. [Source: National Army Museum](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/war-austrian).

15. **Internal contradiction and exception-breaking absolute — high confidence.** “They touched nothing on the ships except the tea.”

    The same passage describes opening cargo chests and breaking a captain’s padlock. The intended distinction is between destroying the tea and deliberately damaging or stealing other property. “Touched nothing” contradicts the scene itself.

16. **Reader loses the institutional thread.** “He kept it together through defeats, desertion, and a winter in which two thousand of his men died of cold and disease in their huts while Congress failed to send shoes.”

    This is the first use of “Congress,” without identifying it as the colonies’ joint assembly or connecting it to the delegates previously meeting in Philadelphia. The reader then meets a wartime Congress, a Confederation Congress and a new two-house Congress without the changes being explained. One short introduction and one sentence marking the later replacement would supply the missing thread.

17. **Reader cannot picture the constitutional mechanism.** “Each could block the others.”

    The preceding sentence assigns jobs to president, Congress and judges, but does not explain how any could stop another. Give one concrete example—such as a presidential veto and Congress’s power to override it. This is where style.md’s instruction to explain how the machine works matters most. [Source for the mechanism: Constitution, Article I, Section 7](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript).

18. **Reader loses the distinction between rights and amendments.** “Ten were added in 1791.”

    The antecedent is “rights,” but ten **amendments** were ratified, several containing multiple protections. Identify them as amendments and name the Bill of Rights. Otherwise the reader is left with an unexplained number and never learns the familiar name of the settlement. [Source: National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution/how-did-it-happen).
