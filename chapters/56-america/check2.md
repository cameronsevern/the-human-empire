## Factual flags

1. **Major — new: Washington did have written restraints.**

   > “Nothing on paper stood between him and the government of the country.”

   His commission expressly required obedience to the current or a future Congress and allowed Congress to revoke it. The Articles of Confederation also assigned military authority to Congress. Washington’s willingness to respect civilian government matters precisely because that government already possessed formal authority. The correct distinction is between written restraints and their practical enforceability. [Washington’s commission, 19 June 1775](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/03-01-02-0004).

2. **Major — new: resistance was already intercolonial before the punishment of Boston.**

   > “Until then the quarrel had been about tax, and it had been possible to see it as Boston's quarrel.”

   The Stamp Act Congress brought delegates from nine colonies to **New York in 1765**. Resistance also concerned jury trials and constitutional rights, not merely tax payments. The punishment of Massachusetts intensified solidarity; it did not transform a previously Boston-centred dispute into an intercolonial one. [Library of Congress, “No Taxation Without Representation”](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/magna-carta-muse-and-mentor/no-taxation-without-representation.html).

3. **Minor — new: enforcement of the Ohio claims had already begun.**

   > “Sooner or later one of them was going to try to make its claim good.”

   At this point the narrative is in 1754. France had already built forts in the disputed region in 1753, and Washington had already delivered Virginia’s demand for withdrawal. Britain’s colonial representatives were also acting on their claims. The confrontation was already under way. [National Park Service, “Prelude to war in North America”](https://www.nps.gov/articles/prelude-to-war.htm).

4. **Minor — new: the Confederation was created during the war.**

   > “They had just fought a war against a distant government that claimed the power to tax them, and they took care not to build another one.”

   Following the recognition of independence, this makes the Confederation sound like a postwar creation. Congress adopted the Articles in **1777**, and they came into force in **1781**, before Yorktown and the peace treaty. The distrust of central taxation is a defensible explanation; the completed-war sequence is wrong. [National Archives, Articles of Confederation](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/articles-of-confederation).

5. **Minor — new framing: distinguish French backing from formal intervention.**

   > “However, there was no sense in backing rebels who were going to lose.”  
   > “That was the evidence the French needed.”

   Around the Saratoga sentence, these additions imply that France waited for that victory before backing the Americans. France was already providing clandestine assistance before Saratoga. The victory helped secure the **formal alliance of February 1778**, rather than the beginning of French support. [U.S. Department of State, French alliance](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1776-1783/french-alliance).

6. **Minor — new: the prosperity claim contradicts the chapter’s own population description.**

   > “The people who were to be asked for the money were not suffering.”

   This introduces the colonial population without distinguishing free settlers from enslaved people. The chapter subsequently states that one fifth of that population was enslaved. Relative prosperity and high wages among free colonists do not establish an absence of suffering across the population. The correct claim concerns comparatively favourable material conditions for free colonists. **Source:** the chapter’s own “The loudest yelps” section.

7. **Minor — unchanged, not previously flagged: the population figure belongs later.**

   > “There were about two and a half million of them, in thirteen colonies, and their numbers doubled every twenty-five years.”

   The surrounding narrative places this before the Stamp Act of 1765. Approximately 2.5 million is the familiar population around independence, a decade later. Census historical estimates give about **1.59 million in 1760** and **2.15 million in 1770**. Either identify the later date or use a figure appropriate to the early 1760s. [U.S. Census Bureau, colonial population estimates](https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/1960/compendia/hist_stats_colonial-1957/hist_stats_colonial-1957-chZ.pdf).

8. **Major — unchanged, not previously flagged: British control extended beyond captured cities.**

   > “The British took New York, and then Philadelphia, and each time found that they held a city and nothing else.”  
   > “The moment a British army marched into the countryside, the countryside rose behind it.”

   Britain held substantial surrounding territory, including Long Island and Staten Island, as well as Manhattan. These were not merely cities temporarily surrounded by uniformly insurgent countryside. The correct point is that capturing major cities did not end the rebellion or secure control of the colonies as a whole. [National Park Service, the Revolutionary War’s “Neutral Ground”](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/the-revolutionary-war-neutral-ground-of-westchester-county-new-york.htm).

9. **Minor — unchanged, not previously flagged: “three times” is not an exhaustive count.**

   > “The word ‘slave’ appears nowhere in the document, but slavery is in it three times.”

   The three provisions subsequently described are the principal slavery clauses, but **Article V also protected the slave-importation provision against amendment before 1808**. Calling them three principal provisions is accurate; saying slavery occurs only three times overlooks that additional protection. [Constitution transcript, Article V](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript).

## Clarity flags

1. **The tea transition now gives “that reason” the wrong immediate antecedent.**

   > “A crowd that simply wanted cheap tea would have welcomed the ships. The men of Boston destroyed it for that reason.”

   “That reason” now appears to mean wanting cheap tea or welcoming the ships. The intended reason—cheap tea would encourage acceptance of parliamentary taxation—arrives afterwards.

2. **Repetition without additional explanation.**

   > “From London, the reasoning looked hard to argue with.”

   The paragraph already begins “To the ministers in London, the next step seemed plain” and concludes its argument with “It was only fair…”. This third statement of London’s perspective adds little.

3. **The paper-tax explanation is overextended.**

   > “A tax on paper falls on the people who work with paper.”

   The next sentence names the affected occupations, and the following sentence explains their political influence. The quoted sentence is the least useful of the three.

4. **A standalone signpost.**

   > “The rounds followed a pattern.”

   This announces an explanation instead of supplying it, contrary to the explicit prohibition on signpost sentences. The next sentence already states the pattern.

5. **An exception-breaking absolute.**

   > “As a result, anyone who could read, or could listen to someone reading, was able to follow him.”

   Accessible prose does not guarantee comprehension by everyone. The preceding description already communicates Paine’s plain style.

6. **Immediate statistical repetition.**

   > “One person in five in the thirteen colonies was enslaved. A declaration that all men are created equal had come from a society that held a fifth of its people as property.”

   The second sentence usefully brings the contradiction into focus, but repeats the same statistic immediately.

7. **“Choice” confuses the desired outcome with the difficulty of acting.**

   > “For a person held in slavery, the choice between a master who talked of liberty and a king who offered it was not a hard one.”

   Wanting freedom is straightforward; deciding whether and how to flee could be dangerous and difficult. The next sentences introduce attempts and deaths, making “not a hard one” especially jarring.

8. **An abstract transition obscures its concrete meaning.**

   > “Distance was made worse by the shape of the enemy.”

   “Shape” could mean geography, military formation or political organisation. The following sentence supplies the actual point: no captured capital would end the resistance.

9. **Repeated explanation of Washington’s strategy.**

   > “The British had to destroy that army to end the war, while he had only to stop it from being destroyed.”

   This restates the preceding two sentences about keeping an army in existence, while introducing the stronger “had to” and “only” formulation.

10. **The financial shorthand blurs a useful distinction.**

    > “A government with no money of its own can do very little.”

    The surrounding issue is Congress’s inability to levy taxes directly. “No money of its own” sounds like an absence of all funds, rather than dependence on contributions and borrowing.

No hedging stacks, chapter self-reference or explicit body cross-references stood out. Most of the added transitions comply with the style sheet’s instruction to give readers room; additional length alone is not a defect.

## Overall

The revision preserves the existing numerical and quoted material, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. Several bridges introduce misleading chronology or stronger claims, especially about Washington’s authority and resistance before 1774. The earlier check’s repaired points are not repeated here.
