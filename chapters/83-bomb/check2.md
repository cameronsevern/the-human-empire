## Factual flags

1. **Minor — new chemical absolute.**

   > “Because the two forms behave alike in every chemical reaction, no chemist's trick could pull them apart.”

   Uranium isotopes have extremely similar chemical behaviour, but not identical behaviour in **every** reaction. Measurable chemical isotope effects exist. The accurate historical point is that ordinary chemical separation could not provide the straightforward solution the project needed; chemical separation is not universally impossible. [Experimental research on uranium isotope effects](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/ja954075s).

2. **Minor — wrong destination for the telephone call.**

   > “A coded telephone call went to Washington.”

   Arthur Compton called James Conant at **Harvard, in Cambridge, Massachusetts**, with the news of the Chicago experiment. Washington is the wrong destination. [“The First Pile,” reproduced by the American Nuclear Society](https://www.ans.org/pubs/magazines/nn/docs/2002-11-2.pdf).

3. **Minor — Pentagon completion predates itself.**

   > “In 1942 the project was handed to the army, under a general who had just finished building the largest office block in the world, the headquarters of the armed forces in Washington.”

   Groves took charge in **September 1942**; the Pentagon was completed on **15 January 1943**. He had overseen its construction, but had not yet finished it. [Army Historical Foundation’s biography of Groves](https://armyhistory.org/lieutenant-general-leslie-r-groves-jr/).

4. **Minor — “harmless” confuses inability to sustain a chain reaction with absence of danger.**

   > “A lump of bomb metal below a certain size is harmless, because too many neutrons escape from its surface before they can split another atom.”

   Such material remains radioactive and can present health hazards, particularly if inhaled or ingested. The intended statement concerns whether a chain reaction can sustain itself, not whether the material is harmless. [Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s plutonium backgrounder](https://www.nrc.gov/regulations-legislation/fact-sheets-brochures/backgrounder-on-plutonium).

5. **Minor — several details in the hospital scene contradict its source.**

   > “At the main hospital one surgeon, with broken glasses, worked for three days without sleep, moving along corridors packed with ten thousand wounded and able to do little more than paint burns with iodine.”

   Hersey describes Sasaki using **borrowed, ill-fitting glasses**, getting **one hour’s sleep** over three days, and using **mercurochrome**, bandages, and subsequently stitches. The ten thousand patients occupied the hospital and surrounding grounds and streets, not just its corridors. The sentence compresses distinct stages into an inaccurate scene. [John Hersey, “Hiroshima”](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1946/08/31/hiroshima).

## Clarity flags

1. **An explicit signpost has been added.**

   > “They had several reasons.”

   This merely announces the list that follows. It directly breaks “No signpost sentences.”

2. **Two additions label importance instead of explaining it.**

   > “That was the news that mattered.”

   > “The reasons against it were practical.”

   The surrounding sentences already explain both points. These add padding rather than the useful breathing room encouraged by the style sheet.

3. **The new size explanation clashes with the next example.**

   > “The trick of a bomb is to bring the metal past that size in an instant.”

   The next paragraph describes squeezing a ball of metal. A general reader will reasonably wonder how making something smaller takes it past the stated size. The explanation makes “size” sound like a single universal threshold.

4. **An inserted plural disrupts an existing singular reference.**

   > “They had to be sorted by weight. To separate it from the rest, when the two differ by about one part in a hundred in weight, was the hardest industrial problem that anyone had attempted.”

   “They” refers to both forms; “it” then reaches back several sentences to the lighter form. The insertion makes the referent harder to follow.

5. **Repeated wording in the radiation definition.**

   > “It was radiation, an invisible energy given off by the splitting atoms, which had passed invisibly through their bodies in the first second.”

   “Invisible” and “invisibly” repeat the same information within one sentence.

6. **The ending leaves its time interval undefined.**

   > “What can be said is that the war ended in a week, without an invasion…”

   A week from Hiroshima reaches 13 August; the second imperial decision came on 14 August and the broadcast on 15 August. “Without an invasion” also needs an explicit reference to the Japanese home islands, since the chapter has just described the invasion of Manchuria.
