1. **The genetic evidence does not establish a nationwide date for caste closure.** [chapters/16-caste/chapter.md:5](chapters/16-caste/chapter.md:5)  
   > “The custom that made rank so hard to escape, marrying only inside one's own small birth group, spread slowly after that, and it was not general across India until about 1,900 years ago.”

   The study estimates ancestry-mixture dates in selected populations; it does not date the universal establishment of caste. Its principal analysis excludes several major population groups. The same overreach returns in “across the country” at line 164 and the supposed resolution of the colonial-history debate at line 179. [Source: Moorjani et al., 2013](https://moorjanilab.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/2013_ajhg_priya_india_date.pdf).

   **Replacement:** “Over the following centuries, many communities increasingly restricted marriage to their own birth group, making inherited rank harder to escape.”

2. **The ancestry explanation conflates different populations.** [chapters/16-caste/chapter.md:26](chapters/16-caste/chapter.md:26)  
   > “Almost every community in India today descends from both.”

   “Both” means steppe newcomers and Indus descendants. That is not equivalent to the ancestral northern/southern mixture discussed later. The ancestral southern population formed without the steppe contribution described here. [Source: Narasimhan et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6822619/).

   **Replacement:** “The descendants of the Indus population mixed with newcomers from the steppe in the north and with other populations farther south, helping to form the ancestry of modern South Asia.”

3. **Credit did not stop at the village boundary.** [chapters/15-money/chapter.md:39](chapters/15-money/chapter.md:39)  
   > “That web has a limit, however, and the limit is the edge of the village.”

   The following claim that nobody gives a passing stranger credit compounds the error. Long-distance Assyrian commerce used loans, contracts and enforceable obligations—already described in chapter 11, lines 45–47. [Source: Veenhof, *The Archives of Old Assyrian Traders*](https://www.openstarts.units.it/handle/10077/8658).

   **Replacement:** “Credit becomes harder when the parties lack a shared circle of people who can remember and enforce the debt.”

4. **The explanation of the intellectual breakthrough rests on a false contrast with Bronze Age literacy.** [chapters/17-thinkers/chapter.md:168](chapters/17-thinkers/chapter.md:168)  
   > “The bronze palaces had also had rival kings and busy cities, but their writing had belonged to the palace clerks.”

   Merchant households kept extensive private correspondence centuries earlier, as chapter 11 itself explains. The next sentence—“Now words could be kept by people whom no palace paid”—therefore cannot identify the new development. [Source: University of Hamburg, research on Assyrian merchant education](https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/publications/mesopotamia/2025-09-12.html).

   **Replacement:** “These teachers depended on communities of pupils who kept their arguments alive, whether by copying them or learning them by heart.”

5. **The opening gives one explanation for walls built under very different circumstances.** [chapters/22-wall/chapter.md:5](chapters/22-wall/chapter.md:5)  
   > “Less to keep raiders out than to hold pasture that had been taken from them, and to decide who crossed the frontier and where trade took place.”

   The conquest argument fits important early walls, but the comparative “less” is unsubstantiated, and the answer appears to cover the Ming wall too. The later wall had a substantial defensive purpose. [Sources: discussion of Di Cosmo’s early-frontier research](https://archives.history.ac.uk/history-in-focus/Migration/reviews/pines.html); [*Cambridge History of China*, Ming introduction](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805212/43322/excerpt/9780521243322_excerpt.pdf).

   **Replacement:** “The early walls of Qin and Han guarded conquered ground, protected settlements and channelled movement through gates where the state could control it.”

6. **Silk production was not exclusively Chinese.** [chapters/22-wall/chapter.md:85](chapters/22-wall/chapter.md:85)  
   > “It is light, strong and brilliant when dyed, and for a very long time nobody outside China knew how to make it.”

   Archaeological silk from the Indus civilisation includes evidence of local silkworking in the third millennium BCE. The sentence confuses China’s particular domesticated-silkworm industry with all silk production. [Source: Good, Kenoyer and Meadow, 2009](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Good%20Kenoyer%20Meadow%202009%20Indus%20Silk.pdf).

   **Replacement:** “It is light, strong and brilliant when dyed, and Chinese households produced it in quantities that made it a mainstay of imperial payments.”

7. **The Guanzhuang date range is wrong.** [chapters/15-money/chapter.md:139](chapters/15-money/chapter.md:139)  
   > “The dates show that the foundry began casting them between about 2,650 and 2,575 years ago, in the same decades in which the kings of Lydia were stamping their lions.”

   The published range is approximately 640–550 BCE: roughly 2,665–2,575 years before 2026. More importantly, the evidence supplies a range, not a demonstrated synchronism within particular decades. [Source: Zhao et al., 2021](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/radiocarbondating-an-early-minting-site-the-emergence-of-standardised-coinage-in-china/178ECC2B245A017BF684BE1EFC732BD1).

   **Replacement:** “Radiocarbon dates place the beginning of minting between about 640 and 550 BCE, during the early history of coinage in western Asia.”

8. **One Buddhist collection becomes the entirety of the Buddha’s teaching.** [chapters/17-thinkers/chapter.md:115](chapters/17-thinkers/chapter.md:115)  
   > “Monks then handed the teaching on by chanting it in groups, and some four centuries passed before the whole of it was put into writing.”

   The first-century-BCE tradition concerns the Pali canon in Sri Lanka. There were multiple developing collections, not one recoverable “whole.” [Source: *History of the Buddhist Canon*](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62340/reference-article-abstract/554134298).

   **Replacement:** “Monks handed collections of teachings on by chanting them in groups; according to Sri Lankan tradition, the collection preserved there was written down in the first century BCE.”

9. **The account of Chinese writing teaches the wrong mechanism.** [chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:30](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:30)  
   > “Each sign stands for a whole word, where a letter of an alphabet stands for a single sound, and a modern reader can still pick out the signs for sun, moon and rain.”

   Characters generally represent a syllable carrying a unit of meaning; many words require several characters. The whole-word claim recurs at line 101, where it becomes an explanation of communication across languages. Shared written conventions also need explaining. [Source: Cambridge, *The Sounds of Chinese*](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805216/03980/excerpt/9780521603980_excerpt.htm).

   **Replacement:** “A Chinese character usually represents a syllable with a meaning, sometimes a word on its own and sometimes part of a longer word; modern readers can still recognise some early signs, including sun, moon and rain.”

   At line 101, replace the repeated claim with: “Officials learned a common written language, allowing them to read the same orders even when their spoken languages differed.”

10. **Ashoka’s reprieve is incorrectly described as the most he offered.** [chapters/20-ashoka/chapter.md:170](chapters/20-ashoka/chapter.md:170)  
    > “The most that Ashoka offers a condemned prisoner is a delay of three days, so that his relatives can appeal for his life and he can prepare for the next world.”

    An appeal could secure the prisoner’s life: the sentence itself contradicts “the most.” The edicts also record repeated amnesties, although they do not specify that every category of prisoner benefited. [Source: Pillar Edicts IV–V](https://www.cs.colostate.edu/~malaiya/ashoka.html).

    **Replacement:** “Ashoka grants prisoners sentenced to death three days in which their relatives can appeal for mercy and they can prepare for the next world.”

11. **The inspection interval is turned into a mandatory waiting period.** [chapters/20-ashoka/chapter.md:176](chapters/20-ashoka/chapter.md:176)  
    > “An inspector every five years, however, is a long wait for a man in prison.”

    A scheduled imperial inspection does not establish that a prisoner had no other opportunity for review. The same edict mentions other inspection arrangements. [Source: Separate Kalinga Edict I](https://www.cs.colostate.edu/~malaiya/ashoka.html).

    **Replacement:** “A promise of inspection still left a prisoner dependent on officials whose conduct the emperor could not watch from his capital.”

22. **The geometry leaves out a necessary condition.** [chapters/19-alexander/chapter.md:174](chapters/19-alexander/chapter.md:174)  
    > “If the Earth were flat, those rays would strike every upright stick at the same angle, and at any one moment all sticks would cast the same shadow.”

    Sticks of different heights cast different-length shadows. The explanation needs equal sticks, not an absolute covering all sticks.

    **Replacement:** “On a flat Earth, parallel rays would strike every upright stick at the same angle, so sticks of equal height would cast shadows of equal length at the same moment.”
