## Factual flags

1. **Major — privatisation is placed after the miners’ defeat.**

   > “With the unions beaten, she began to shrink the state itself.”

   The miners returned in March 1985, but Right to Buy began in 1980 and British Telecom’s first share sale occurred in November 1984. Privatisation and council-house sales were already under way. The new transition creates an incorrect chronology. Sources: [House of Commons Library on Right to Buy](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-12193/), [BT’s share-sale history](https://www.bt.com/about/investors/individual-shareholders/shareholder-administration/faqs).

2. **Major — the new transition gives Greece the wrong route into crisis.**

   > “Having taken on the banks' debts, governments now found their own debts under suspicion, and the crisis moved on to them.”

   Bank rescues were central to Ireland’s sovereign crisis. Greece’s initial crisis centred on longstanding fiscal imbalances and the revelation of substantially understated deficits. The sentence makes absorbing bank debts the explanation for the Greek example that follows. These were different routes into the wider sovereign-debt crisis. Source: [European Stability Mechanism’s account of the crisis’s origins](https://www.esm.europa.eu/publications/safeguarding-euro/defending-symbol-euro-here-stay).

3. **Major — selling a mortgage did not necessarily eliminate the originator’s exposure.**

   > “In other words, the person who decided whether a family could afford a house no longer bore any of the cost if it could not.”

   This conflates a commission-paid broker with the lender approving and originating the mortgage. Originators commonly made contractual representations and warranties and could have to repurchase defective loans or loans that defaulted early. The incentive problem is real, but “any of the cost” is false as a general description. Source: [Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission staff report on securitisation](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/archival-collection/financial-crisis-inquiry-commission-4967/preliminary-staff-report-520526/fulltext).

4. **Minor — AAA describes creditworthiness, not intended scarcity.**

   > “That grade was meant to be rare.”

   AAA signifies an exceptionally strong capacity to meet financial commitments. Rarity is not its defining criterion or a quota imposed by the rating system. The relevant failure was assigning unjustifiably high assessments of creditworthiness, not issuing too many ratings merely because the category was supposed to be scarce. Source: [S&P’s explanation of credit ratings](https://www.spglobal.com/ratings/en/credit-ratings/about/understanding-credit-ratings).

5. **Major — the controllers’ employment ban has the wrong scope.**

   > “When the country's air traffic controllers went on strike, he dismissed eleven thousand of them and banned them from federal employment for life.”

   The initial general federal-employment restriction was three years. In December 1981 Reagan permitted the dismissed controllers to apply for federal jobs outside the FAA; the continuing exclusion concerned FAA employment. It was not a lifetime ban from all federal employment. Sources: [Reagan’s memorandum](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/speech/memorandum-about-federal-employment-discharged-air-traffic-controllers), [his accompanying statement](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/speech/statement-federal-employment-discharged-air-traffic-controllers).

6. **Minor — the unemployment level is presented as the number newly deprived of work.**

   > “The head of the central bank, Paul Volcker, had meanwhile raised interest rates to twenty per cent, and put one worker in ten out of a job.”

   The recession brought total unemployment to about 10.8%; it did not newly remove one-tenth of workers from employment. Unemployment was already 7.4% at the recession’s beginning. The monetary-policy explanation can stand, but the sentence confuses the resulting unemployment rate with the increase. Source: [Federal Reserve history of the 1981–82 recession](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/recession-of-1981-82).

7. **Major — Greek youth unemployment uses the wrong denominator.**

   > “A quarter of Greek workers and half of the young were left without jobs.”

   The figures describe unemployment among the respective **labour forces**, not half of all young people. Students and other economically inactive young people are excluded from the unemployment-rate denominator. Nor does the rate establish that everyone counted had lost a previous job. Correctly stated, overall unemployment reached roughly a quarter, and youth unemployment exceeded half of the youth labour force. Sources: [Eurostat’s explanation](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/SEPDF/cache/25184.pdf), [Greek statistical service’s December 2012 release](https://www.statistics.gr/documents/20181/988214/Labour%2BForce%2BSurvey%2B%28LFS%29/5c5d874f-d377-4829-8250-3e5f21a9fa9a?version=1.0).

8. **Minor — the elephant curve is described using a different measure.**

   > “At the far end, it shoots up the raised trunk, where the very richest took the largest share of all.”

   The elephant curve plots **percentage growth in real income**, not each group’s share of total income gains. Large absolute gains at the top are supported by a separate calculation, but they are not what the trunk’s height measures. This is distinct from the earlier check’s concern about identifying the people in the trough. Source: [Christoph Lakner’s explanation of relative and absolute gains](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/distribution-income-amongst-world-s-citizens).

## Clarity flags

1. **New signpost sentences breach the explicit rule.**

   > “How the richest countries on Earth came to stand so close to the edge is a story that begins thirty-five years earlier, with a different kind of crisis.”

   > “To see what was taken away, it helps to recall what had been put in place.”

   Both announce the narrative’s next move. The first also reintroduces the kind of signposting identified in the earlier check.

2. **“By then” attaches to the wrong apparent date.**

   > “Its finance minister used the crisis to abolish most of the permits and open the country to trade, and India grew faster over the next thirty years than it ever had.”

   > “By then the argument seemed to be over.”

   The nearest endpoint is approximately 2021, but the next sentences return to the Soviet collapse and 1996. The transition makes the chronology harder to follow.

3. **The bowl analogy needs an overflow mechanism.**

   > “Picture water poured into a stack of bowls: the top bowl fills first, and the bottom one goes dry first.”

   Ordinary stacked bowls do not pass water down automatically. Nor does filling them explain why one subsequently “goes dry”. The image needs to distinguish priority in receiving payments from priority in absorbing losses.

4. **The Occupy cross-reference is ambiguous.**

   > “Their slogan was that they were the ninety-nine per cent, and the one per cent they had in mind were the people at the top of the chart.”

   Two different distributions have appeared: American income shares and global income growth. “The chart” is both an unclear reference and an avoidable body cross-reference.

5. **A sequence of new sentences repeats or announces the next point.**

   > “That last detail turned out to matter most.”

   > “That is the good news, and it is very large.”

   > “What there was instead was bad enough.”

   > “To many people the lesson seemed plain.”

   These add little explanation. The following sentences already supply the significance, contrast or lesson.

6. **The leverage explanation repeats itself.**

   > “Their own money was a thin cushion, and a small slip in prices was enough to go straight through it.”

   This immediately follows the concrete statement that a three-per-cent fall would wipe the banks out. The numerical example has already made the point clearly.

7. **Several additions introduce brittle absolutes.**

   > “As a result, every bank was suspected.”

   > “The whole structure rested on one assumption, that house prices would not fall everywhere at once.”

   > “The point was that a family's savings would never be riding on a trader's bet.”

   “Every”, “one” and “never” make these claims broader than the explanation needs. Banking separation did not eliminate all securities activity: commercial banks could, for example, underwrite government securities. Source: [Federal Reserve history of Glass–Steagall](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/glass-steagall-act).

8. **The comparative claim about publicity is unexplained.**

   > “The people in the trough were the ones who heard the most about success.”

   “The most” suggests a comparison of what different populations were told, but nothing establishes that comparison. The following sentence about factory workers already conveys the intended contrast.

9. **Production metadata remains in a caption.**

   > “The file is 640 pixels wide.”

   This tells the reader about the image file rather than the historical scene and breaks the narrative voice.

I found no hedging stacks or explicit references to “this chapter”.

## Overall

The revision improves pacing but is not fact-neutral. Its most consequential new errors are the privatisation chronology and the explanation leading into Greece. Several other additions strengthen claims unnecessarily or restore prohibited signposting. The fixed issues from the earlier check have not been repeated here. No files were edited.
