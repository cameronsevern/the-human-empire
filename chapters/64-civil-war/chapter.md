# 64. Why did America fight a civil war?

*The United States, from 1850 to 1877*

America fought a civil war because eleven states tried to leave the country in order to protect slavery, and the rest of the country went to war to stop them leaving. The North did not set out to free the slaves. It ended by doing so, because the enslaved people themselves, by the hundred thousand, walked away from their masters and turned a war about the Union into a war about freedom.

On the night of 23 May 1861, three men stole a small boat on the coast of Virginia and rowed across the mouth of a river to a stone fortress on the far shore. Their names were Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory and James Townsend. They were enslaved field hands, and their owner, a colonel of the Virginia militia, had put them to work on Confederate defences. They had learned that he meant to send them further south, away from their families.

The fortress was still held by the army of the United States. Its commander, a general who had been a lawyer and politician in Massachusetts until a few weeks before, now had a problem. The war was six weeks old. The president had said repeatedly that it was being fought to restore the Union and that he had no intention of interfering with slavery. Federal law required that escaped slaves be returned to their owners.

The next day a Confederate officer came to the fortress and asked for the three men back. The general thought it over. Virginia claimed to be a foreign country, he said, and the law of the United States no longer applied to it. The men had been used to build fortifications against him. He would therefore keep them, as he would keep any enemy property being used for war. He called them "contraband".

It was a lawyer's trick, and word of it spread through the slave quarters of Virginia overnight. More people arrived at the gate within days, and then whole families. By the summer there were nine hundred, and the government in Washington had been forced to start answering a question it had hoped to avoid.

## The greatest material interest

In 1860 there were four million enslaved people in the United States, one in every eight Americans. Nearly all of them lived in the fifteen states of the South.

They were, in the cold arithmetic of the market, the most valuable property in the country after the land itself. A healthy young field hand sold for about as much as a house. Added together, the people held as slaves were worth more than all the nation's railways, banks and factories combined.

What made them so valuable was cotton. The mills of Lancashire and New England needed a river of raw fibre, and the hot, wet lands along the lower Mississippi could grow it better than anywhere on Earth. By 1860 the American South supplied three quarters of the cotton spun in British mills, and cotton alone made up more than half of everything the United States sold abroad. To plant it, a million enslaved people had been taken from the old tobacco states of the east coast to the new lands of the south-west. Many went with their owners. Hundreds of thousands were sold to traders and marched there in chains, in a business that broke up families on an enormous scale.

Slavery in 1860 was not a dying institution that would have faded quietly if left alone. It had never been more profitable. Only about a quarter of white Southern families owned slaves, but those families held the wealth, the offices and the newspapers, and most of the rest hoped to join them. When the state of Mississippi explained its reasons for leaving the Union, it began by saying that its position was thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery, which it called the greatest material interest of the world.

## Free soil

The states of the North had all but ended slavery within their own borders in the decades after independence, and by 1860 they made up a different kind of society. They had nineteen million people, most of the country's factories, and a creed. A man who worked hard could expect to rise, from hired hand to owner of his own farm or shop, and no one was born to be another's master.

Most Northerners held that creed without much love for Black people, and without any plan to free the slaves of the South. A small, noisy minority, the abolitionists, demanded an immediate end to slavery as a sin. They included men and women who had escaped from it and could say what it was. They were widely regarded as dangerous fanatics. A novel about a slave sold down the river, published in 1852, sold three hundred thousand copies in a year and made a great many Northern readers weep, and most of them still drew the line at interfering with another state's affairs.

What the North would not accept was slavery's spread. The quarrel was over the West. As the country grew, each new territory had to be either open to slavery or closed to it. To Northern farmers, the West was where a poor man got his start, and they did not intend to compete for land with planters who worked it with unpaid gangs. To Southern planters, the West was survival. Cotton wears out the soil. Since every state has two senators, each new free state was two more votes against them. If slavery could not expand, the slave states would shrink into a permanent minority, at the mercy of a majority that disliked their way of life.

For forty years Congress had kept the peace by drawing lines and balancing admissions. In the 1850s every bargain failed. A law of 1850 obliged citizens of free states to help catch runaways, and brought the slave catcher onto the streets of Boston. A law of 1854 tore up the old dividing line and opened the northern plains to slavery wherever the settlers voted for it. Supporters and opponents poured into the territory of Kansas with rifles to settle the vote by killing one another. In 1857 the Supreme Court ruled that Congress had no power to keep slavery out of any territory, and that no Black person, enslaved or free, could be a citizen of the United States. The chief justice wrote that the country's founders had regarded Black people as having no rights which the white man was bound to respect. Two years later an abolitionist named John Brown led twenty-one men in a raid on a federal weapons store in Virginia, hoping to start a slave rising. He was hanged. In the North, church bells tolled for him.

A new political party had formed in the middle of all this, with one main principle: no slavery in the territories, anywhere, ever. It called itself Republican.

## The cornerstone

In November 1860 the Republican candidate, Abraham Lincoln, was elected president. He was a tall, awkward, self-educated lawyer from Illinois, born in a log cabin, who had said in a famous speech that a house divided against itself cannot stand, and that the country could not endure for ever half slave and half free. He had also said, many times, that he had neither the right nor the wish to touch slavery in the states where it already existed.

The manner of his victory mattered as much as his views. He won with four votes in every ten. Presidents are chosen state by state, and he carried the free states, which was enough. In ten Southern states his name had not even appeared on the ballot. A president had been chosen without any help from the South at all, by a party that existed to contain slavery. Southern leaders concluded that they had lost control of the national government for good.

South Carolina declared itself out of the Union six weeks after the election. By February 1861 six more states of the cotton belt had followed, and together they formed the Confederate States of America. In April, Confederate guns opened fire on a federal fort in the harbour of Charleston. Lincoln called for troops to put down the rebellion, and four more states left to join the rest.

In later years it was often said that the South had fought for the rights of the states against an overbearing central government, and that slavery was incidental. The men who led the secession did not say that. The vice-president of the Confederacy explained in a speech, a few weeks after taking office, how the new government differed from the old. The United States, he said, had been founded on the assumption that the races were equal. That was an error. The cornerstone of the new government rested upon the great truth that the Negro is not equal to the white man, and that slavery, subordination to the superior race, is his natural and normal condition.

## Twenty-two million against nine

On paper the contest was uneven. The states that stayed in the Union, including four slave states on the border, had twenty-two million people. The Confederacy had nine million, of whom three and a half million were enslaved. The North made nine tenths of the country's manufactured goods, including nearly all its guns and locomotives, and it had more than twice the South's length of railway.

The South, however, did not need to conquer anybody. It needed only to hold on until the North grew tired. Its territory was the size of western Europe. Its officers included many of the best in the old army, and its soldiers were defending their homes.

Southern leaders were also counting on cotton. Nearly half a million people worked in the cotton mills of England, and several times that number lived by the trade. If the supply were cut off, they reasoned, the mills would close, the workers would riot, and Britain would be forced to break the Union's naval blockade and recognise the Confederacy. One senator had put it in three words before the war: cotton is king.

The mills did close. By the end of 1862, hundreds of thousands of Lancashire people were living on charity. Britain stayed out all the same. Its warehouses had been full when the war began, it found new supplies in Egypt and India, and its government would not go to war on the side of slavery once the issue was plain. At a meeting in Manchester on the last day of 1862, working men voted to send a letter to Lincoln, telling him that they supported his cause. He wrote back to thank them for an instance of sublime Christian heroism.

## The rifle and the camera

Both sides expected a short war, settled by one great battle. It lasted four years, and it was a new kind of war.

Part of the cause was a small piece of lead. For two hundred years the foot soldier's weapon had been a musket with a smooth barrel, which could not be relied on to hit a man at eighty metres. Armies therefore fought standing up, shoulder to shoulder, and a determined charge could cross the killing ground before the defenders had fired more than once or twice. A barrel with spiral grooves cut inside it, which spin the bullet, is far more accurate. It had been too slow to load for the ordinary foot soldier, until a French officer designed a bullet with a hollow base that slid down the barrel easily and swelled to grip the grooves when fired.

The rifles that most soldiers carried by the middle of the war could kill at two or three times the range of the old muskets. The generals, who had been trained in the old tactics, went on ordering charges. On a single day in September 1862, beside a creek in Maryland, twenty-three thousand men were killed, wounded or missing, more than on any day in American history before or since.

Other machines changed the war as well. Railways carried whole armies hundreds of kilometres in days and kept them fed, so that campaigns were fought for control of junctions. The telegraph let the president sit in the war office in Washington reading messages from generals in the field as they were sent. At sea, two warships plated with iron fought each other to a standstill in 1862, and every admiral in the world understood that the wooden battleship's day was over.

A few weeks after the battle in Maryland, a gallery in New York exhibited photographs taken on the field before the dead were buried. The crowds who came to look had seen paintings of battles, full of flags and rearing horses. A newspaper wrote that the photographer had done something very like bringing the bodies and laying them in the dooryards and along the streets.

## Forever free

Meanwhile the three men at the fortress had been followed by tens of thousands. Wherever a Union army marched, enslaved people left the plantations and came into its lines, on foot, in carts and in stolen boats, bringing their children. They dug the army's trenches, cooked its food, drove its wagons, and told its officers where the enemy was.

Their flight forced a choice. Lincoln had begun the war insisting that it was about the Union alone, partly from conviction and partly because four slave states had stayed loyal and he could not afford to lose them. In the summer of 1862 he wrote that if he could save the Union without freeing any slave, he would do it, and if he could save it by freeing all the slaves, he would do that. By then he had already decided. The South's three and a half million slaves were growing its food and digging its defences. Every one who left weakened the rebellion, and every one who took up a rifle strengthened the Union.

On 1 January 1863 he signed a proclamation declaring that all persons held as slaves in the areas still in rebellion were, from that day, forever free. It did not apply to the loyal slave states or to districts already under Union control, and it could be enforced only where Union armies went. All the same, it changed what the war was. From then on, each advance of the Union army was an advance of freedom, and it became far harder for any British government to side with the South.

The proclamation also threw the army open to Black men. By the end of the war about a hundred and eighty thousand had served, one Union soldier in every ten, and a great many of them had been slaves two or three years before.

That November, Lincoln travelled to a town in Pennsylvania where, in three days of July, fifty thousand men had been killed, wounded or captured in the largest battle of the war. He had been asked to say a few words at the opening of a cemetery. He spoke for two minutes. The war, he said, was a test of whether a nation founded on the proposition that all men are created equal could survive. The living owed it to the dead to see that government of the people, by the people, for the people did not perish from the Earth.

## The march

The Union won in the end by turning its advantages in men and factories into a deliberate strategy. In 1864 Lincoln gave command to Ulysses Grant, who understood that the Confederacy would fight on as long as it had armies and the means to feed them. Grant set out to destroy both.

He himself took the main army against the Confederate capital. He kept hold of the enemy for a year without a pause, sidestepping after each battle to strike again, and accepted losses that horrified the North, because he knew that he could replace his men and the enemy could not. The fighting there ended in fifty kilometres of trenches. Meanwhile a second army, under William Sherman, captured Atlanta, the railway hub of the deep South, and then marched nearly five hundred kilometres to the sea, cutting loose from its supply lines. It lived off the country and wrecked everything of use to the enemy along a path a hundred kilometres wide: railways, mills, cotton gins and barns. Sherman said that he meant to make the South feel the hard hand of war.

In April 1865 the main Confederate army, starving and surrounded, surrendered at a village in Virginia. Grant let the defeated soldiers keep their horses, since they would need them for the spring ploughing.

Five days later, on the evening of Good Friday, Lincoln went to the theatre in Washington. An actor who supported the Confederacy walked into his box during the third act and shot him in the back of the head. He died the next morning.

The war had killed about seven hundred and fifty thousand soldiers, about as many as all of America's other wars put together. Two thirds of them died of disease in the camps. In the South, about one white man in five of fighting age was dead. In December 1865 an amendment to the Constitution abolished slavery everywhere in the United States.

Other countries ended bondage in those same years without a war. The tsar of Russia freed twenty-three million serfs by decree in 1861. Britain had bought out its slave owners. The United States was different because its slave owners ruled a compact region of their own, were richer than they had ever been, and believed that they could win.

## Forty acres

Four million people were now free, and nearly all of them owned nothing. What they wanted above all was land. In the last winter of the war, Sherman set aside a strip of abandoned plantations along the coast and settled forty thousand freed people on it in plots of forty acres. Lincoln's vice-president, who succeeded him, was a Southerner who had stayed loyal to the Union and had no love for the freed slaves. Within a year he had pardoned the former owners and given the land back.

Congress overrode him. For a decade it tried to remake the South. It placed the defeated states under military rule and amended the Constitution twice more. Former slaves, and everyone else born under the laws of the United States, were citizens, and no state could deny any person the equal protection of the laws. No man could be denied the vote on account of his race. Black men voted in great numbers, and some two thousand were elected to office. In 1870 a Black minister took his seat in the Senate for Mississippi.

The white South fought this with terror. Secret societies of former Confederate soldiers rode at night in hoods, flogging and murdering Black officeholders, teachers and voters and the whites who worked with them. Stopping them required soldiers, and the North was tired of sending soldiers. In 1877, as part of a bargain over a disputed presidential election, the troops guarding the last Southern governments elected with Black votes were ordered back to their barracks.

Left to themselves, the Southern states spent the next twenty years undoing the result. Most freed people ended up farming their former masters' land for a share of the crop, permanently in debt to the landowner's store. New laws separated the races in schools, trains and hospitals. Others set tests and taxes for voting that were designed to exclude Black men without mentioning race. In Louisiana in 1896 there were a hundred and thirty thousand Black men registered to vote. Eight years later there were thirteen hundred.
