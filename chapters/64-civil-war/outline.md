# Chapter 64 — Why did America fight a civil war?

**Line under the title:** The United States, from 1850 to 1877.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

The war was about slavery. Eleven states left the Union to protect it after losing an election to a party that had promised only to stop it spreading, and their leaders said so at the time. Slavery in 1860 was not a dying relic: the four million enslaved people were the most valuable property in the country, and their cotton fed the mills of the world. The North went to war to keep the country whole, not to free anyone. It ended up doing both, because enslaved people walked to the Union lines by the hundred thousand and made themselves the issue, and because the North needed them as soldiers. The fighting was the first great war of the industrial age, with rifles, railways and telegraphs, and it killed more Americans than any war since. Slavery was abolished. The equal citizenship promised afterwards lasted about ten years.

## The story

1. **Opening: three men in a boat.** Answer first. May 1861: three enslaved men row to a Union fort, and a general has to decide what they are.
2. **The greatest material interest.** What slavery was worth in 1860, what cotton meant, and why nobody who owned slaves thought the system was ending.
3. **Free soil.** What the North wanted. Why the fight was over the new territories. A decade of failed bargains, a court ruling, a raid.
4. **The cornerstone.** The election of 1860, a president elected without a single southern state, and what the seceding states said about their reasons.
5. **Twenty-two million against nine.** The odds. Why the South thought cotton would bring Britain in, and why it did not.
6. **The rifle and the camera.** A new kind of war: rifles that kill at four times the range, armies moved by rail and steered by telegraph, and photographs of the dead.
7. **Forever free.** How a war for the Union became a war against slavery. The proclamation, the Black regiments, a speech of two minutes.
8. **The march.** 1864 and 1865: war on the enemy's farms and railways. The surrender. A theatre. The count of the dead.
9. **Ending: forty acres.** Citizenship and the vote, written into the Constitution. Land promised and taken back. Terror, the soldiers leave, and the voters vanish from the rolls.
