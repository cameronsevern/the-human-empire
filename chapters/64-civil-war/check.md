I found the following issues, ordered by seriousness. Line numbers refer to [chapter.md](chapters/64-civil-war/chapter.md). No files were edited.

1. **Fact — the Emancipation Proclamation’s exceptions are understated.**  
   > “On 1 January 1863 he signed a proclamation declaring that all persons held as slaves in the rebel states were, from that day, forever free.”

   It excluded Tennessee altogether and specified exempt areas in Louisiana and Virginia, as well as excluding loyal slave states. The next sentence therefore does not supply all the necessary qualifications. Say it freed enslaved people in **designated areas still in rebellion**. **Confidence: high.** [National Archives, including the proclamation’s text](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/emancipation-proclamation).

2. **Doubtful explanation — the rifle is made to explain the war’s duration and slaughter.**  
   > “The cause was a small piece of lead.”  
   > “The rifles that both armies carried by 1862 killed at four times the range of the old muskets.”

   Greater potential accuracy is real; routinely fighting at four times the distance is not. Training, visibility and battlefield conditions often kept engagements at short range. Earl Hess’s research directly challenges the claim that rifle muskets explain prolonged, indecisive and unusually bloody warfare. Widespread replacement of smoothbores also continued beyond 1862. Distinguish a weapon’s potential range from its actual battlefield effect. **Confidence: high that this needs qualification; the size of the rifle’s effect remains debated.** [Hess, *The Rifle Musket in Civil War Combat*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv2wdtkt4).

3. **Fact — federal troops did not all leave the South in 1877.**  
   > “In 1877, as part of a bargain over a disputed presidential election, the last federal troops were withdrawn.”

   Hayes withdrew troops protecting the remaining Republican state governments in Louisiana and South Carolina to their barracks. Federal military presence in the South continued. The consequential withdrawal was of **protection for those governments**, not every soldier. **Confidence: high.** [Hayes Presidential Library’s explanation](https://www.rbhayes.org/hayes/frequently-asked-questions-on-the-1876-election/).

4. **Fact — both battle totals include prisoners and missing men.**  
   > “On a single day in September 1862, beside a creek in Maryland, twenty-three thousand men were killed or wounded, more than on any day in American history before or since.”  
   > “That November, Lincoln travelled to a town in Pennsylvania where, in three days of July, fifty thousand men had been killed or wounded in the largest battle of the war.”

   These are approximately **total casualties**, including captured and missing soldiers. NPS lists about 21,000 killed or wounded at Antietam; Gettysburg’s roughly 51,000 casualties likewise include thousands captured or missing. Either retain the familiar totals and explain “casualties,” or use killed-and-wounded totals. **Confidence: high.** [NPS Antietam breakdown](https://www.nps.gov/anti/learn/historyculture/casualties.htm); [NPS Gettysburg historical report](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/FileSource/Get/2257218?filename=GETT_CLR_Record_of_Treatment_v1_508.pdf).

5. **Fact — the citizenship statement erases a major exclusion.**  
   > “Everyone born in the United States was a citizen, entitled to the equal protection of the laws.”

   The Fourteenth Amendment included a jurisdiction qualification, then interpreted to exclude Native people maintaining tribal allegiance. Universal Native American citizenship came through later legislation, culminating in 1924. This omission is particularly conspicuous immediately after chapter 63’s account of Native dispossession. Also, equal protection applies to persons, not just citizens. **Confidence: high.** [Amendment text](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/14th-amendment); [Smithsonian’s explanation of the Native exclusion](https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/reconstruction/citizenship).

6. **Fact — the Dred Scott ruling is replaced by a misleading shorthand.**  
   > “In 1857 the Supreme Court ruled that Congress had no power to keep slavery out of any territory, and that Black people, whether enslaved or free, had no rights which the white man was bound to respect.”

   The operative citizenship ruling excluded people descended from Africans brought to America and enslaved from federal citizenship and the relevant access to federal courts. The notorious “no rights” passage was Taney’s purported description of historical white attitudes, used to justify that exclusion—not a holding abolishing every right Black people possessed under state law. State the citizenship ruling, then attribute the racist language to Taney if retained. **Confidence: high.** [National Archives, full opinion](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/dred-scott-v-sandford).

7. **Fact and reader comprehension — free states already outnumbered slave states.**  
   > “If slavery could not expand, the free states would in time outnumber the slave states, and then they would have the votes to do as they liked.”

   California’s admission in 1850 had already broken the numerical balance. The fear was an **increasing and durable political disadvantage**. Nor did a simple majority permit Congress to do anything it liked: constitutional protections and amendment requirements mattered. Remind readers that two senators per state made new states politically important. **Confidence: high.** [U.S. Senate’s account of the sectional balance and California’s admission](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/civil_war/RoadtoWar.htm).

8. **Fact — enslaved people were not the largest asset category without exception.**  
   > “They were, in the cold arithmetic of the market, the most valuable thing in the country.”

   Land was worth more. The following comparison with railways, banks and factories conveys the enormous investment without this false superlative. **Confidence: high.** [David Blight’s Yale lecture explicitly identifies land as the exception](https://oyc.yale.edu/history/hist-119/lecture-2).

9. **Doubtful statistic — the cotton percentage needs its denominator checked.**  
   > “By 1860 the American South supplied three quarters of the world's cotton, and cotton alone made up more than half of everything the United States sold abroad.”

   The export-value claim is sound. The world-production share is questionable: NPS gives roughly two-thirds, while Sven Beckert gives **77 percent of cotton consumed in Britain** in the late 1850s. Three-quarters of British supply is not three-quarters of worldwide production, which includes cotton consumed within producing countries. **Confidence: medium-high that the present percentage needs correction or a specific supporting source.** [NPS economic overview](https://www.nps.gov/articles/industry-and-economy-during-the-civil-war.htm); [Beckert’s figures](https://www.wcfia.harvard.edu/publications/empire-cotton-0).

10. **Doubtful explanation — Grant’s strategy becomes continuous frontal attrition.**  
    > “He himself took the main army against the Confederate capital, attacking continuously for a year and accepting losses that horrified the North, because he knew that he could replace his men and the enemy could not.”

    Grant maintained pressure, but repeatedly manoeuvred to turn Lee’s positions, then conducted siege operations against Petersburg and its supply routes. “Attacking continuously” and the single explanation about replaceable men obscure that strategy. The Army’s campaign history expressly challenges the traditional picture of Grant simply overwhelming Lee through expendable manpower. **Confidence: high on the operational correction; medium-high on the interpretation of motive.** [U.S. Army, *The Overland Campaign*](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo50645/pdf/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo50645.pdf).

11. **Fact — forced migration is conflated with sale and marching.**  
    > “To plant it, a million enslaved people had been sold away from the old tobacco states of the east coast and marched in chains to the new lands of the south-west, in a trade that broke up families on an enormous scale.”

    The approximately one million describes forced migration. Some people moved with enslavers rather than through sale, and transport included ships as well as overland marches. “Forcibly moved” preserves the scale and brutality without assigning everyone the same experience. **Confidence: high on the distinction; estimates of the respective shares vary.** [NPS research discussing migration versus sale](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/FileSource/Get/2293486?filename=AfAm+NCA+HSR_Final_June2022_ED_508.pdf); [Historic New Orleans Collection](https://hnoc.org/virtual-exhibitions/purchased-lives).

12. **Internal inconsistency and reader confusion — “North” and “South” change boundaries.**  
    > “It had twenty-two million people, most of the country's factories, and a creed.”  
    > “The South's four million slaves were growing its food and digging its defences.”

    The first sentence follows a description of the North as having abolished slavery, but twenty-two million represents the **Union states, including loyal slave states**. The second uses the national enslaved population as though everyone were supporting the Confederacy, despite the earlier Confederate figure of three and a half million. Establish whether each passage means free states, Union states, slave states or Confederate states. **Confidence: high; the chapter’s own figures reveal the mismatch.**

13. **Fact and absolute — northern emancipation was not complete.**  
    > “The North had ended slavery within its own borders in the decades after independence, and by 1860 it was a different kind of society.”

    New Jersey retained people in lifetime “apprenticeship,” a continuation of bondage, until the Thirteenth Amendment. “Almost entirely ended slavery” would cover the exception without derailing the account. **Confidence: high.** [New Jersey Historical Commission history](https://www.nj.gov/state/historical/assets/pdf/topical/afro-americans-in-nj-short-history.pdf).

14. **Misleading election summary — New Jersey was an exception.**  
    > “He won with four votes in every ten, carrying every free state.”

    Lincoln received four of New Jersey’s seven electoral votes, but lost its popular vote to the opposition fusion ticket. “Carrying every free state” suggests an uncomplicated sweep. This is also where a general reader needs one sentence explaining how state electoral votes allowed a candidate with roughly 40 percent of the national popular vote to win. **Confidence: high.** [New Jersey’s official report, discussing the 1860 result](https://www.nj.gov/governor/equity/docs/reports/WDTF_Report.pdf).

15. **Fact — Black army service preceded the proclamation.**  
    > “The proclamation also opened the army to Black men.”

    Black regiments were already being formed in Kansas, Louisiana and South Carolina during 1862. The proclamation authorised and expanded recruitment as national policy; it did not initiate all Black army service. **Confidence: high.** [NPS account of the earlier units](https://www.nps.gov/articles/exceeding-expectations.htm).

16. **Fact and absolute — rifles had already been used in battle.**  
    > “It had been too slow to load for battle, until a French officer designed a bullet with a hollow base that slid down the barrel easily and swelled to grip the grooves when fired.”

    Earlier rifles were slow to load, but riflemen had fought effectively in the American Revolution and subsequent wars. The improvement made rifles practical for widespread infantry use; it did not make battlefield use possible for the first time. **Confidence: high.** [NPS on rifles and muskets in the Revolution](https://home.nps.gov/teachers/classrooms/rifles-vs-muskets-in-the-revolutionary-war.htm).

17. **Fact and absolute — wooden fleets remained useful.**  
    > “At sea, two warships plated with iron fought each other to a standstill in 1862, and every wooden navy in the world was obsolete by the afternoon.”

    Hampton Roads demonstrated the vulnerability of unarmoured ships against ironclads. It did not make wooden navies useless: fourteen wooden vessels participated in the successful Union attack at Mobile Bay in 1864. Narrow the claim to the implications for future armoured battle fleets. **Confidence: high.** [U.S. Naval History and Heritage Command, Mobile Bay](https://www.history.navy.mil/news-and-events/news/2025/nhm-080525.html).

18. **Doubtful explanation and absolute — emancipation did not alone determine British policy.**  
    > “From then on, each advance of the Union army was an advance of freedom, and no British government could think of siding with the South.”

    Emancipation made intervention on the Confederacy’s behalf politically harder. “No British government could think” converts that constraint into an unsupported impossibility. Military prospects, Union diplomacy and European concerns also shaped nonrecognition. **Confidence: high that the absolute overstates the evidence.** [State Department historical account](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1861-1865/confederacy).

19. **Fact and absolute — some freed people already owned property.**  
    > “Four million people were now free, and they owned nothing.”

    Most emerged from slavery without land or substantial assets, but some had earned wages and purchased land during the war, notably around Port Royal. The point is widespread deprivation and the failure of general land redistribution, not universal possession of nothing. **Confidence: high.** [NPS on freedpeople’s land purchases beginning in 1863](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/land-ownership-an-effect-of-the-port-royal-experiment.htm).

20. **Reader comprehension — the 1854 law’s explosive significance is missing.**  
    > “A law of 1854 opened the northern plains to slavery if the settlers voted for it, and supporters and opponents poured in with rifles to settle the vote by killing one another.”

    Chapter 63 has already explained the Missouri Compromise line. The reader needs to hear that this law overturned its restriction, and that the violent contest occurred particularly in Kansas. “Northern plains” does not make that connection. This is a missing bridge to earlier knowledge, rather than a contradiction.

21. **Reader comprehension — anonymity makes the ending harder to follow.**  
    > “In the last winter of the war, the general who had marched to the sea set aside a strip of abandoned plantations along the coast and settled forty thousand freed people on it in plots of forty acres.”  
    > “Within a year the new president, a Southerner, had pardoned the former owners and given the land back.”

    Sherman recurs enough to justify naming him when his march begins. Identifying Andrew Johnson as Lincoln’s vice-president and a Southern Unionist would explain how a Southerner succeeded the murdered Union president. The present wording leaves an avoidable political puzzle.

22. **Style — explicit signposting.**  
    > “This matters for understanding the war.”  
    > “There was one more novelty.”  
    > “For a decade, nevertheless, something remarkable was attempted.”

    These announce importance, novelty or a remarkable development before giving it. They conflict with *style.md*’s instruction to state the thing itself. The first two can disappear without losing information; the third needs to introduce the attempted political reconstruction directly.
