## Factual flags

1. **Major — the division of Grant’s and Sherman’s tasks is false.**

   > “Grant set out to destroy both, and he gave each task to a separate army.”  
   > “The enemy's armies he took on himself.”  
   > “The means to feed those armies were left to a second army, under William Sherman.”

   Sherman was assigned both to defeat Johnston’s army and to destroy Confederate resources. Grant’s April 4, 1864 orders explicitly combine those objectives. This was coordinated campaigning against armies and resources, not a division between Grant fighting soldiers and Sherman destroying supplies. [Grant’s orders, reproduced in Sherman’s memoirs](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5852/old/shr3w10h.html).

2. **Major — the new charging-distance claim reinstates a previously corrected error.**

   > “A charge now had to cross two or three times as much ground under fire that could kill.”

   A weapon’s potential range does not establish the distance over which battlefield charges actually received effective fire. Visibility, training, terrain and firing practices often kept engagements at short range. The preceding “could kill” formulation preserves the distinction; this addition removes it. This flag concerns the newly introduced sentence, not the already corrected range figure. [Earl Hess, *The Rifle Musket in Civil War Combat*](https://kansaspress.ku.edu/9780700623839/).

3. **Major — emancipation measures preceded January 1863.**

   > “Until then, the Union army had been fighting to bring the rebel states back as they were.”

   By 1862, federal policy already prohibited military returns of fugitives and provided freedom for specified enslaved people under the confiscation legislation. The proclamation greatly expanded emancipation; it did not mark the first departure from restoring the slave states unchanged. The sentence also clashes with the chapter’s preceding account of refugees already changing the war. [National Archives, “The Revolutionary Summer of 1862”](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2017/winter/summer-of-1862).

4. **Major — Haiti contradicts the claim of an unprecedented wartime price for abolition.**

   > “That was a terrible price for ending an institution, and it had not been paid elsewhere.”

   Saint-Domingue/Haiti secured emancipation through revolution and devastating warfare, including resistance to France’s attempt to restore slavery. Britain and Russia remain valid contrasting examples; they cannot establish that emancipation through bloody war had happened nowhere else. [U.S. Department of State, “The United States and the Haitian Revolution, 1791–1804”](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1784-1800/haitian-rev).

5. **Minor — Congress still had subjects and mechanisms for bargaining.**

   > “If Congress could not close a territory to slavery, there was nothing left for Congress to bargain with.”

   Dred Scott undermined territorial restriction through ordinary legislation. It did not eliminate constitutional amendments or bargaining over other slavery protections. Crittenden’s 1860 proposals explicitly attempted a constitutional settlement after the ruling. The accurate point is that the decision narrowed the scope for legislative compromise. [U.S. Senate, “The Crittenden Compromise”](https://www.senate.gov/about/chronology/1820-1860/crittenden-compromise.htm).

6. **Minor — soil exhaustion did not make fresh land universally necessary.**

   > “Cotton wears out the soil, so a planter was always going to need fresh land.”

   Continuous cultivation could exhaust soil, but rotation and fertilization offered alternatives to acquiring fresh land. Some antebellum planters used them: the Prud’hommes in Louisiana rotated crops and fertilized fields during the 1830s. Soil depletion encouraged expansion; it did not make expansion unavoidable for every planter. [National Park Service history, “Statehood/Antebellum Period (1812–1860)”](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-I29-PURL-gpo235511/pdf/GOVPUB-I29-PURL-gpo235511.pdf).

7. **Minor — “stopped short of action” is too broad and contradicts the subsequent narrative.**

   > “Feeling against slavery ran wider than the abolitionists' own ranks, yet it stopped short of action.”

   Antislavery feeling outside abolitionist circles produced substantial political action, including opposition to the Kansas–Nebraska Act and formation of the Republican Party—events the chapter itself describes. Many Northerners stopped short of demanding immediate emancipation within Southern states, which is the narrower distinction needed here. [National Archives, Kansas–Nebraska Act](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/kansas-nebraska-act).

## Clarity flags

- **Unclear pronoun:**  
  > “A crop on that scale needed labour, and the labour was moved to them.”

  “Them” has no clear nearby referent. The crop is singular; the mills are the nearest prominent plural noun, but the labour was moved to cotton-growing lands.

- **Signposting instead of explanation:**  
  > “To Southern planters, by contrast, the West was survival, and for two reasons.”  
  > “The first lay in the ground.”  
  > “The second lay in Washington.”

  The first/second sequence is permitted by the style sheet, but these separate sentences announce explanations before giving them. The soil and Senate facts can carry the transition themselves.

- **Padding and repeated wording:**  
  > “To Southern leaders the lesson was hard to miss.”

  This announces the conclusion immediately before explaining it. “Southern leaders” then repeats two sentences later.

- **Redundant explanation:**  
  > “At the time they said the opposite, and said it with pride.”  
  > “He was naming slavery, in so many words, as the foundation of his country.”

  These bracket a speech that already makes both points unmistakably. The final sentence repeats the meaning of “cornerstone” and “natural and normal condition.”

- **Unnecessary statement of the obvious:**  
  > “The camera, too, was a machine, and it changed how the war looked from home.”

  The second clause connects the ideas; “The camera, too, was a machine” adds little for a general reader.

- **Ambiguous override:**  
  > “Congress overrode him.”

  Immediately after Johnson returns plantation land, this can suggest that Congress reversed that land decision. The following sentences concern Congressional Reconstruction more broadly. Specify what Congress overrode.

- **Unclear backward reference:**  
  > “In the end it stopped.”

  “It” means the North, and “stopped” means providing military protection, but both must be reconstructed from the preceding sentence. The next sentence already describes the consequential withdrawal.

- **Signpost before an effective ending:**  
  > “The effect can be read in the voting rolls of a single state.”

  This announces evidence that the Louisiana figures immediately provide. The figures can land without the introduction.

The new absolutes concerning fresh land, congressional bargaining and abolition elsewhere are addressed under factual flags. I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or prohibited cross-references.
