## Changed facts

No existing number, name, date or quotation has been replaced or removed. Most additions restate existing claims. These additions introduce factual content or materially broaden it; for inserted sentences, “old” identifies the preceding wording.

1. **Gold-standard definition added.**  
   **Old:** “The old gold standard had been one cause.”  
   **New:** “The old gold standard had been one cause. Under it, a country promised to exchange its money for a fixed weight of gold.”  
   This adds a useful explanation.

2. **A universal outcome added to competitive devaluation.**  
   **Old:** “Each measure was answered in kind.”  
   **New:** “Each measure was answered in kind, so that nobody gained for long and trade shrank for everyone.”  
   The consequences are new claims, not merely a transition. See factual flag 1.

3. **The operation of Keynes’s proposed institution made explicit.**  
   **Old:** “There would be a world bank for governments, issuing a currency of its own, which he called bancor.”  
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by: “Picture it as a bank in which every government kept an account.”  
   This adds an account-based explanation of the proposed clearing system.

4. **League unanimity given an explicit consequence.**  
   **Old:** “The United States had never joined, and the League could take no serious step unless its members were unanimous.”  
   **New:** “First, the United States had never joined. Second, the League could take no serious step unless its members were unanimous, so that a single objector could stop it.”  
   The additional clause reinforces the existing claim rather than changing its intended meaning.

5. **A historical generalisation about sovereignty added.**  
   **Old:** “The war had shown what a state could do to its own citizens when nothing outside it had any right to object.”  
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by: “Until then, the way a government treated its own people had been largely its own business.”  
   This adds a qualified historical claim, consistent with the existing argument.

6. **Different reasons assigned to every abstaining country.**  
   **Old:** “Eight abstained, among them the Soviet Union, which objected to the freedoms, South Africa, which objected to the equality, and Saudi Arabia, which objected to the right to change one's religion.”  
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by: “Each abstainer, in other words, objected to a different part.”  
   This extends three examples into a claim about all eight countries. See factual flag 2.

7. **“Viceroy” defined.**  
   **Old:** “Japan was occupied by an American army under a general who ruled it like a viceroy.”  
   **New:** “Japan was occupied by an American army under a general who ruled it like a viceroy, a governor ruling a distant land on a monarch's behalf.”  
   This is an explanatory addition, with the comparison still clearly figurative.

8. **NATO’s commitment strengthened into a promise to fight.**  
   **Old:** “In 1949 the United States, Canada and ten countries of western Europe signed a treaty declaring that an attack on any one of them would be treated as an attack on them all.”  
   **New:** The same sentence, followed by: “The United States, in other words, promised to fight for western Europe as if for itself.”  
   This changes the substance of the treaty commitment. See factual flag 3.

9. **Trade nondiscrimination explained.**  
   **Old:** “In 1947 twenty-three countries agreed to cut their tariffs and to give one another equal treatment.”  
   **New:** “In 1947 twenty-three countries agreed to cut their tariffs and to give one another equal treatment, which meant that a cut offered to one member was offered to all.”  
   This adds the basic most-favoured-nation principle.

10. **Prosperity extended to every home.**  
    **Old:** “Unemployment almost vanished. An ordinary family in France or Italy that had owned a bicycle in 1950 owned a car, a refrigerator and a television set in 1970.”  
    **New:** “Unemployment almost vanished. The change could be seen inside any home. An ordinary family in France or Italy that had owned a bicycle in 1950 owned a car, a refrigerator and a television set in 1970.”  
    “Any home” adds a universal claim absent from the representative-family example.

11. **A new restriction placed on other countries’ spending.**  
    **Old:** “A country that owns the world's money can buy more than it sells year after year, and run its government in debt, and the rest of the world will finance it by holding its banknotes and its bonds.”  
    **New:** The same sentence, followed by: “Other countries have to earn before they spend, whereas America, within limits, could spend first.”  
    This introduces a false distinction and contradicts the paragraph’s allowance for borrowing. See factual flag 4.

The other additions—including the voting arithmetic, Marshall Plan household comparison, multiplying dollar promises and explanation of Nixon’s suspension—restate facts already present.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — newly introduced overstatement about recovery.**

   > “Each measure was answered in kind, so that nobody gained for long and trade shrank for everyone.”

   This bundles currency depreciation together with trade restrictions and declares that none produced lasting gains. Leaving gold and the associated monetary expansion supported sustained recovery; retaliatory tariffs are a different mechanism. The sentence also pulls against the chapter’s corrected statement, “When countries abandoned it, they recovered.” The correct distinction is between recovery enabled by monetary freedom and damage caused by trade barriers. [Federal Reserve: *Money, Gold, and the Great Depression*](https://www.federalreserve.gov/boarddocs/speeches/2004/200403022/default.htm).

2. **Minor — newly introduced claim about all eight abstainers.**

   > “Each abstainer, in other words, objected to a different part.”

   The preceding sentence identifies three examples, not eight distinct positions. Six abstainers belonged to the communist group and shared substantial objections. Saudi Arabia also objected to both marriage provisions and religious conversion, rather than one unique passage. The correct fact is that objections differed **among groups of abstainers**, with considerable overlap. [UN voting record](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/670964?ln=en); [Columbia University’s account of the final debate](https://ccnmtl.columbia.edu/projects/mmt/udhr/udhr_general/drafting_history_overview_plenary_session_of_the_third_general_assembly_session_10.html).

3. **Major — newly introduced misstatement of NATO’s obligation.**

   > “The United States, in other words, promised to fight for western Europe as if for itself.”

   Article 5 obliges members to assist an attacked ally, but each chooses the action it considers necessary; armed force is a possible response, not an automatic requirement. NATO’s own explanation says the United States specifically resisted an automatic commitment to deploy armed forces. The correct fact is a promise of collective assistance, potentially including military action. [NATO: Collective defence and Article 5](https://www.nato.int/en/what-we-do/introduction-to-nato/collective-defence-and-article-5).

4. **Major — newly introduced contradiction about borrowing.**

   > “Other countries have to earn before they spend, whereas America, within limits, could spend first.”

   Other countries can borrow, attract investment or draw down reserves to finance spending beyond current earnings. The same paragraph already says they can “earn or borrow dollars.” America’s advantage concerns the international demand for its currency and liabilities, not an exclusive ability to spend before earning. This addition reintroduces the problem the earlier check’s borrowing correction had addressed. [IMF: Current Account Deficits](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/series/back-to-basics/current-account-deficits).

5. **Minor — existing chronological ambiguity retained.**

   > “He had died the previous spring, worn out by the negotiations.”

   Immediately after the summer 1947 sterling crisis, “the previous spring” ordinarily means spring 1947. Keynes died on **21 April 1946**. “In spring 1946” would give the correct chronology unambiguously. This was not flagged in the earlier check. [Fondazione Luigi Einaudi: anniversary of Keynes’s death](https://www.fondazioneeinaudi.it/en/news/80th-anniversary-of-the-death-of-john-maynard-keynes-21-april-1946).

6. **Minor — existing error about signing the declaration.**

   > “It gave people everywhere a standard, signed by their own governments, to hold those governments to.”

   Governments did not sign the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. The General Assembly adopted it by vote; it was not a treaty open to signature or ratification. The new following sentence correctly refers to governments having “voted for the words,” making the inconsistency more visible. [UN documentation guide](https://research.un.org/en/docs/humanrights/).

## Clarity flags

- **Exception-breaking absolute:**  
  > “The change could be seen inside any home.”

  “Any” unnecessarily turns a representative account of rising living standards into a claim covering every household.

- **Repeated wording and an announcement instead of information:**  
  > “The speech was short, but the sums that followed were large.”

  The opening sentence already calls it “a short speech,” and the next sentence gives the amount. This is both repetition and the kind of anticipatory signpost the style sheet prohibits.

- **Immediate repetition:**  
  > “The habit stuck. Countries that had learned to plan together went on doing so.”

  The second sentence expands the first without adding explanation. The coal-and-steel example already shows how the habit persisted.

- **Repeated setup:**  
  > “The plan had been offered to the whole continent, and the refusals traced the line that divided it.”

  The paragraph begins, “The offer had been made to all of Europe.” The second half supplies a useful consequence; the first half repeats the setup.

- **Unclear pronoun after the new analogy:**  
  > “Picture a household giving away a twentieth of one year's income, a little at a time over four years. It arrived as wheat, coal, tractors and machine tools…”

  The inserted household comparison separates “It” from the American aid it refers to. A reader briefly has to work backwards to recover the subject.

- **Weak transition that flattens a meaningful difference:**  
  > “Germany followed the same course.”

  After the detailed account of Americans writing Japan’s constitution, this can suggest that Germany’s constitution was produced in the same way. Specify that the shared course was economic recovery and alliance with America.

- **Unhelpful abstraction:**  
  > “It also contained a flaw, and the flaw lay in its arithmetic.”

  “The flaw lay in its arithmetic” announces the explanation without supplying it. The next sentences already explain the mismatch between dollar liabilities and gold.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit cross-references. Most of the added explanations fit the style sheet’s instruction to give readers room.

## Overall

The revision generally improves accessibility, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. The new claims about NATO, spending before earning, Depression-era gains and the abstainers need attention. Two older errors remain that the earlier check did not identify. No files were edited.
