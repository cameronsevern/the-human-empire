1. **Factual error and direct contradiction with chapter 77 — high confidence.**  
   > “When countries abandoned it, they did worse.”

   Leaving gold generally enabled recovery; it did not make the Depression worse. Chapter 77 explicitly says: “Countries recovered in almost exactly the order in which they gave up gold.” Distinguish the planners’ fear of competitive devaluation from what abandoning gold actually achieved. [Federal Reserve: Money, Gold, and the Great Depression](https://www.federalreserve.gov/boarddocs/speeches/2004/200403022/default.htm).

2. **False explanation of how countries obtain dollars — high confidence.**  
   > “Every other nation had to earn dollars in order to trade, by selling something that Americans wanted.”

   Countries could obtain dollars through borrowing, American investment, aid, or sales to other countries that paid in dollars. Trade also continued in other currencies. The chapter’s own Marshall Plan account supplies a counterexample. American overseas investment was a major source of dollars even while America ran a current-account surplus. [Federal Reserve history](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/bretton-woods-launched).

3. **Overstatement of American control — high confidence.**  
   > “Important decisions needed a majority of four fifths, and so nothing could be done against American wishes.”

   The conclusion does not follow. Special voting thresholds gave America blocking power over specified decisions, not every decision. Ordinary IMF decisions required a majority of votes cast. The preceding description of votes as strictly proportional to contributions also omits the basic votes allotted to each member. [Original IMF Articles, Article XII, section 5](https://www.cvce.eu/content/publication/2003/12/12/736e2f6e-fcd2-49d7-8b62-d601a3a8b839/publishable_en.pdf).

4. **False universal claim about Bretton Woods — high confidence.**  
   > “At a conference in the mountains of New Hampshire it tied every other currency to its own, and in the five years that followed it founded most of the institutions that have managed world affairs ever since.”

   Bretton Woods established rules for participating countries, not every currency on Earth. Even Canada, a founding participant, floated its currency from 1950 to 1962. The opening needs to distinguish the intended system from its geographic reach and subsequent exceptions. This also breaches the rule against exception-breaking absolutes. [Bank of Canada history](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2010/07/1950-62.pdf).

5. **Overstatement of the UN veto — high confidence.**  
   > “Each of the five could block any decision by voting no.”

   Permanent members cannot veto procedural decisions. Specify substantive Security Council decisions. [UN Charter, Article 27](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/chapter-5).

   > “It meant that the United Nations could never act against any of the five, or against a friend of any of the five.”

   This improperly extends the Security Council veto to the whole UN. During the Suez crisis, the General Assembly demanded withdrawal and established a peacekeeping force despite British and French opposition. Chapter 85 has already introduced readers to General Assembly action, so the institutional distinction matters. [UN account of the Suez response](https://www.un.org/en/node/217682).

6. **Misleading account of occupation policy — high confidence.**  
   > “The victors of 1945, or at least the Americans among them, did the opposite.”  
   > “They set out to turn their two great enemies into prosperous allies.”

   This presents a later change of policy as the original policy of 1945. The initial American occupation directive for Germany expressly prohibited steps toward economic rehabilitation except for specified occupation needs. Reconstruction and alliance-building emerged through a subsequent change in priorities. [American occupation directive, JCS 1067](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v03/d313).

7. **Misleading explanation of the dollar crisis — high confidence about the missing distinction.**  
   > “The world needed more dollars every year as trade grew, and the only way to supply them was for America to spend more abroad than it earned.”

   Following the paragraph about buying more than America sold, this invites readers to understand the problem as a necessary trade deficit. America generally still ran current-account surpluses during the 1960s; overseas investment and other capital outflows exceeded those surpluses. Explain that dollars could accumulate abroad because America lent and invested there, not simply because it imported more than it exported. [IMF historical account](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781557751966/ch003.xml).

8. **Wrong or ambiguous winter, and an exaggerated starting point — high confidence.**  
   > “The winter of 1946 was the coldest in Europe for half a century, and it fell on a continent that had not begun to recover.”

   The relevant winter was **1946–47**, with the exceptional British weather occurring principally in early 1947. “Coldest in Europe” also needs a defined geographic measure. More substantially, recovery had begun before this crisis; industrial production had already regained prewar levels in several countries. Describe a recovery interrupted or threatened, not one that had never started. [Met Office](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/case-studies/severe-winters); [Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/RL/PDF/97-62/97-62.3.pdf).

9. **False claim about American alliance history — high confidence.**  
   > “It had avoided permanent alliances since its foundation, and after the previous war it had brought its soldiers home and left Europe to itself.”

   The United States concluded an alliance with France in 1778 containing a perpetual mutual guarantee; it lasted until 1800. “Since its foundation” is therefore wrong. “Left Europe to itself” also conflicts with chapter 77’s account of American lending sustaining the European reparations-and-debt system. Distinguish military withdrawal from financial disengagement. [National Archives: Treaty of Alliance](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/treaty-of-alliance-with-france).

10. **Overgeneralisation of Black disenfranchisement — high confidence.**  
    > “The countries that voted for it included Britain and France, which ruled several hundred million colonial subjects without their consent, and the United States, where in a third of the states a Black citizen could not vote, eat in a restaurant of his choice or sit at the front of a bus.”

    Disfranchisement and segregation were extensive, but the sentence makes three different restrictions sound universal across the same states. An estimated 12% of voting-age Black southerners were registered in 1947. Say that most were prevented from voting across much of the South, and distinguish that from segregation laws and practices. [Historical voter-registration figures](https://jimalt.scholars.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum8626/files/2026-04/1994%20Alt%20-%20Impact%20of%20Voting%20Rights%20Act%20on%20Black%20and%20White%20Voter%20Registration%20in%20the%20South.pdf).

11. **False claim of historical uniqueness — high confidence.**  
    > “The arrangement gave the United States an advantage that no country had held before.”

    Britain had previously enjoyed advantages from sterling’s international role. Moreover, the dollar itself had overtaken sterling in important measures of international use during the 1920s. Bretton Woods consolidated an existing position; it did not invent international-currency privilege. [ECB review of the dollar–sterling transition](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/other-publications/ire/article/html/ecb.ireart202306_02~8c333b3f27.sl.html).

12. **Doubtful present-day majority claim — medium-high confidence.**  
    > “Oil is priced in dollars, and so are most of the goods that cross a border.”

    Dollar invoicing is dominant in many regions, but “most” global goods trade is not established by the available figures. Recent ECB estimates put both the dollar and euro at more than 40% of global export invoicing, with results sensitive to coverage and treatment of intra-European trade. “Much international trade” is defensible; a global majority needs a specific dataset and denominator. [ECB analysis of trade invoicing](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/other-publications/ire/article/html/ecb.ireart202506_02~a8e66f5ea3.el.html).

13. **Institutional conflation — high confidence.**  
    > “A committee of eighteen worked on the text for two years.”

    The Commission on Human Rights had eighteen members; the formal drafting committee had eight. These were different bodies. Identify the commission here, or describe the drafting process without merging them. [UN history of the declaration](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/udhr/history-of-the-declaration).

14. **Another exception-breaking absolute — high confidence.**  
    > “The United States had never joined, and the League could do nothing unless its members were unanimous.”

    Unanimity was the general rule, with explicit exceptions, including procedural matters decided by majority. The broad explanation of the League’s weakness is reasonable; “could do nothing” is not. [League Covenant, Article 5](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/about/league-of-nations/covenant).

15. **Reader loses the identities of the central institutions.**  
    > “Two institutions were set up to keep the system working.”

    Neither institution is subsequently named. Readers should learn that the “fund” is the **International Monetary Fund** and the “bank” is the **International Bank for Reconstruction and Development**, the original World Bank institution. These names directly serve the chapter’s promise to explain institutions that still shape the world.

16. **Missing explanation connecting this account to chapter 77.**  
    > “The dollar alone would be fixed to gold.”

    Chapter 77 has told readers that Roosevelt took the dollar off gold. Explain the distinction between ordinary people’s former right to redeem dollars and the later international gold-conversion arrangement for foreign monetary authorities. Without that bridge, this sounds like an unexplained reversal. [Federal Reserve account of Bretton Woods](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/bretton-woods-launched).

17. **The monetary mechanism is asserted rather than explained.**  
    > “Every country would fix the value of its currency against the dollar, and keep it there.”

    A general reader needs one concrete transaction: when a currency weakened, its central bank sold dollars from its reserves and bought its own currency. That would explain why reserves mattered, how they could run out, and why the fund supplied loans. It would also prepare readers for the sterling crisis rather than introducing “reserves drained away” as an unexplained event.

18. **Unsupported unanimity, not objectionable confidence.**  
    > “Keynes had the better arguments, and everyone present knew it.”

    “Keynes had the better arguments” is an editorial judgment the style permits. “Everyone present knew it” asserts universal private agreement, including among advocates of the American plan. Remove that second claim unless there is extraordinary evidence for it.

19. **An absolute contradicted by ordinary commercial practice.**  
    > “It had kept currencies stable, and trade needs stable currencies, since no merchant will sign a contract if he cannot tell what the money will be worth when the goods arrive.”

    Merchants routinely contract despite exchange-rate uncertainty. Stability makes trade easier and reduces risk; it is not a prerequisite for any contract. “No merchant” breaches the explicit style rule against absolutes that one exception breaks.
