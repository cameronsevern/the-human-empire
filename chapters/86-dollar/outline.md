# Chapter 86 — Why does the world use the American dollar?

**Line under the title:** The United States and the world, from 1944 to 1971.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

The world uses the dollar because the rules of the world economy were written in 1944 and 1945, when the United States held most of the world's gold, made half its goods, and was the only great power whose cities were intact. At a hotel in New Hampshire it tied every other currency to its own. In the next five years it founded, housed and largely paid for the institutions that still run world affairs: a bank and a fund for the world economy, the United Nations, a system of trade rules, and a network of alliances and bases. It did so out of calculation and out of a real determination not to repeat the 1930s, and the result was the longest boom in history for those inside it. The gold link ended in 1971. The dollar stayed, because by then everyone else was already using it.

## The story

1. **Opening: a hotel in the mountains.** Answer first. July 1944: seven hundred delegates, an Englishman with a weak heart and an American who held the cards.
2. **Never again.** What the planners had learned from the Depression. The two rival designs: a world currency, or the dollar.
3. **Thirty-five dollars an ounce.** What was agreed, how it worked, and who had the votes. A loan to Britain repaid in 2006.
4. **Five vetoes.** The United Nations: what was copied from the old League and what was changed. A site by the river.
5. **All human beings.** The declaration of 1948: who wrote it, one word changed by a delegate from India, and who could enforce it. A petition from Black Americans.
6. **Five per cent.** The winter of 1947 and the plan to rebuild Europe. Stalin's refusal. Coal and steel: the beginning of a European union.
7. **A constitution in a week.** Japan and Germany remade as allies.
8. **Bases.** Alliances and garrisons; trade rules. Who was not in the room in 1944.
9. **Thirty years.** The long boom, and what the dollar's position gave America.
10. **Ending: a Sunday broadcast.** August 1971, and why the dollar stayed.

## Left for later

The Cold War itself. The end of the empires. The oil shock, inflation and the return of the market. China's entry into world trade. Sanctions in the 2020s.

## To verify

- The conference: place, dates, delegates and countries, the state of the hotel, Keynes's health, the American negotiator and his later fate. American share of world gold and manufacturing in 1944 to 1945.
- Keynes's plan and its world currency; what was adopted: the pegs, the gold price, the fund and the bank, the American voting share. The Soviet Union's attendance and non-ratification. African and Asian states present.
- The British loan: amount, interest, the convertibility condition and the crisis of 1947, the final payment.
- The United Nations: the founding conference (place, dates, countries), the council and the veto, the site in New York and who paid for it.
- The declaration: date, place, vote and abstentions; the chair; the drafters; the change to "all human beings" and who made it; its legal status. The 1947 petition to the United Nations and its author.
- The European plan: the speech (date, place), the amount, the years, the number of countries, its share of American output; Communist votes in France and Italy; Czechoslovakia's withdrawal. The coal and steel community (year, members); the treaty of 1957.
- Japan: the drafting of the constitution in 1946 (how long, by whom), the clause renouncing war, the young woman who wrote the equality clause.
- The alliance of 1949 and its central promise. The trade agreement of 1947 (countries). American bases abroad.
- Growth 1950 to 1973. The "exorbitant privilege" remark and who made it. The cost of printing a banknote. 15 August 1971: the broadcast. The dollar's share of world reserves and of currency trades today; the share of banknotes held abroad.
