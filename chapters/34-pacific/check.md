I found no clear contradiction with chapters 2 or 33, and no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit cross-references. The findings below are ordered by seriousness. No files were edited.

1. **Factual overstatement: settlement did not immediately end long-distance voyaging.**  
   > “Once they were settled, the long voyages stopped.”

   Archaeological evidence supports continued voyages between distant archipelagos after settlement. A Hawaiian stone adze found in the Tuamotus is a concrete counterexample. Distinguish initial settlement, subsequent exchange and eventual isolation; they were separate stages. **Confidence: high.** [Research team’s account](https://news.uq.edu.au/2007-09-24-uq-research-solves-mystery-ancient-hawaiian-voyaging)

2. **Doubtful inference: contact with Americans does not establish this particular round trip.**  
   > “The sweet potato is an American plant, and Polynesians were growing it before any European reached the Pacific, because some of them had sailed all the way to South America and come back.”

   Pre-European contact is well supported, but the direction and number of voyages remain unresolved. The 2024 genetic study explicitly distinguishes evidence of contact from evidence identifying who travelled where. State the contact confidently without treating this itinerary as demonstrated. **Confidence: high about the evidential limitation.** [Original genetic study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07881-4)

3. **Factual overstatement: the loss of reading knowledge cannot be assigned wholly to the raids.**  
   > “The men who could read the boards were taken in 1862.”

   People claiming instruction in rongorongo survived: Metoro supplied readings to Bishop Jaussen after the raids, and Ure Va‘e Iko was interviewed in 1886. Their readings are problematic, but their existence prevents this clean account of every reader disappearing in 1862. Describe the destruction and eventual loss of the tradition without claiming that all its readers were taken together. **Confidence: high that the sentence is too categorical; surviving informants’ actual literacy is uncertain.** [Specialist discussion](https://academic.oup.com/book/58672/chapter/485388496), [records of Metoro’s readings](https://www.kohaumotu.org/rongorongo_org/metoro/index.html)

4. **Missing historical cause: the count of 111 is presented as though everyone else had died.**  
   > “In 1877 a visitor counted the people of Rapa Nui.”  
   > “There were 111.”

   The count concerns residents remaining on the island. The jump directly from slave raids and smallpox omits subsequent coercion, plantation recruitment and substantial departures to Tahiti and Mangareva. One sentence explaining those departures would prevent readers from mistaking the resident count for the entire surviving Rapanui population. **Confidence: high.** [Study of the Rapanui diaspora](https://www.academia.edu/20156041/_2015_The_Rapanui_diaspora_in_Tahiti_and_the_lands_of_Pamatai_1871_1970_)

5. **Doubtful inference: the tablet’s wood does not date its inscription.**  
   > “One of the boards is cut from wood that grew in the 1400s, so the script may well be older than any visit from outside, and if the islanders invented it themselves it is one of the very few times that writing has been invented from nothing.”

   The dated wood falls around the late fifteenth/early sixteenth century, but it could have been inscribed later. The study expressly acknowledges this limitation. The independent-invention possibility is legitimate; the missing distinction between the wood’s age and the carving’s age makes the reasoning misleading. **Confidence: high.** [Original radiocarbon study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-53063-7)

6. **Evidence overstated: a supported reconstruction becomes complete knowledge.**  
   > “They were right, and almost everything about how it was done is now known.”  
   > “They were describing what happened.”

   Polynesian authorship is secure. Upright “walking” has substantial experimental and archaeological support, including further work published in 2025, but that does not establish almost every construction detail or directly demonstrate the transport history of every statue. Keep the explanation and the judgement that walking best fits the evidence; distinguish that judgement from an observed historical fact. **Confidence: high about that distinction.** [Lipo and Hunt’s 2025 study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440325002328)

7. **Unsupported universal claim and invented explanation for missing animals.**  
   > “Every island received the same kit, and every island killed off part of it.”  
   > “Neither pigs nor dogs survived the voyage or the first years.”

   A shared agricultural tradition does not establish identical cargoes on every voyage. Rapa Nui lacks pre-European archaeological evidence of pigs and dogs; that does not establish that settlers carried them and they died en route or shortly afterwards. Say they were absent, leaving the cause unstated. The first sentence also breaks the rule against absolutes vulnerable to an exception. **Confidence: high.** [Review of animal evidence and Polynesian interaction](https://doi.org/10.1002/arco.5321)

8. **Wrong population comparison at Hōkūleʻa’s arrival.**  
   > “He brought the canoe to Tahiti in about a month, and more than half the population of the island came down to the harbour to meet it.”

   The commonly reported crowd is about 17,000. Tahiti already had approximately 79,500 residents in 1971 and 95,600 in 1977, so that was nowhere near half the island. The “half” claim appears even in the voyaging society’s account, but conflicts with census figures. Retain the crowd estimate and remove the fraction. **Confidence: high.** [Voyaging society account](https://worldwidevoyage.hokulea.com/voyages/our-story/), [census table reproduced in a government research report](https://infoterre.brgm.fr/rapports/RR-41041-FR.pdf)

9. **Misdescription of the farming study’s estimate.**  
   > “In 2024 a survey of these rock gardens from satellite images found enough of them to feed about three thousand people, which is roughly the number Roggeveen saw.”

   The paper’s average estimate for rock gardening alone was about **2,000** people. Its approximately **3,000** total included an allowance for marine food and food from other cultivated areas. The chapter’s overall demographic argument survives, but the sentence wrongly attributes the whole food supply to the mapped gardens. **Confidence: high.** [Original study, discussion](https://obtlab.climate.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/Publications%20%28PDFs%29/Davis_etal_SciAdv_2024.pdf)

10. **Incorrect universal astronomy claim.**  
    > “Every star rises at the same point on the eastern horizon night after night, and sets at the same point in the west.”

    Some stars remain above the horizon; others never rise at a given location. Rising and setting bearings also change with the observer’s latitude—relevant when describing a long voyage. Limit the statement to suitable rising and setting stars viewed from roughly the same latitude. This is also an exception-breaking absolute under `style.md`. **Confidence: high.** [Polynesian Voyaging Society’s explanation](https://worldwidevoyage.hokulea.com/education-at-sea/polynesian-navigation/polynesian-non-instrument-wayfinding/the-celestial-sphere/)

11. **Over-simple explanation of eastward exploration.**  
    > “A canoe that went looking for new land sailed eastward, into the wind, which was slow and hard work.”  
    > “That is why Polynesia was settled from west to east, against the weather.”

    Exploring against the *prevailing* winds offered a safer return, but explorers could exploit temporary westerlies to travel east with favourable winds. Climate reconstructions also identify favourable sailing windows. Distinguish the prevailing wind pattern from the weather during a particular voyage; the present wording implies continuous upwind sailing and makes one explanation do too much. **Confidence: high that the distinction is needed; particular prehistoric routes remain reconstructed.** [Research on voyaging climate windows](https://www.stevenphipps.com/publications/goodwin2014_pnas.pdf)

12. **Overstatement of scholarly consensus.**  
    > “In the 1950s the standard view was that the islands had been settled by fishermen blown off course in storms.”

    Andrew Sharp’s accidental-voyaging argument was influential, but controversial, and encompassed more than fishing trips. Contemporary responses show a debate, not a single settled position. “An influential theory” would preserve the point accurately. **Confidence: high.** [Contemporary 1956 review](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/periodicals/NZLIST19560622.2.24.1), [1964 discussion of the controversy](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/periodicals/TAH196403.2.28.6)

13. **Internal inconsistency: the opening appears to move every statue.**  
    > “They cut nearly a thousand of them out of the side of one volcano with tools of stone, and they moved them across the island without wheels, animals or metal.”

    “Them” makes the nearly thousand statues the objects of both actions. Later, the chapter correctly says about four hundred remain at the quarry, including unfinished examples. Separate the total carved from the subset transported. **Confidence: high.** [Archaeological team’s overview](https://news.arizona.edu/news/easter-island-your-computer-u-archaeologist-helps-create-digital-3d-model-view-iconic-statues)

14. **Unnecessary precision in the American-contact date.**  
    > “About a tenth of the islanders' ancestry was Native American, and it had entered the family tree in the 1300s, long before Europeans crossed the Pacific.”

    The study estimated **1250–1430**, not exclusively the 1300s. “Around 1300” would be a fair simplification; “in the 1300s” excludes parts of the supported interval. **Confidence: high.** [Original genetic study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07881-4)

15. **Reader-orientation gap: the narrative returns to 1722 without marking the return.**  
    > “Other ships followed over the next century, and they brought the diseases of the crowded continents to people who had never met them.”

    The preceding section has already reached Cook in 1774; this section resumes with Roggeveen. “The next century” therefore requires readers to reset the chronology themselves. Anchor this to “the century after Roggeveen’s visit,” particularly because the distinction between pre-contact and post-contact disaster carries the argument.

16. **Reader-comprehension gap: an apparently waterless island supports thousands of people.**  
    > “It has no permanent streams and no coral reef with a quiet lagoon full of fish.”

    Having removed the obvious freshwater supply, the chapter never explains what people drank. A brief explanation of freshwater sources would close a consequential gap in the account of settlement and survival. Research identifies coastal groundwater discharge as an important resource. [Archaeological study of freshwater and monument locations](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0210409)

17. **Explicit signpost sentences prohibited by `style.md`.**  
    > “The answer begins far to the west.”  
    > “Something else happened on the farthest islands.”

    Both announce information that the following sentence supplies. Start directly with the ancestors’ movement from the west, and with the eventual weakening of distant voyaging connections. These are specific structural violations, not objections to the narrator’s confidence.
