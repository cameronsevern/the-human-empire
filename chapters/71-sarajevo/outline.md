# Chapter 71 — Why did the First World War start?

**Line under the title:** Europe, from 1871 to the end of 1914.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

The war began because the great powers of Europe had tied themselves into two armed camps, and each had a plan that only worked if it moved first. When a Bosnian Serb student shot the heir to the Austrian throne, the governments in Vienna and Berlin decided to risk a general war in order to crush Serbia, and the government in Russia decided to risk one in order to protect it. Each expected the other side to back down, or the fighting to be short. Railway timetables and treaties did the rest in five weeks. No government wanted the war that it got, and every government preferred war to giving way. Millions of men reported when called, because they believed their country had been attacked. By the end of the year a line of trenches ran from Switzerland to the sea.

## The story

1. **Opening: a wrong turn.** Answer first. Sarajevo, 28 June 1914: a bomb that misses, a driver who takes the wrong street, a student with a pistol.
2. **Two camps.** Europe in 1914: five great powers, two alliances, and what each was afraid of. Germany between France and Russia. The naval race.
3. **Eleven nations under one crown.** Austria-Hungary and Serbia. Why Vienna thought its survival was at stake.
4. **The timetable.** Armies of millions moved by rail. Germany's plan to beat France in six weeks, through Belgium, before Russia was ready. Why calling up the army meant war.
5. **The blank cheque.** July: Berlin's promise, Vienna's ultimatum, Serbia's reply.
6. **Willy and Nicky.** The last week: two cousins exchange telegrams, the armies are called up, Belgium is invaded, Britain comes in. The lamps.
7. **Why they went.** Crowds, volunteers, socialists who voted for the war. What men thought they were fighting for.
8. **Before the leaves fall.** August and September: what machine guns and artillery did to armies in the open. The bloodiest day in French history. The Marne. The trenches.
9. **Ending: whose fault.** How historians divide the blame, and the line from Switzerland to the sea.
