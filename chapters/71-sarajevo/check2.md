## Factual flags

1. **Major — newly introduced universal claim about military planning.**

   > “The result was slaughter on a scale that nobody had planned for.”

   This makes all military planning assume something less destructive. German planners explicitly anticipated a devastating mass war; Moltke’s expectation of attrition influenced demands for increased manpower and preparations for a prolonged conflict. Inadequate preparation is not the same as nobody planning for mass slaughter. The defensible distinction is between anticipating enormous losses and successfully preparing for their consequences. [Oliver Stein, “Pre-war Military Planning (Germany)”](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/pdf/1914-1918-Online-pre-war_military_planning_germany-2021-03-03.pdf).

2. **Minor — the defensive advantage did not suddenly originate after the Marne.**

   > “Defence had suddenly become far stronger than attack.”

   In context, this places the change after the Germans began digging in September. Modern defensive firepower and substantial trench systems had already demonstrated their strength in earlier wars, particularly the Russo-Japanese War. What developed in autumn 1914 was a continuous entrenched Western Front. This also sits awkwardly with the chapter’s earlier explanation that exposed attackers could not survive modern fire. [“The Rocky Road to the Great War: The Evolution of Trench Warfare to 1914”](https://doi.org/10.2307/j.ctt1ddr7hs.10).

3. **Minor — unchanged caption dates the start of the naval race too late.**

   > “HMS Dreadnought, the British battleship of 1906 whose all-big-gun design set off the naval race with Germany, at sea about 1906.”

   The Anglo-German naval race was already under way before 1906. *Dreadnought* changed and intensified it, initiating its dreadnought-building phase. [National WWI Museum and Memorial, “What Caused World War I?” teaching materials](https://www.theworldwar.org/sites/default/files/2024-07/2024-07-24-focused-inquiry-origins-wwi.pdf).

4. **Minor — unchanged ammunition detail gives the wrong standard material.**

   > “A modern field gun could put fifteen shells a minute onto a target several kilometres away, and the commonest kind of shell burst in the air and sprayed the ground with steel balls.”

   Standard shrapnel ammunition of the kind described contained lead or hardened lead-alloy balls, not characteristically steel balls. “Metal balls” would preserve the explanation without introducing another technical detail. This is separate from the earlier, now-fixed claim that *every* shell worked this way. [US Army Center of Military History, “Shrapnel and Shell Fragments”](https://history.army.mil/Research/Frequently-Asked-Questions/Shrapnel-and-Shell-Fragments/).

5. **Minor — the Marne passage misdates the new army and merges different commanders’ actions.**

   > “In the first week of September, with the Germans in sight of Paris, the French commander scraped together a new army, some of it rushed to the front in the taxis of the capital.”

   > “He struck the exhausted invaders in the flank along the river Marne.”

   The Sixth Army was already under Maunoury on **26 August**. Joffre directed the general counter-offensive; Gallieni organised the taxi reinforcement; Maunoury commanded the Sixth Army’s flank attack. The prose turns these into one unnamed commander’s consecutive actions and implies September formation. The taxi troops reinforced an existing army during the battle. [French defence archives, Sixth Army](https://www.servicehistorique.sga.defense.gouv.fr/ark/1126653); [Musée de l’Armée, “Marne taxi”](https://www.musee-armee.fr/en/collections/museum-treasures/marne-taxi.html).
