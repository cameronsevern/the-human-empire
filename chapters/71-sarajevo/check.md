Read the requested files; no files changed. Findings are ranked by seriousness. Confidence refers to the correction, not merely to whether the wording could be improved.

1. **The central explanation makes war too automatic.**
   > “The war started because the great powers of Europe had bound themselves into two armed alliances, and each had a war plan that worked only if it struck first.”
   >
   > “Neither of them was in control of it.”

   **Misleading; high confidence.** The powers did not all have plans dependent on striking first. Britain’s understandings were not a binding military alliance, as the chapter later correctly explains. Military plans constrained choices, especially Germany’s, but governments still chose whether to mobilise, declare war and invade. The second sentence also conflicts with the subsequent account of Nicholas cancelling and then authorising mobilisation. Preserve the argument about dangerous plans without making political agency disappear. Sources: [Hew Strachan on British planning](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/ams/959721/pdf/1914-1918-Online-pre-war_military_planning_great_britain-2018-02-07.pdf), [Annika Mombauer on German planning](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/pre-war-military-planning-germany/?format=pdf).

2. **The final allocation of responsibility gets the sequence wrong.**
   > “Austria-Hungary chose to start a war, and Germany encouraged it and was the first to attack its neighbours.”

   **Wrong as written; high confidence.** Austria-Hungary had already attacked Serbia before Germany attacked its neighbours. The chapter itself describes Austrian gunboats shelling Belgrade on 29 July. Germany widened the war through its declarations and invasions; it did not launch the first interstate attack of the crisis. Source: [Samuel Williamson, “The Way to War”](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/the-way-to-war/?format=pdf).

3. **“Mobilise” needs the crucial qualification “general”.**
   > “Russia was the first great power to mobilise.”

   **Wrong; high confidence.** Austria-Hungary ordered partial mobilisation against Serbia on 25 July. Russia was the first great power to order **general mobilisation**, on 30 July. Without that distinction, the sentence misleadingly shifts the starting point of military escalation. Source: [Austrian State Archives, including the original mobilisation order](https://wk1.staatsarchiv.at/diplomatie-zwischen-krieg-und-frieden/allgemeine-mobilisierung-1914/).

4. **The generals’ supposed ignorance conflicts with the earlier Japanese story.**
   > “They met weapons that their generals had seen used only against colonial peoples.”

   **Wrong; high confidence.** Machine guns and modern artillery had been used in the Russo-Japanese War, which European military observers studied. Chapter 66 already describes Japan fighting Russia in “enormous and bloody battles”: that supplies an important counterexample to the implied progression from colonial slaughter straight to European discovery. The problem was how armies interpreted and applied existing evidence, not an absence of evidence. Source: [*Building the Old Contemptibles*, US Army Command and General Staff College research](https://cgsc.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/api/collection/p4013coll2/id/354/download). This also breaks the prohibition on exception-fragile “only” claims.

5. **The assassination’s central coincidence contains a myth and an incorrect superlative.**
   > “It had stopped in front of a delicatessen, and standing on the pavement outside, a metre and a half away, was the youngest of the conspirators, a nineteen-year-old named Gavrilo Princip, who had given up and gone to buy a sandwich.”

   **Unsupported sandwich story; incorrect “youngest”; high confidence.** There is no reliable contemporary evidence for the sandwich or the associated claim that Princip had abandoned the attempt. Vaso Čubrilović was seventeen, younger than Princip. Keep Princip’s presence near the shop without inventing his lunch or state of mind. Sources: [Mike Dash’s investigation of the sandwich story](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/gavrilo-princips-sandwich-79480741/), [Annika Mombauer, “July Crisis 1914”](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/july-crisis-1914-1-1/?format=pdf).

6. **The car did have reverse.**
   > “The driver braked and began to push the car backwards, since it had no reverse gear.”

   **Wrong; high confidence.** The absence of reverse gear is another circulating myth. Accounts describe the driver attempting to reverse; the sentence additionally leaves the reader picturing him getting out and pushing the vehicle. Sources: [historian Sean Munger’s correction](https://www.gardenofmemory.net/a-tragedy-i-know-too-well-the-assassination-of-franz-ferdinand-110-years-on/), [Margaret MacMillan’s account](https://time.com/3880415/margaret-macmillan-on-assassination-of-franz-ferdinand-and-sophie/).

7. **Germany did not have Europe’s largest army.**
   > “By 1914 it had sixty-five million people, the largest army in Europe, and an industry that produced more steel than Britain, France and Russia put together.”

   **Wrong on army size; high confidence.** Russia’s peacetime army numbered roughly 1.4 million, against Germany’s roughly 840,000. “Largest” cannot substitute for an argument about organisation or effectiveness. Sources: NZ History’s [Russian](https://nzhistory.govt.nz/war/russian-empire-facts) and [German](https://nzhistory.govt.nz/war/germany-facts) military figures.

8. **The western deployment is confused with the Belgian sweep.**
   > “Seven eighths of the German army would wheel through neutral Belgium, sweep down behind Paris, and pin the French armies against their own forts.”

   **Misleading; high confidence.** Concentrating most German forces against France did not mean sending all those forces through Belgium. Significant forces remained opposite the French frontier in Alsace-Lorraine. The sentence also blends the earlier Schlieffen conception with its modified execution in 1914. Distinguish the western concentration from the flanking force. Source: [NZ History’s map and explanation of the plan and invasion](https://nzhistory.govt.nz/media/photo/schlieffen-plan-and-german-invasion-1914).

9. **Serbia’s reservation is described too broadly.**
   > “It accepted nearly everything, humbly, and declined only the point about Austrian officials operating on its soil.”

   **Misleading; high confidence.** Serbia rejected Austrian participation in its judicial investigation, while responding conditionally to the separate demand for collaboration in suppressing subversion. Those were distinct provisions, not a blanket refusal to allow Austrian officials to operate in Serbia. Other acceptances also carried qualifications. The favourable judgment of Serbia’s diplomacy can stand; the substance needs precision. Sources: [the Serbian reply](https://alphahistory.com/worldwar1/serbias-response-austro-hungarian-ultimatum-1914/), [Council on Foreign Relations analysis](https://www.cfr.org/articles/twe-remembers-serbia-responds-austrias-ultimatum).

10. **The wedding anniversary is wrong.**
    > “It was a Sunday, and their wedding anniversary.”

    **Wrong; high confidence.** They married on **1 July 1900**, not 28 June. The Sunday is correct. Source: [Historical Association, *The Historian*, issue 120](https://www.history.org.uk/files/download/13482/1391606104/Historian_120.pdf).

11. **Expectation of a short war is overstated.**
    > “Nearly everyone expected it to be short.”

    **Doubtful; moderate-to-high confidence.** Popular hopes of a short war were widespread, but important military leaders anticipated a protracted conflict. German planning itself included consideration of a long war. Distinguish hoping for a quick victory from confidently expecting one; “nearly everyone” effaces a disagreement that matters to the chapter’s causal argument. Sources: [Matthew Seligmann, “Pre-war Military Planning”](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/pdf/1914-1918-Online-pre-war_military_planning-2019-06-26.pdf), [Dennis Showalter, “On the Road to Modern War”](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/pdf/1914-1918-Online-on_the_road_to_modern_war-2014-10-08.pdf).

12. **Austria-Hungary shared more than an emperor.**
    > “It had fifty million people who spoke eleven main languages, among them Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Croats and Serbs, and all they had in common was an emperor, Franz Joseph, who was eighty-three and had been on the throne since 1848.”

    **Wrong absolute; high confidence.** The monarchy also had shared institutions, including a common army and foreign service, and a common currency. Its ethnic diversity does not establish that the emperor was its sole connection. Grammatically, the examples following “languages” are peoples rather than languages. Sources: [the common army](https://ww1.habsburger.net/en/chapters/army-austria-hungary-its-entirety), [the monetary union](https://historyandpolicy.org/policy-papers/papers/a-stable-currency-in-search-of-a-stable-empire-the-austro-hungarian-experie/). This is precisely the kind of absolute `style.md` prohibits.

13. **The description makes every artillery shell a shrapnel shell.**
    > “A modern field gun could put twenty shells a minute onto a target several kilometres away, and each shell burst in the air and sprayed the ground with steel balls.”

    **Wrong generalisation; high confidence.** That describes shrapnel ammunition, not every shell. High-explosive shells killed through blast and fragments of their casing. Specify the ammunition being described; the distinction also helps explain why digging in changed the fighting. Source: [US Army Center of Military History, “Shrapnel and Shell Fragments”](https://history.army.mil/Research/Frequently-Asked-Questions/Shrapnel-and-Shell-Fragments/).

14. **The ending silently makes the Western Front stand for the whole war.**
    > “The war of movement was over, and about a million men were already dead.”

    **Misleading scope; high confidence.** Movement had largely stopped on the Western Front; major mobile campaigns continued elsewhere, especially in the east. Give the geographical limit. Also identify whether the death estimate covers all fronts and which endpoint it uses: the preceding sentence says November, whereas the outline anticipates end-of-year losses. Source for the geographical distinction: [Showalter, “On the Road to Modern War”](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/pdf/1914-1918-Online-on_the_road_to_modern_war-2014-10-08.pdf).

15. **Italy enters the explanation and then disappears.**
    > “Germany was allied with Austria-Hungary, and less reliably with Italy.”

    **Reader-orientation gap.** A reader following the two-camp explanation needs to know that Italy remained neutral in 1914. “Less reliably” does not explain its absence from the subsequent war. One short statement would also demonstrate that alliances did not automatically compel participation. Source: [National WWI Museum, “Italy Enters World War I”](https://www.theworldwar.org/learn/about-wwi/italy-enters-world-war-i).

16. **The relationship between Serbia, Bosnia and the Slavs needs one more step.**
    > “Serbia was a small, poor kingdom of peasants, which had won its independence from the Turks and dreamed of uniting all the South Slavs in one state.”

    **Reader-orientation gap / unexplained term.** “South Slavs” arrives without explanation, although it carries the argument connecting Serbia’s ambitions, Bosnia’s population and Russia’s intervention. Briefly distinguish Serbia, the independent country, from Serbs living inside Austria-Hungary, and explain that South Slavs included other related peoples, such as Croats and Slovenes. The later statement that the assassins were Bosnian subjects helps, but comes after the reader first needs the distinction.

17. **“Lost” invites a serious misunderstanding of the casualty figure.**
    > “By the end of the month the French had lost a quarter of a million men and were in full retreat.”

    **Reader-orientation gap.** Immediately after twenty-seven thousand soldiers “were killed”, readers can reasonably interpret this as another death toll. State explicitly whether the larger figure includes wounded, missing and prisoners, and make its reporting period clear. Military shorthand obscures the distinction the general reader needs.

18. **The timetable section opens with an explicit signpost.**
    > “Before following the diplomats, it is necessary to know what was in the generals’ desks, because their plans left the diplomats very little time.”

    **Clear style breach.** This announces the order of exposition rather than telling the story, contrary to “No signpost sentences”. The substantive point—that military plans compressed diplomatic time—can be stated directly.
