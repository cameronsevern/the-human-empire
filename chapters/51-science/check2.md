## Factual flags

1. **Major — the new Kepler bridge misrepresents earlier astronomy.**
   
   > “The measurement had been allowed to overrule the theory, where for centuries it had been the other way round.”

   Earlier astronomers did revise their accounts in response to observations. Even Tycho, immediately before Kepler, used measurements of the 1572 nova and 1577 comet to challenge established doctrines about the heavens and their solid spheres. The chapter also describes Ibn al-Haytham testing claims experimentally centuries earlier.

   **Correct fact:** Kepler’s particular achievement was abandoning circular planetary paths because his model failed against sufficiently precise observations. Measurement overruling an established explanation was not new. [Museo Galileo’s account of Tycho’s observations and conclusions](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/biography/TychoBrahe.html)

2. **Minor — lacking longitude did not make distance from land unknowable.**
   
   > “Longitude is a ship's position to the east or west, and a captain who does not know it cannot tell how far he is from land.”

   Captains could estimate their position through speed and heading, measure latitude, recognise coastal landmarks, and take depth soundings. Depending on the coastline and route, these could establish proximity to land without an independently measured longitude.

   **Correct fact:** Without reliable longitude, a captain could be dangerously uncertain about his east–west position and the distance remaining to a destination. The problem was unreliable positioning, not complete inability to judge distance from land. [Royal Museums Greenwich, traditional navigation methods](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/maritime-history/traditional-ways-determine-ships-position)

3. **Minor — twice-daily tides are not universal.**
   
   > “The same force bends the planets into Kepler's ellipses, it keeps Jupiter's moons circling Jupiter, and it raises the tides twice a day.”

   The tidal frequency claim was already present before this revision. Some coasts experience one high tide and one low tide per day; others have two, with varying heights.

   **Correct fact:** Gravity produces tides, but their local frequency and height depend on the astronomical configuration and the geography of ocean basins. “Raises the tides” preserves the point without the incorrect universal frequency. [NOAA, tidal patterns](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/high-tide.html)

## Clarity flags

1. **New signposting**
   
   > “A hard test of these habits came from an unlikely quarter.”

   Announces the demonstration before telling it, contrary to “No signpost sentences.” The cloth merchant’s introduction already supplies the surprising contrast.

2. **New padding around the checking process**
   
   > “Some way was needed of telling a discovery from a tall story.”
   
   > “Instead, they treated the letter as a claim to be checked.”
   
   > “The check took time.”

   The first repeats the paragraph’s opening question. The second previews the immediately following requests for details and replication. The third previews “After several failed attempts.” These additions explain what adjacent sentences already make clear.

3. **Repeated payment point**
   
   > “The telescope had paid its way from the start.”
   
   > “Two hours' warning of an approaching ship was worth paying for.”

   Both frame the same demonstration and salary increase. The final sentence repeats the practical benefit and financial reward already established.

4. **Vague transition that diminishes the preceding account**
   
   > “Numbers of that quality were of little use until somebody did something with them.”

   “Somebody did something” supplies no explanatory content. It also leaves readers wondering why Tycho’s measurements had little use after the chapter has stressed their exceptional quality. The useful transition is from collecting observations to calculating Mars’s path.

5. **Unclear pronoun after the added borrowing discussion**
   
   > “Two men may arrive at the same device independently. They are far less likely to label it in the same way. The borrowing did not stop there, either. His model for the motion of the Moon matches one worked out in Damascus around 1350.”

   “His” means Copernicus, but the intervening “two men” makes the reference less immediate. Naming Copernicus here would restore clarity.

6. **An ambiguous ordinary word**
   
   > “Even then the book went out with an apology attached.”

   A general reader may understand an expression of regret. The following sentence describes a disclaimer about the model’s truth, not an apology in that everyday sense.

7. **Brittle absolutes remain**
   
   > “Every educated person had been taught that the Earth sat still at the centre of the universe and that everything in the sky revolved around it.”
   
   > “His banned dialogue was translated into Latin and read everywhere north of the Alps.”

   “Every educated person” has an unnecessarily worldwide scope in a chapter that acknowledges multiple intellectual traditions. “Everywhere north of the Alps” turns widespread circulation into universal readership. Neither absolute is needed for the argument.

8. **An unexplained scale comparison**
   
   > “In 1676 he wrote to the Royal Society, in Dutch, that he had looked at a drop of rainwater and found it full of living creatures, thousands of times smaller than anything visible to the eye.”

   “Thousands of times smaller” does not identify whether it compares length, area, or volume. Those imply very different sizes. The reader cannot form a reliable picture from this comparison.
