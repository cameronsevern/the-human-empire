## Changed facts

No names, numerical figures, dates or quotations were changed or removed. However, the revision adds factual and causal claims beyond changes to flow. The substantive changes are below; simple restatements of facts already present are excluded.

1. **Salt monopoly becomes exclusive manufacture and sale.**

   **Old:** “The British government of India held a monopoly on salt.”

   **New:** “The British government of India held a monopoly on salt, which meant that the right to make and sell it belonged to the government alone.”

   The explanation introduces an incorrect claim about who could legally manufacture and sell salt.

2. **The population ratio acquires a misleading arithmetic connection.**

   **Old:** “In all of India there was roughly one Briton for every two or three thousand Indians.”

   **New:** “Taken together, in all of India there was roughly one Briton for every two or three thousand Indians.”

   “Taken together” now implies that the preceding thousand officials and sixty thousand soldiers produce this ratio. They do not: against three hundred million Indians, they produce approximately one to five thousand. The original ratio requires a larger British population, including civilians.

3. **Limited popular reach becomes inability to reach ordinary Indians.**

   **Old:** “It remained a movement of the educated, in suits, and most Indians had never heard of it.”

   **New:** “Even so, it remained a movement of the educated, in suits, and most Indians had never heard of it. As a result, it could not touch the millions of ordinary Indians on whom British rule actually rested.”

   The added sentence strengthens limited reach into an assertion of incapacity.

4. **The train episode’s timing changes slightly.**

   **Old:** “A week after he landed, he was travelling by night train with a first-class ticket when a white passenger objected to sharing a compartment with him.”

   **New:** “Within a week of landing, he learned how South Africa treated a man of his colour. He was travelling by night train with a first-class ticket when a white passenger objected to sharing a compartment with him.”

   “A week after” becomes “within a week,” and the time marker now attaches to his discovery of discrimination. Gandhi’s own account places departure from Durban on the seventh or eighth day after arrival; “about a week” would preserve that account more precisely. [Gandhi’s autobiography](https://www.mkgandhi.org/autobio/chap33.php)

5. **Dyer’s emotional response is newly asserted.**

   **Old:** “The general told the inquiry that he had gone there to produce a moral effect, and that he would have used his machine guns if the lane had been wide enough for the armoured cars.”

   **New:** “The general showed no regret afterwards. He told the inquiry that he had gone there to produce a moral effect, and that he would have used his machine guns if the lane had been wide enough for the armoured cars.”

   This adds a claim about his subsequent attitude, rather than merely connecting sentences. His defence of the massacre supports the intended point. [Ohio State University’s historical account](https://origins.osu.edu/index.php/milestones/april-2019-amritsar-massacre-gandhi-dyer-rowlatt-acts-punjab)

6. **Requested withdrawals become accomplished withdrawals.**

   **Old:** “Lawyers were to leave the courts, students the government colleges, and officials their posts.”

   **New:** “Lawyers were to leave the courts, students the government colleges, and officials their posts. Each of those steps withdrew some of the Indian hands on which the empire depended.”

   The addition moves from what the campaign requested to what participants actually did.

7. **A definition of the criminal charge is introduced.**

   **Old:** “A month later he was put on trial for stirring up disaffection.”

   **New:** “A month later he was put on trial for stirring up disaffection, which meant turning people against the government.”

   This is a new explanatory gloss, broadly faithful to the charge.

8. **A reason for villagers’ supposed indifference is introduced.**

   **Old:** “A demand for independence meant little to a villager.”

   **New:** “A demand for independence meant little to a villager, because it was an idea about government far away.”

   The revision supplies a psychological explanation that the original did not state.

9. **Continuous political damage is introduced.**

   **Old:** “The march to the sea filled the newspapers of the world for three weeks.”

   **New:** “The march to the sea filled the newspapers of the world for three weeks, and every day of it made the government look helpless.”

   The added clause asserts a daily effect on the government’s public standing.

10. **Negotiations acquire an explicit causal explanation.**

    **Old:** “The following year the viceroy released Gandhi and invited him to the palace to negotiate, as one leader with another.”

    **New:** “The pressure told. The following year the viceroy released Gandhi and invited him to the palace to negotiate, as one leader with another.”

    The new transition explicitly attributes the release and negotiations to the preceding pressure.

11. **The naval mutiny becomes the disappearance of the government’s remaining support.**

    **Old:** “In 1946 Indian sailors mutinied in Bombay harbour, and the government in London concluded that it could no longer count on the Indian forces to hold the country down.”

    **New:** “The last prop gave way the next year. In 1946 Indian sailors mutinied in Bombay harbour, and the government in London concluded that it could no longer count on the Indian forces to hold the country down. Without them, the thousand officials had nothing behind them.”

    The new framing goes beyond doubts about military reliability and implies that effective support had vanished.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — the revision reintroduces the salt error that the earlier check had corrected.**

   > “The British government of India held a monopoly on salt, which meant that the right to make and sell it belonged to the government alone.”

   Licensed private manufacture and sale were permitted. The government controlled and taxed the trade; it was not the sole lawful manufacturer and seller. The following sentence’s reference to licences also makes the new explanation internally misleading. Keep the distinction between government control and unauthorised production. [Salt Department institutional history](https://saltcomindia.gov.in/About_HistorySaltDepartment.aspx)

2. **Major — military support did not disappear in 1946.**

   > “The last prop gave way the next year.”  
   > “Without them, the thousand officials had nothing behind them.”

   The mutiny raised serious doubts about the future reliability of Indian forces, but it did not remove the government’s armed support. About 80,000 British troops remained in India in October 1946, and Indian troops continued carrying out government orders. A November parliamentary statement explicitly reported their service in suppressing communal disorders. The defensible fact is that continued coercive rule looked increasingly costly and unreliable, not that officials had no forces behind them. [Study of British military commitments](https://pure.port.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/1078217/ASTERIS_2013_cright_CBH_British_Overseas_Military_Commitments_1945_47.pdf), [Hansard, 27 November 1946](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Lords/1946-11-27/debates/9fc99029-d023-4752-acd7-b6ecc3215e94/Prayers)

3. **Minor — “nearly all British” is wrong for the period established by the opening.**

   > “At the top was a civil service of about a thousand men, nearly all British.”

   Around 1930, Indians already formed a substantial share of the Indian Civil Service. Official figures for 1932 give **843 Europeans and 465 Indians**, making Indians approximately 36 percent of the total. “Mostly British” fits those figures; “nearly all” describes an earlier period. [Hansard, 26 June 1933](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/1933-06-26/debates/59fa1655-2e2c-43bc-ad48-4f3821472d81/WrittenAnswers)

4. **Minor — the marriage restriction is presented as universal and timeless.**

   > “They had to carry passes and pay special taxes, and their marriages were not recognised.”

   The marriage issue arose particularly from the **1913 Searle judgment**, twenty years after Gandhi’s arrival. It affected marriages celebrated under Indian religious traditions without the recognised civil formalities; Christian and legally registered marriages were exceptions. The sentence merges restrictions from different jurisdictions and periods into a condition apparently applying to all Indians throughout his stay. [Gandhi’s account of the judgment](https://www.mkgandhi.org/satyagraha_safrica/39marriage.php)

5. **Minor — the new sentence overstates Congress’s exclusion from popular politics.**

   > “As a result, it could not touch the millions of ordinary Indians on whom British rule actually rested.”

   The earlier correction acknowledging the post-1905 movement remains intact, but this new sentence partly undermines it. Before Gandhi’s national leadership, Swadeshi activists had already organised sections of railway and jute workers and connected economic grievances with nationalist politics. Gandhi enormously broadened participation; ordinary Indians were not previously beyond the movement’s reach. [IGNOU’s account of Swadeshi workers’ mobilisation](https://egyankosh.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/19925/1/Unit-11.pdf)

## Clarity flags

- **Misleading arithmetic:**  
  > “Taken together, in all of India there was roughly one Briton for every two or three thousand Indians.”

  “Taken together” connects incompatible totals, as explained above. The ratio concerns the wider British population, not just the officials and soldiers just counted.

- **New pronoun ambiguity:**  
  > “He gave way, bitterly, to save his life, in exchange for a larger number of seats reserved for his people within the general vote.”

  “He” means Ambedkar, but “his life” means Gandhi’s. The revision replaces the original named subject with a pronoun and makes this harder to follow.

- **An explicit signpost:**  
  > “The method itself fits into a few plain rules.”

  This announces the explanation. The next sentence—“When a law is unjust, do not obey it and do not hide”—already begins it directly.

- **Repetition immediately after the setup:**  
  > “The two men agreed that untouchability was wrong, but they disagreed about the cure.”  
  > “Gandhi condemned untouchability and campaigned against it.”

  The second sentence immediately repeats Gandhi’s half of the agreement. The disagreement about reforming or breaking the caste order carries the passage forward.

- **A redundant explanation:**  
  > “In other words, it worked by shaming a government in front of its own people.”

  This largely repeats the preceding sentence about Britain’s divisions, free press and concern for appearances. It also sits awkwardly beside the opening explanation that non-cooperation deprived government of practical assistance: readers may take this restatement as the whole mechanism.

- **Padding before a concrete fact:**  
  > “India’s part in the war was large.”  
  > “More than a million Indians served.”

  The first sentence adds little orientation; the second already makes its point plainly.

- **An absolute added unnecessarily:**  
  > “The march to the sea filled the newspapers of the world for three weeks, and every day of it made the government look helpless.”

  “Every day” creates the kind of exception-sensitive absolute the style sheet forbids. The overall political effect does not require a claim about each day.

- **A feared outcome becomes an arithmetic certainty:**  
  > “Their fear followed from simple arithmetic. If the British left and India became a democracy, the Hindu majority would rule for ever.”

  The reader needs to understand this as the critics’ fear. Religious population shares do not mathematically guarantee a permanent political bloc. “For ever” also breaks the rule against vulnerable absolutes.

- **Unexplained historical vocabulary:**  
  > “The following year the viceroy released Gandhi…”

  “Viceroy” first appears without explanation. “The king’s representative in India” would supply the orientation required by the plain-words rule.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit chapter cross-references.

## Overall

The revision preserves the chapter’s dates, figures and quotations, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. The salt explanation and the disappearance of military support need correction. Several other additions repeat nearby sentences or turn a useful connection into an unnecessarily absolute claim. The earlier fixes otherwise largely survive. No files were edited.
