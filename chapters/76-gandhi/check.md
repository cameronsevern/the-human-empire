1. **Disputed fact — high confidence that this needs qualification.**  
   > “The harvest had not been especially poor.”

   The size of the crop shortfall before the Bengal famine is disputed. The 1942 crop suffered cyclone and disease damage; scholars disagree about how much this reduced food availability relative to the effects of prices, purchasing power and government policy. Presenting an unexceptional harvest as settled fact suppresses a disagreement central to the explanation. See the [exchange between Mark Tauger and Amartya Sen](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2011/05/12/bengal-famine/).

2. **Factual error — very high confidence.**  
   > “The British arrested the entire leadership of Congress within hours and held them for the rest of the war.”

   Gandhi was released on **6 May 1944**, more than a year before the war ended. “Entire leadership” also overstates the sweep. The immediate correction is to distinguish Gandhi’s detention from that of other Congress leaders. A [contemporary American diplomatic report](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v05/d239) reproduces the announcement of his unconditional release.

3. **Factual error in the closing scene — very high confidence.**  
   > “He spent the day of independence fasting and spinning, in a house that he had chosen to share with a Muslim politician, and in the weeks before and after he walked from village to village, barefoot, asking the people to stop.”

   This merges separate episodes. His barefoot Noakhali peace march ran from **January to March 1947**. Around independence he was staying in urban Calcutta, at Haidari Mansion from 13 August to 7 September. Keep the independence-day scene, but move the village pilgrimage to its proper time. Sources: Gandhi Heritage Portal’s accounts of [the Noakhali march](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/kok/gandhi_heritage_site/writeup/NDI%3D) and [Haidari Mansion](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/ml/gandhi_heritage_site/writeup/MjA%3D).

4. **Misleading history — high confidence.**  
   > “For thirty years it met once a year, in English, passed respectful resolutions, and was ignored.”

   This carries Congress’s early moderate phase all the way to Gandhi’s return in 1915. It omits the boycott and nationalist mobilisation surrounding Bengal’s partition in 1905, and Congress’s bitter split in 1907 over political methods. Gandhi greatly expanded mass politics; he did not introduce it into an organisation that had spent the preceding thirty years doing nothing beyond respectful petitioning. Gandhi himself discussed these divisions in [*Hind Swaraj*](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805215/74310/excerpt/9780521574310_excerpt.pdf).

5. **Factual overstatement; break of the rule against vulnerable absolutes — high confidence.**  
   > “It was illegal for any Indian to make it, to collect it from the shore, or to buy it from anyone except the government, which taxed it.”

   Salt manufacture was permitted under licence, including by private manufacturers. The prohibition was against **unauthorised production and collection**, not manufacture by every Indian or purchase from every nongovernment seller. The [Salt Department’s institutional history](https://www.saltcomindia.gov.in/About_HistorySaltDepartment.aspx) describes the licensing system.

6. **Misleading generalisation — high confidence.**  
   > “To them, Gandhi's movement, with its Hindu hymns, its holy man and its talk of the rule of the god Rama, looked very much like a Hindu revival.”

   “Them” refers to all Indian Muslims. Many Muslims participated in Gandhi’s movement, and Muslim Khilafat leaders endorsed non-cooperation before Congress did. Attribute this perception to Jinnah and other Muslim critics rather than the whole population. The [government’s Gandhi chronology](https://www.publicationsdivision.nic.in/catalog/view/theme/default/images/new_ngm_final/timeline/pages/page9.html) documents that alliance. Chapter 74 has already explained Indian Muslim attachment to the caliph, so the reader has useful groundwork for a brief explanation.

7. **Contradiction in emphasis and chronology with chapter 61 — high confidence.**  
   > “India had once clothed the world, and the machine-made cottons of Lancashire had destroyed its hand weavers.”

   Chapter 61 explicitly says: “Indian weavers kept supplying most of India's own cloth until at least the 1860s,” and distinguishes the earlier devastation of **hand spinning** from the slower displacement of weaving. Here, following “a reason that went back a century,” the reader gets an earlier, wholesale destruction of hand weaving. Preserve the earlier distinction: export markets collapsed, hand spinners suffered particularly severely, and hand weaving survived despite substantial losses. See [chapter 61](chapters/61-coal/chapter.md).

8. **Misrepresentation of Gandhi’s argument — high confidence.**  
   > “They had missed the purpose, which was to give millions of people who could not read, vote or fight something that they could do every day against the empire.”

   Political participation was one purpose, but Gandhi also explicitly argued that spinning would provide income and relieve rural unemployment and poverty. Economists disputing its economic usefulness were therefore addressing a claim he actually made. His [1931 explanation to a Lancashire deputation](https://www.gandhipedia150.in/static/data/highlighted_pdfs_output/Round%20Table%20Conference_volume48_book_53.pdf) makes that economic purpose clear.

9. **Material omission and reader confusion — high confidence.**  
   > “When the British offered his people their own separate voters' rolls, Gandhi, in prison, announced that he would starve himself to death unless the offer was withdrawn.”  
   > “Ambedkar gave way, bitterly, to save his life.”

   “Separate voters’ rolls” does not explain the stakes: these were arrangements allowing the oppressed castes to elect their own representatives. Nor was the outcome simply withdrawal. The **1932 Poona Pact** replaced separate electorates with joint electorates while substantially increasing reserved seats. One sentence explaining that bargain would preserve the coercion and bitterness without erasing Ambedkar’s negotiation. Source: [the pact and its historical introduction](https://www.constitutionofindia.net/historical-constitution/poona-pact-1932-b-r-ambedkar-and-m-k-gandhi/).

10. **Overstated causation — high confidence.**  
    > “Gandhi went to London that autumn, visited the cotton towns whose trade his boycott had ruined, and was cheered by the mill girls.”

    The boycott contributed to Lancashire’s distress, but its industry had been declining for years and was also suffering from the Depression and international competition. “Whose trade his boycott had ruined” makes him responsible for the whole collapse. Say the boycott had hurt their trade. See [the historical account of the Lancashire visit](https://www.mkgandhi.org/newannou/When-Mahatma-Gandhi-was-welcomed-by-textile-mill-workers-of-Lancashire.html).

11. **Imprecise constitutional claim — high confidence about the wording; the intended settlement remains interpretively disputed.**  
    > “In 1940 Jinnah's party declared that the Muslims of India were a separate nation and must have a state of their own.”

    The Lahore Resolution called for **“Independent States”**, plural, in the Muslim-majority northwestern and eastern regions, with autonomous and sovereign constituent units. It did not unambiguously specify the single Pakistan eventually created. Describe the demand for independent Muslim-majority territories without making the final arrangement appear settled in 1940. Source: [the resolution’s text](https://ccek.edu.pk/assets/uploads/Notes/c2-LahoreResolution.pdf).

12. **Reader orientation.**  
    > “It came to two countries, since Jinnah had won his separate state.”

    Pakistan is never named. Even with the details of partition reserved for later, a reader needs to know that the two countries were **India and Pakistan**. “His separate state” also makes the leap from the 1940 demand to the 1947 settlement feel automatic.

13. **Reader orientation and the “plain words” rule.**  
    > “The word he settled on means, roughly, ‘holding firmly to the truth’.”

    The word itself—**satyagraha**—never appears. The preceding sentence sets up a naming story and then withholds its answer. Give the word alongside the translation, or remove the naming competition.

14. **Break of the rule against vulnerable absolutes.**  
    > “Everything else was done by Indians.”

    The preceding paragraph names only senior civil servants and British soldiers, leaving British police officers and other British personnel outside those categories. The core point is sound: Indian labour sustained the administration. “Everything else” needlessly makes it literally false; “Most of the everyday work” would carry the argument. [Parliamentary figures](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/1933-04-03/debates/269f8bcf-0e44-4363-9ef7-a479b8cc7567/CivilService%28Indianisation%29) explicitly record European officers in the Indian Police.

15. **Signpost sentence.**  
    > “The idea was this.”

    This announces the explanation rather than giving it, contrary to style.md. The next sentence—“When a law is unjust, do not obey it and do not hide”—already does the work.
