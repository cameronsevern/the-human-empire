# Chapter 26 — Why did people become monks?

**Line under the title:** India, Central Asia, China, Egypt and Italy, from about the year 100 to about 550.
**Length:** 4,600 to 5,200 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

In the same centuries, at opposite ends of Asia, two religions built the same institution: a house of unmarried men or women living under a written rule, owning everything in common. People joined for the sake of their souls, and also for food, safety, learning, and escape from marriage, taxes and the army. Merchants and kings paid for these houses because they gave something back: a bed on the road, a safe place for goods and money, prayers for the dead, and a blessing on the ruler. A monastery does not marry, divide its land among heirs or die, so it outlasts the governments around it. That is how Buddhism walked from India to China along the caravan roads, and how Christian learning survived in the West. Once rulers were the patrons, though, belief became their business: governments counted monks, taxed or closed monasteries, and treated the wrong doctrine as disloyalty.

## The story

1. **Opening: the bones in the sand.** Answer first. Faxian, about sixty, walks from China to India in 399 to fetch a book of rules. He sleeps in monasteries all the way.
2. **The begging bowl.** What the Buddha's order was: no property, no family, a bowl. The rains retreat becomes a house. The bargain of merit. Why merchants paid: the cave halls of western India and the endowment lent out at interest.
3. **A Buddha in a Greek robe.** The Kushans across the passes. The first statues. The road through the oases.
4. **A religion against the family.** China's objections: sons, ancestors, the bow to the emperor. Why it took root anyway after the Han fell. The translator from Kucha. Merit for dead parents. Emperors carved as Buddhas; the emperor who had to be ransomed. Monks who pay no tax; the first closures.
5. **The desert a city.** Egypt, a farmer's son, and the flight from the world after Constantine. The soldier who wrote a rule. Tax flight and the emperor's law.
6. **Guests at the gate.** The bishop's hospital. Benedict's rule: work, reading, the guest received as Christ. Women's houses.
7. **The emperor's doctrine.** When the ruler is the patron: one creed by law, the first execution for wrong belief, the mob in Alexandria, the churches that split, Christians suspected in Persia.
8. **Ending.** Two gates, eight thousand kilometres apart.
