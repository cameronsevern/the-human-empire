Ranked by seriousness below. No files were edited.

1. **Fact and contradiction with chapter 20 — Buddhism’s expansion predates the Kushans.**  
   > “For its first few centuries, Buddhism stayed inside India. What carried it out was an empire, and one founded by a people already met on the Chinese frontier.”

   Buddhism reached Sri Lanka in the third century BCE, centuries before the Kushan empire. Chapter 20 already tells that story. The Kushans helped its expansion through Central Asia; they did not initiate its movement outside India. **Confidence: high.** [Source: UNESCO’s account of early Sri Lankan monasteries](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6774/).

2. **Fact — merchants were not generally excluded from Brahmanical ritual.**  
   > “In the old order of the priests, a trader ranked low however rich he became, and the rituals that mattered were closed to him.”

   Vaishyas—the rank associated with trade, farming and animal husbandry—were among the three “twice-born” groups entitled to Vedic study and sacrifice. Ranking below priests and warriors did not mean exclusion from these rituals. This weakens the chapter’s stated explanation for merchant patronage. **Confidence: high.** [Source: ANU Press, introduction to *The Viṣṇu Purāṇa*](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n8264/html/introduction.xhtml?page=5).

3. **Fact — Han Buddhism was not confined to foreign merchants.**  
   > “For as long as the Han dynasty stood, the new religion remained a curiosity practised by foreign merchants.”

   An imperial edict of 65 CE already records the Chinese prince Liu Ying’s Buddhist observances and support for monks and lay followers. Buddhism remained limited during the Han, but the sentence excludes documented Chinese participation. **Confidence: high.** [Source: Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Earliest developments”](https://www.rep.routledge.com/articles/overview/buddhist-philosophy-chinese/v-1/sections/earliest-developments).

4. **Fact — the Chinese suppression sequence misplaces events and understates the violence.**  
   > “Twice in these centuries, a northern emperor lost patience, ordered the monasteries closed and sent the monks back to their fields.”

   The two major suppressions were in 446 and 574–577. The second falls outside the stated endpoint of about 550; the first precedes the paragraph’s apparent starting point around 500. The 446 persecution also included executions, not simply compulsory return to farming. **Confidence: high.** [Source: University of Melbourne, Buddhist persecutions](https://library.unimelb.edu.au/asc/whats-on/exhibitions/here-lives-our-culture-introduction/research-essays/the-socio-economic-rationale-behind-buddhist-persecutions).

5. **Doubtful claim — the ending gives Faxian an unsupported expectation.**  
   > “When Faxian set out for India, he took it for granted that there would be a bed for him at the end of each day's walk, and for most of fourteen years there was.”

   His account establishes extensive monastic hospitality, but not this expectation or calculation. It also describes thirty-five days through uninhabited country and an establishment where inadequate hospitality forced companions to seek support elsewhere. The network is real; the dependable nightly bed is an embellishment. **Confidence: high that the cited account does not substantiate this wording.** [Source: Faxian, *Record of Buddhistic Kingdoms*, chapter II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2124/2124-h/2124-h.htm).

6. **Fact — the chapter turns an ideal of poverty into a universal description of practice.**  
   > “The order had found a way to be rich while each of its members stayed poor. The monks did not touch a coin.”

   Buddhist monastic evidence includes individual as well as communal wealth, and financial activity by monks themselves. The Nasik endowment illustrates one arrangement involving guilds; it cannot establish that monks generally remained penniless or never handled money. **Confidence: high on the general correction; the individual recipients’ conduct is unknown.** [Source: research on western Indian monasteries and trade](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352226721000532).

7. **Fact — Persia had persecuted Christians before Constantine.**  
   > “Persia had a sizeable Christian minority, and its kings had left them alone while Rome was persecuting them.”

   Christians experienced persecution under Bahram II in the third century. Shapur II’s later persecution was more extensive, and suspicion of Roman allegiance matters, but it did not follow an uninterrupted period of toleration. **Confidence: high.** [Source: Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Martyrs, Christian”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/martyrs-christian/).

8. **Fact and contradiction with chapter 17 — celibacy does not erase existing children.**  
   > “No monk could sell it or leave it to a son, since he had no son and owned nothing.”

   Men could enter the order after becoming fathers. Chapter 17 explicitly says the Buddha “married and had a son.” Communal property was protected from personal inheritance because it belonged to the order, not because every monk was childless. **Confidence: high.** [Earlier passage: chapter 17](chapters/17-thinkers/chapter.md:97).

9. **Contradiction with chapter 17 and an exception-breaking absolute.**  
   > “Everything in Chinese moral teaching began with the family.”

   Chapter 17 introduces Mozi’s objection to favouring one’s own family and state, alongside other competing traditions. This sentence makes the Confucian position stand for all Chinese moral thought. Identify the influential teaching being described rather than erasing the alternatives already introduced. **Confidence: high.** [Earlier passage: chapter 17](chapters/17-thinkers/chapter.md:58).

10. **Fact and style — a convent was not the only respectable alternative to marriage.**  
    > “A house of nuns was the one respectable way to refuse.”

    Christian women could practise consecrated virginity within domestic settings. Eustochium’s religious life in Rome provides a contemporary example. Monastic communities expanded women’s options, but “the one” is untenable. **Confidence: high.** [Source: Jerome, Letter 22 to Eustochium](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001022.htm).

11. **Doubtful dating — the thousand monasteries belong to a later picture of Luoyang.**  
    > “By about the year 500 there were more than a thousand monasteries in the northern capital alone.”

    The familiar figure of 1,367 describes Northern Wei Luoyang’s development through the period ending in 534; it does not securely establish that number by 500. “By the early sixth century” still needs care; the safer reference point is the city before its destruction in 534. **Confidence: medium-high.** [Source: *Luoyang Qielan Ji*, contextual account](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?if=en&res=751279).

12. **Fact — Antony gave away the land rather than selling it.**  
    > “He sold it, gave away the money, and went to live alone at the edge of the desert, and later deep inside it, eating bread and salt and fighting off the demons that he believed came to tempt him.”

    Athanasius says Antony gave his inherited land to the villagers and sold his movable possessions. Since the preceding sentence identifies “it” as the farm, the transaction is wrong. **Confidence: high about what the surviving account says.** [Source: Athanasius, *Life of Antony*, §§2–3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2811.htm).

13. **Doubtful generalisation — merchant indifference to doctrine is asserted without evidence.**  
    > “A merchant who gave a pillar to a cave wanted merit and did not care what the monks taught.”

    Seeking merit does not imply indifference to religious teaching. The inscriptions establish gifts and sometimes their beneficiaries or purposes; they do not establish this universal state of mind. The political contrast can rest on an emperor’s power to enforce conformity without making merchants doctrinally indifferent. **Confidence: high that the inference is unsupported.** [Source: study of Buddhist patronage and its inscriptional evidence](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arasi_0004-3958_2008_num_63_1_1665).

14. **Reader orientation — the unnamed capitals change underneath the narrative.**  
    > “Around 460 the same dynasty had five colossal Buddhas carved into a sandstone cliff near its capital, each taller than a four-storey house, and it is thought that each stood for one of its emperors.”

    This refers to Yungang near Pingcheng; the later “northern capital” with over a thousand monasteries is Luoyang. Without mentioning the move, a reader naturally pictures one city. A brief geographical transition would prevent that confusion. **Confidence: high.** [Sources: Princeton’s *Chinese Architecture: A History*](https://assets.press.princeton.edu/chapters/s13470.pdf), [Library of Congress description of the Luoyang record](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_17877_001/?sp=2&st=gallery).

15. **Reader orientation and unsupported precision — the translation problem lacks its starting language.**  
    > “The two languages have nothing in common, and Chinese had no words for half the ideas.”

    Only Chinese has been named. The reader cannot tell whether the other language is Sanskrit, an Indian vernacular or Kumarajiva’s native language. “Nothing in common” is an unnecessary absolute, and “half” supplies a numerical claim without evidence. Explain the difficulty through one unfamiliar Buddhist concept and its translation.

16. **Reader orientation — “houses” changes meaning within the Pachomius paragraph.**  
    > “When he died, Pachomius was the head of nine such houses holding several thousand men, and of two for women.”

    Earlier, a “house” contains about twenty monks within a larger establishment. Here it means an entire monastery. The reader is left reconciling nine twenty-person houses with several thousand residents. Distinguish residential houses from monasteries.

17. **Reader orientation — the final doctrinal sequence is too compressed.**  
    > “Two centuries later, when invaders arrived who cared nothing for the formula either way, that resentment mattered.”

    The invaders are unidentified, the formula has not been explained, and “mattered” never says what consequence followed. The sentence asks an inexperienced reader to supply the Arab conquests and a disputed causal connection to them. Either explain the consequence and substantiate it, or leave this later development for the chapter that tells it.

18. **Style — a confident claim becomes an absolute contradicted by the chapter itself.**  
    > “Being a Christian no longer cost anything.”

    The intended point is the end of imperial persecution and the new opportunities under Constantine. The chapter subsequently describes Christians suffering for the wrong doctrine and Christians killed in Persia. Narrow the claim to the changed position of Christians under imperial patronage.

19. **Style — explicit signposting.**  
    > “Two features of the order turned it into one.”

    This announces the explanatory structure before presenting it. It falls under style.md’s prohibition on announcing that a point or answer is coming. The weather can begin the explanation directly.

20. **Style — explicit signposting.**  
    > “The timing is the interesting part.”

    This announces what the reader should find interesting instead of stating the historical connection. The following sentence already supplies that connection.

21. **Style — an explicit backward reference.**  
    > “What carried it out was an empire, and one founded by a people already met on the Chinese frontier.”

    “Already met” points outside the story to the reader’s earlier reading. The next paragraph supplies the Yuezhi’s history naturally, making this cross-reference unnecessary.
