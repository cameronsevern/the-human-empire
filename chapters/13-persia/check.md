I found the following issues, ordered by seriousness. I found no clear contradiction with chapters 9–11, no hedging stacks, and no explicit chapter self-reference or prohibited cross-reference. No files were edited.

1. **Factual error — the comparison with Assyria reverses the chronology.**

   > “Even so, the arrangement lasted for more than two centuries, which was far longer than the empire of terror that came before it.”

   The Achaemenid empire lasted roughly 550–330 BCE, about 220 years. The Neo-Assyrian empire conventionally runs from 911 to 612/609 BCE, about 300 years. Persia therefore did **not** last “far longer.” This matters because the sentence makes longevity evidence for the chapter’s central argument. **Confidence: high.** [Metropolitan Museum chronology](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Assyria_to_Iberia_Art_and_Culture_in_the_Iron_Age.pdf).

2. **Factual overreach — a regional archive becomes an empire-wide accounting office.**

   > “The receipts were sent up to Persepolis, where accountants added them together and checked them against the stores.”

   In context, this covers stations throughout the empire. The Fortification Archive documents an administration centred on Persepolis and surrounding southwestern Iran. It records travellers **from** distant provinces; that does not establish that receipts **issued throughout those provinces** went to Persepolis. Restrict the explanation to the documented regional system. **Confidence: high.** [The archive’s editors describe its geographical coverage and auditing procedures](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/persepolis-admin-archive/persepolis-administrative-archives-i-persepolis-fortification-archive/).

3. **Factual error and breakable absolute — Persian tax exemption.**

   > “The Persians themselves paid nothing.”

   Persians were exempt from the monetary tribute described by Herodotus, but not from every levy. The Fortification tablets record taxes in kind in the Persian heartland, including livestock. “Paid nothing” materially misrepresents how the system was supplied. **Confidence: high.** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Achaemenid Taxation”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/achaemenid-taxation/).

4. **Doubtful claim — twenty tribute districts become twenty newly created provinces.**

   > “Darius divided the empire into about twenty provinces.”

   The following sentence assigns each one a satrap. This presents a disputed reconstruction as a straightforward administrative reform. Satraps already served under Cyrus and Cambyses; Herodotus’s twenty divisions cannot securely be equated with twenty administrative provinces newly established by Darius. The safe distinction is between reorganising tribute and inventing the provincial system. **Confidence: high that the current account oversimplifies; medium on the precise reconstruction.** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Achaemenid Satrapies”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/achaemenid-satrapies/).

5. **Factual error — nine kings were not kings of nine separate countries.**

   > “In all, nine men in nine countries declared themselves kings, and one of them did so in Persia itself.”

   Darius’s list includes two claimants associated with Persia, two with Elam and two with Babylon. It also includes Gaumata, whose overthrow the preceding paragraph has already narrated. The sentence conflates his list of nine defeated kings with his separate list of rebellious countries. **Confidence: high.** [Behistun inscription, §§21 and 52](https://www.livius.org/articles/place/behistun/behistun-3/).

6. **Doubtful claim — direct adoption of Zoroaster’s teaching needs qualification.**

   > “The Persian kings took up that teaching in their own fashion.”

   Darius’s devotion to Ahuramazda and hostility to the Lie are documented. Whether these establish adherence to Zoroaster’s teaching, rather than related Iranian religious traditions, remains disputed. The problem is the asserted historical connection, not the narrator taking a side: the chapter gives readers no indication that the connection is an interpretation. **Confidence: high that the identification is disputed; medium on which interpretation is right.** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Achaemenid Religion”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/achaemenid-religion/).

7. **Unsupported reconstruction — the opening invents personal knowledge.**

   > “Nobody there knew him.”

   > “The governor whose seal he carried was some two and a half thousand kilometres away, and the storekeeper had never set eyes on him.”

   The tablet supports the ration transaction and authorisation, not either claim about whom the storekeeper had met. The seal’s usefulness does not require complete strangers. These details should be removed or clearly presented as an imagined illustration. **Confidence: high that these assertions are unsupported by the cited tablet’s contents.** [Translation of PF 1401, item 299](https://omphale.arthistory.wisc.edu/pdf/Sardis_M2.pdf).

8. **Misleading evidence — the surviving travel letter draws on a prince’s estates.**

   > “One of the travel documents themselves has survived, and it shows how carefully they were drawn up.”

   > “A Persian prince who governed Egypt is sending his steward home from Iraq, and the letter is addressed by name to the officials at each stop along the way.”

   Arshama’s letter explicitly orders provisions from **his estates**. Its relationship to the public provisioning system needs explaining before it can serve as direct confirmation of the preceding account. “From Iraq” is also too definite: the Oxford presentation reconstructs the departure as Susa, in Iran. **Confidence: high on estate provisioning; medium on the departure point.** [Oxford’s translation and commentary](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/travel-authorisation-arshama-0).

9. **Factual inconsistency — 111 daily stages cannot make a ninety-day journey.**

   > “According to their account, it had one hundred and eleven stations along it, a day's walk apart, and a man on foot needed ninety days to cover its length.”

   Herodotus gives 111 stages and separately calculates ninety days from distance and an assumed daily pace. He does not thereby make every station one walking day apart. Remove that equation; it also underlies the opening’s promised stop “the next evening.” **Confidence: high.** [Herodotus 5.53](https://lexundria.com/hdt/5.53.1/mcly).

10. **Technical error and breakable absolute — the qanat gradient is not a universal threshold.**

    > “If the slope is steeper than that, the running water will tear the tunnel apart.”

    > “If it is flatter, the water will not run.”

    “That” means one metre per kilometre. Suitable gradients vary with ground conditions, tunnel design and discharge; water does not cease flowing immediately below that gradient. A published field study describes a qanat gallery with a gradient of 0.4%, four times the chapter’s supposed destructive threshold. Explain the balance between erosion and sediment accumulation without making one approximate figure a physical law. **Confidence: high.** [Field study in *Remote Sensing*](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/23/4468).

11. **Factual error and internal contradiction — qanats have continuing costs.**

    > “The cost is all at the start.”

    Qanats require recurring clearance and repairs. The chapter itself has just explained that shafts provide access for repairs. The defensible point is that they need no pumping energy, not that operation costs nothing. **Confidence: high.** [Encyclopaedia Iranica documents annual maintenance expenditure](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/abyari-irrigation-in-iran/).

12. **Breakable absolute — local government does not eliminate royal expenditure.**

    > “A people that keeps its priests, its judges and its noble families governs itself, at no cost to the king.”

    The chapter’s own governors, garrisons, couriers, accountants and provisioning network contradict “at no cost.” Retaining local institutions can reduce the central government’s costs; it cannot erase them. This is a problem with the absolute, not with the argument that indirect rule was economical.

13. **Reader confusion — the opening party appears to contain thirty-six people.**

    > “He was travelling with twenty-three men and twelve servant boys.”

    > “Nevertheless, the mark of that seal was enough to feed thirty-five strangers.”

    Ordinary English makes Dauma additional to the twenty-three men and twelve boys: thirty-six people. If he is included among the twenty-three, say so. Otherwise readers cannot reconcile the party size with the flour calculation.

14. **Reader confusion — the succession story withholds a useful name.**

    > “According to Darius, he was a priest who was pretending to be the younger son of Cyrus.”

    Readers must track Cambyses, his unnamed brother, an unnamed alleged impostor and Darius, across several paragraphs and two competing versions of events. Naming Cyrus’s younger son **Bardiya** would help substantially. This is precisely the exception allowed by “Few names”: a name needed to follow the story.

15. **Reader confusion — the authentication mechanism remains unexplained.**

    > “Nevertheless, the mark of that seal was enough to feed thirty-five strangers.”

    The chapter explains what authorisation accomplished but not how a distant storekeeper recognised it as valid. Since seals carry the central argument, give one plain sentence explaining what a seal was and how its impression identified the issuing authority. Chapters 9–11 explain seals chiefly as protection against tampering; that does not fully explain this additional function.

16. **Explicit style violations — announcements of a point or surprise.**

    > “Notice what did not happen at that storehouse.”

    > “That is the real interest of it.”

    > “There is a twist in this, and it concerns the man lying under the king's foot.”

    These announce the significance or surprise before delivering it, contrary to “No signpost sentences.” The third is especially close to the prohibited “Here is the strange part.” Each can disappear without losing its following explanation.

17. **Style violation — stacked numbers in the opening.**

    > “The storekeeper gave them flour: about a litre and a half for each man and a litre for each boy, which came to forty-six and a half litres.”

    Coming immediately after the two headcounts and journey distance, this turns the opening into an arithmetic exercise. It breaks “One number per point” and the instruction to round. One memorable total or one daily ration would carry the provisioning point; the exact calculation does not advance the story.
