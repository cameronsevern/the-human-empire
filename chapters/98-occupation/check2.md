## Factual flags

1. **Major — a new absolute misrepresents the diplomatic history.**

   > “With no one to bargain with, Israel simply kept what it held.”

   Israel did have Arab interlocutors after the war and Khartoum. A contemporary intelligence memorandum records secret negotiations between King Hussein and Israeli representatives in May 1968, including an Israeli settlement proposal and Hussein’s counterproposal. The correct distinction is between **negotiations failing to produce an agreement** and **there being nobody willing to negotiate**. [Source: CIA memorandum, 4 June 1968, reproduced in *Foreign Relations of the United States*](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v20/d187).

2. **Major — the new explanation contradicts the chapter’s own example.**

   > “In other words, they were placed where they might help to defend the country, rather than among the Palestinian towns.”

   The preceding sentence’s “chiefly” allows exceptions; this addition removes that qualification. The chapter has just described government-supported settlement at Hebron before 1977. Construction of Kiryat Arba began under the Labour-led government in 1970. The correct fact is that Labour generally favoured particular strategic areas, **while also supporting settlements beside Palestinian population centres**. [Source: contemporary JTA report, 16 September 1970](https://www.jta.org/archive/construction-work-begun-on-residential-section-for-jews-in-hebron).

3. **Minor — the new Oslo sentence contradicts the sentence immediately before it.**

   > “Away from cameras and officials, the two sides could test ideas that neither could yet say in public.”

   The preceding sentence explicitly identifies “three of Arafat’s officials.” PLO officials participated from the outset; the initial Israeli participants were academics working through an unofficial channel. These were talks away from publicity, **not away from officials on both sides**. [Source: US State Department history of the Oslo Accords](https://2001-2009.state.gov/r/pa/ho/time/pcw/97181.htm).

4. **Minor — an existing sentence confuses take-off with the attack time.**

   > “At a quarter to eight on the morning of 5 June, almost the entire Israeli air force took off, flew out over the Mediterranean below the reach of radar, and turned south.”

   **07:45 was the scheduled time of the strikes on Egyptian airfields**, not the departure time. The aircraft had taken off earlier to reach their targets then. This error was not among the earlier check’s resolved flags. [Source: IDF account of Operation Moked](https://www.idf.il/en/mini-sites/wars-and-operations/operation-moked-1967/).

5. **Minor — the new ending compresses the arrangement’s chronology too tightly.**

   > “The uneasy rule for that platform goes back to the week of the war itself.”

   The paragraph identifies the rule with Dayan’s meeting with Muslim administrators. That meeting took place on **17 June 1967**, a week after the war ended and ten days after the Old City’s capture. Initial decisions began during the conquest, but the meeting described belongs to its aftermath. The following “A few days later” is also imprecise for that ten-day interval. [Source: Shmuel Berkovits, “The Risk of Changing the Status Quo on the Temple Mount,” INSS](https://www.inss.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Adkan28.1_3.pdf).

## Clarity flags

1. **The revision breaks a pronoun’s connection to its noun.**

   > “The damage worked on both leaders at once. Each one cost the Israeli government support.”

   “Each one” now appears to refer to the two leaders. It actually means each bombing, mentioned in the previous paragraph. This is the clearest flow regression.

2. **The transition into Sadat creates a misleading chronological connection.**

   > “One Arab leader had drawn a different conclusion from all this.”

   “All this” follows the 1982 Lebanon invasion and massacre, but Sadat’s peace initiative occurred in 1977 and he died in 1981. The backward jump needs to be apparent; as written, the transition implies he responded to events after his death.

3. **Two additions announce their subject rather than state it.**

   > “The numbers show how fast the project grew.”

   > “The puzzle, then, is not what a peace would look like.”

   Both are signposts of the kind the style sheet prohibits. The second also conflates knowing **which questions require settlement** with knowing **what their agreed answers would be**.

4. **The new military sentence reintroduces an absolute.**

   > “Without planes to cover them, the Arab armies on the ground were exposed from the first day.”

   “Without planes” is stronger than the preceding, deliberately corrected “crippled.” It suggests complete absence rather than overwhelming Israeli air superiority.

5. **Several explanatory additions merely repeat what their neighbours already say.**

   > “Conquered land could be handed back, and peace was the price Israel meant to ask for it.”

   This follows an explicit description of returning territory for peace treaties.

   > “Egypt could offer recognition, and Israel could offer the whole of Sinai.”

   The preceding treaty paragraph already supplies exactly this exchange.

   > “In other words, the organisation whose charter had called for an end to Israel now accepted a state beside it.”

   The immediately preceding sentence already says Arafat accepted a state beside Israel rather than replacing it. These additions supply little of the orientation or reasoning that the style sheet welcomes.

6. **The revision invites an unnecessary detour into uncertainty.**

   > “Whether or not it was, the anger had been building for two decades.”

   Following the rumour that the collision was deliberate, this explicitly reopens a minor evidentiary question without resolving it. That conflicts with the instruction to settle minor doubts before writing.

7. **An existing absolute makes a surprising decision sound literally unforeseen.**

   > “The next year Sharon, who was now prime minister, did what nobody had expected of him.”

   “Nobody” is unnecessary and indefensible as a literal claim. The intended point is the reversal by a politician associated with settlement expansion, not universal ignorance of his plans.

8. **The new border sentence is both absolute and undefined.**

   > “Any border would now leave somebody's town on the wrong side.”

   “Wrong” could mean outside its residents’ preferred state, outside an internationally recognised boundary, or physically disconnected. “Any” also rules out every conceivable arrangement without explaining why. The preceding sentence already makes the clearer point that settlements complicated drawing a border.

9. **One sentence remains hard to attribute.**

   > “Israel recognised Arafat's organisation as the representative of the Palestinian people, and it recognised Israel's right to exist.”

   Grammatically, “it” can refer to Israel. The intended subject of the second clause is the PLO.
