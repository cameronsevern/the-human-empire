I found no direct contradictions with chapters 61, 62 or 66, and no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or prohibited cross-references. The findings below are ordered by seriousness. No files were edited.

1. **Factual error; broken absolute.** “Nobody had yet proved that one particular microbe caused one particular disease.” Also: “It was the first proof that a specific germ caused a specific disease.”

   Agostino Bassi demonstrated that a microscopic fungus caused silkworm disease in 1835, forty years before Koch’s anthrax work. Koch’s achievement was a decisive experimental demonstration involving bacteria, including cultivation outside the host and reinfection. Both sentences erase an established predecessor central to the chapter’s question. **Confidence: high.** [Bassi’s original investigation, in translation](https://apsjournals.apsnet.org/doi/book/10.1094/9780890545256).

2. **Factual error in the concluding argument.** “In the countries that adopted these measures, that figure began to fall around 1900, and it has kept falling.”

   This conflates infant mortality with mortality throughout the first five years. In England and Wales, mortality among children aged one to four was declining by the middle of the nineteenth century, clearly before 1900. Infant mortality followed a different timetable. **Confidence: high.** [Robert Woods, *The Demography of Victorian England and Wales*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/demography-of-victorian-england-and-wales/origins-of-the-secular-decline-of-childhood-mortality/207DA8709BAF6ECB6F3737A93E1260EA).

3. **Unsupported universal claim; broken absolute.** “For as long as there had been people, about two children in every five had died before their fifth birthday, in the palaces of kings as well as in hovels.”

   High historical child mortality is well established; this uniform rate across all human history and social classes is not. Even London’s estimated under-five mortality had fallen to about 30% by the second quarter of the nineteenth century. A figure needs a defined population and period, rather than being made timeless. **Confidence: high that the universal formulation is untenable.** [Research on London mortality, 1550–1850](https://www.peterrazzell.co.uk/Papers/The_History_of_Infant_Child_and_Adult_Mo.pdf).

4. **Factual error in the Snow story.** “Snow took his map to the parish officials, and on 8 September they removed the handle of the pump.”

   The handle-removal date is correct, but the map belongs later. Snow presented his findings to the guardians on 7 September; his documented presentation of the map was in December. The investigation persuaded the officials, not the famous finished map. **Confidence: high.** [Brody and colleagues, “Map-making and myth-making in Broad Street”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0140673600024429).

5. **Misleading causal sequence; internal contradiction.** “Once it was known that disease travelled in water, milk and filth as living particles that could be filtered out or killed, cities set about doing so.”

   Substantial water and sanitation improvements preceded acceptance of germ theory. The chapter itself has already explained that London’s sewers were authorised under the influence of miasma theory. Germ theory strengthened and redirected sanitation; it did not initiate it. **Confidence: high.** [UCLA’s Snow archive, including the chronology of water-supply reforms](https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream3_GrandExperiment_a.html).

6. **Factual error.** “Yeast is a microscopic plant that feeds on sugar and gives off alcohol.”

   Yeast is a fungus. Calling it a plant could represent nineteenth-century classification, but this sentence presents a modern explanation in the narrator’s voice. **Confidence: high.** [American Phytopathological Society, introduction to fungi](https://www.apsnet.org/edcenter/Pages/IntroFungi.aspx).

7. **Doubtful discovery anecdote presented as settled fact.** “One of his cultures of the germ was left on a shelf over the summer holidays.”

   The familiar accidental-holiday story is disputed by research into Pasteur’s notebooks. The secure finding is that his laboratory developed protection using attenuated chicken-cholera cultures; the neat sequence of abandonment, accidental discovery and immediate understanding is not comparably secure. Under “Settle doubt before writing,” retain the demonstrated result rather than this disputed origin story. **Confidence: medium-high.** [Historical review discussing the notebook evidence](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/apm.13325).

8. **Misleading account of the anthrax experiment.** “He applied the method to anthrax, and in 1881 he accepted a challenge to prove it in public.”

   Following the stale-culture story, “the method” implies that the public trial simply used the same ageing procedure. The Pouilly-le-Fort vaccine was prepared using potassium dichromate; Pasteur’s private records differ materially from the familiar public account of attenuation by oxygen. The general principle of attenuation carried over, but the procedure did not. **Confidence: high.** [Gerald Geison, *The Private Science of Louis Pasteur*, discussion of the laboratory records](https://www.academia.edu/66843303/THE_PRIVATE_SCIENCE_OF_LOUIS_PASTEUR_BY_GERALD_LYNN_GEISON).

9. **Doubtful exclusivity and misleading treatment scene.** “Pasteur had been working on a treatment made from the dried spinal cords of infected rabbits, and had tried it only on dogs.” Also: “He agonised for a day and then began.”

   Pasteur’s notebooks record two earlier human treatment attempts, although their preparations and circumstances need distinguishing from Meister’s course. “Only on dogs” therefore needs a narrowly specified meaning. Meister arrived on the morning of 6 July and received his first injection that evening, administered by the physician Jacques-Joseph Grancher. The present sequence suggests a day’s delay and that Pasteur personally performed the treatment. **Confidence: high on the earlier attempts, timing and physician’s role; medium on whether the exact preparation makes “only on dogs” defensible.** [Geison’s study of the human cases](https://www.columbia.edu/itc/history/rothman/COL476C4947.pdf); [history of rabies treatment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6082082/).

10. **Reader comprehension: exposure is confused with established illness.** “Rabies was always fatal.”

    In this position, the sentence invites the reader to think Meister already had fatal rabies and was cured. He had been exposed through bites; vaccination sought to prevent the disease from developing. Explain that distinction and why treatment after a bite can still work. The problem is the missing causal step, not the sentence’s confidence. **Confidence: high.** [WHO explanation of rabies and post-exposure vaccination](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/rabies).

11. **Factual errors; broken absolutes.** “Until then every dye had come from a plant, an insect or a shellfish.” Also: “Mauve was the first to be made in a laboratory, and it launched an industry.”

    Artificial dyes preceded mauve: picric acid was prepared in 1771 and used to colour silk yellow. Perkin’s achievement can confidently be described as launching the modern synthetic-dye industry, without claiming that laboratory-made dyes had never existed. **Confidence: high.** [Science History Institute, history of picric acid](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/picric-acids-volatile-history/).

12. **Misleading discovery chronology.** “The next twenty years were the most fruitful in the history of medicine.” Followed by: “Using his methods, researchers identified the germs of cholera, typhoid, diphtheria, tetanus, pneumonia and plague, at the rate of about one a year.”

    “Next” follows March 1882, but the pneumococcus was identified in 1881, and Pacini had described the cholera organism in 1854, before Koch’s independent work. The list cannot stand as a straightforward sequence of discoveries after 1882 using Koch’s methods. The evaluative claim about fruitful years is not itself the problem. **Confidence: high.** [Robert Koch Institute on Pacini and Koch](https://www.rki.de/EN/Institute/The-RKI/History/Robert-Koch/Robert_Koch_en.html); [historical account of the 1881 pneumococcus work](https://www.mdpi.com/2218-273X/12/4/596).

13. **Chronology obscured by compression.** “The smell went away, and cholera went with it.”

    The passage jumps from the 1858 funding decision to apparent eradication. London suffered another major epidemic in 1866, especially in the East End, where the sewer works were incomplete and the water supply became contaminated. Give the reader a completion interval and the final outbreak; otherwise the sentence suggests an immediate result. **Confidence: high.** [National Audit Office history of water and sewerage development](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2003/01/0203351_his_dev_modern_water.pdf).

14. **Overstated rejection narrative.** “In the 1840s and 1850s, two doctors showed by careful counting that disease was being carried on unwashed hands and in dirty water, and almost nobody believed them.”

    Semmelweis faced serious opposition, but also had prominent supporters, including Škoda, Rokitansky and Hebra, and practitioners who adopted his washing procedure. “Almost nobody” turns contested acceptance into near-total isolation. “Their findings failed to win general acceptance” would preserve the point accurately. **Confidence: high.** [Historical account documenting supporters and adoption](https://epa.oszk.hu/02300/02316/00011/pdf/EPA02316_kaleidoscope_2015_11_173-178.pdf).

15. **Overbroad institutional claim.** “German universities, alone in the world, had turned their professors’ laboratories into training schools, where students learned by doing original experiments and earned a degree for a piece of new research.”

    Germany pioneered an influential model, but research-based laboratory training had already spread abroad before the 1870s endpoint given here. London’s Royal College of Chemistry opened in 1845 under Hofmann, reproducing the German research-school approach; Perkin emerged from that very setting. Distinguish German leadership from an exclusive monopoly, and distinguish laboratory training from the research doctorate. **Confidence: high on the overstatement.** [Contemporary reports of research at the Royal College, 1845–47](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/mg5ac247); [historical study of the German model’s spread](https://academic.oup.com/book/39837/chapter/339974060).

16. **Misleading order of events.** “Another assistant, whose name was Petri, designed a flat glass dish with a lid to hold it.”

    Immediately followed by Koch’s March 1882 tuberculosis announcement, this makes the Petri dish appear to be part of the preceding toolkit. Petri published his modification in 1887. Mark the later date or change the sequence. **Confidence: high.** [CDC’s history of the Petri dish](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7774570/).

17. **Factual date error.** “In the 1820s it began to travel along the trade routes, with the armies and the steamships, and in 1831 it reached Britain.”

    The first cholera pandemic began in 1817, spreading beyond India during that pandemic. The British arrival in 1831 is correct. **Confidence: high.** [US National Library of Medicine, cholera history](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/cholera/introduction.html).

18. **Reader comprehension; unexplained technical term.** “One of his cultures of the germ was left on a shelf over the summer holidays.”

    “Culture” has not been explained. A general reader needs to know that it means microbes grown in a prepared nutrient liquid or other material. Earlier passages show organisms growing, but never connect that process to this new noun. This breaks the requirement to explain a historical or technical term when it first appears.

19. **Reader comprehension: the laboratory argument lacks its connecting step.** “The German dye firms hired them, and did something that no business had done before.”

    The ensuing company-laboratory and Edison material sends the reader away from germs without showing how this organisation changed the medical discoveries just narrated. Koch has been working behind a curtain and Pasteur helping a manufacturer; neither scene yet demonstrates the new institutional arrangement. The section needs a concrete connection back to bacteriology to justify its length.

20. **Signpost sentences.** “The missing piece was supplied by a man who was not a doctor at all.” Also: “The dyes that Koch used had a history of their own, and it shows how the way of making discoveries was changing.”

    Both announce the explanatory role of the passage about to follow. They fit the guide’s prohibition on announcing an answer or point before stating it. Begin with Pasteur’s actual work, and with the concrete connection between chemical dyes and laboratory research.
