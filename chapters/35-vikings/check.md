1. **Factual error affecting the main argument — high confidence.**  
   > “A king with bishops and a treasury had no use for freelance pirates, and he taxed his own farmers instead of robbing other people's.”

   Christian kings could tax their subjects **and** invade other countries. Cnut conquered England in 1016 and received an enormous payment in 1018; he was already Christian. Stronger kingdoms helped change the organisation of warfare, but conversion did not simply replace overseas plunder with domestic taxation. This also undermines “The Viking age ended because raiding stopped paying.” [Source: Royal Household, Cnut](https://www.royal.uk/canute-great-r-1016-1035).

2. **Misleading ending — high confidence.**  
   > “England had been taken by Vikings after all.”

   England had already been conquered by Cnut fifty years earlier. Moreover, calling William’s army “Vikings” changes the chapter’s opening definition from an occupation to ancestry. Norman descent makes the closing joke intelligible, but it does not make the Norman conquest a delayed first Scandinavian conquest. [Source: English Heritage, early medieval England](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/story-of-england/early-medieval/introduction/).

3. **Factual error, contradiction with chapter 27, and prohibited absolute — high confidence.**  
   > “Everybody in Europe knew that danger came by land.”

   Chapter 27 has already described the Vandals crossing into Africa, acquiring a fleet, and destroying an imperial expedition with fireships. Seaborne attack was familiar centuries before Lindisfarne; English Heritage also records northmen killing a royal official after landing in Wessex in 789. The following sentence—“Armies marched along roads, and a place with the sea at its back was a place where nothing could reach you”—repeats the error. Alcuin’s astonishment cannot establish universal European ignorance. [Earlier chapter](chapters/27-fall/chapter.md:113); [English Heritage](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/lindisfarne-priory/History/viking-raid/).

4. **Unsupported inference from DNA — high confidence.**  
   > “They were local men who had joined the trade.”

   The study establishes that some people buried in Scandinavian fashion lacked detectable Scandinavian ancestry. It cannot establish that they personally became raiders, traders, or immigrants’ recruits. Burial practice and genetic ancestry are evidence about cultural affiliation and descent, not a person’s occupation. The preceding claim that DNA “confirmed what the word had always meant” also asks genetics to settle a linguistic question. [Source: Cambridge’s account of the study](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/worlds-largest-ever-dna-sequencing-of-viking-skeletons-reveals-they-werent-all-scandinavian).

5. **Misleading political history — high confidence.**  
   > “England at that time was divided into four kingdoms, and in ten years the army destroyed three of them.”

   Mercia’s king was expelled, but Mercia did not simply disappear alongside the other conquered kingdoms. Ceolwulf II succeeded him, and western Mercia remained under English government after partition. “Destroyed three” obscures the polity that subsequently worked with Wessex. [Source: historian Sean Miller’s account, drawing on the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle](https://www.anglo-saxons.net/hwaet/?do=get&id=873a&type=chron).

6. **Overgeneralisation supporting a doubtful explanation — high confidence about regional variation.**  
   > “Scandinavia has long winters and little good soil.”

   Norway’s limited arable land cannot stand for all Scandinavia, particularly Denmark, with its fertile agricultural country. The following claim that farms usually produced “nothing to spare” also needs evidence: it makes near-universal subsistence poverty the foundation of the expansion story. Describe the relevant regional constraints rather than applying Norway’s geography to Danish and Swedish raiders. [Source: Denmark’s official history overview](https://denmark.dk/people-and-culture/history).

7. **Factual overstatement — high confidence.**  
   > “The old religion had many gods and no priesthood, and it suited a world of independent chiefs.”

   There were recognised cult leaders, including *goðar*, described as heathen priests; chiefs themselves could exercise religious authority. The defensible distinction is the absence of a centralised ecclesiastical hierarchy comparable to the Christian Church, not the absence of priestly functions. [Source: National Museum of Denmark](https://en.natmus.dk/historical-knowledge/denmark/prehistoric-period-until-1050-ad/the-viking-age/religion-magic-death-and-rituals/the-viking-blot-sacrifices/).

8. **Ship dimensions conflated — high confidence.**  
   > “A Viking ship of the largest kind was about as long as two buses parked end to end.”

   The surrounding description appears to use the roughly 24-metre Gokstad ship as the largest type. Much larger warships existed: Roskilde 6 measured about 37 metres. Choose a particular vessel as the example, or remove “of the largest kind.” [Source: Viking Ship Museum, Roskilde 6](https://www.vikingeskibsmuseet.dk/besoeg/udstillinger/digitale-udstillinger/roskildeskibene/roskilde-6).

9. **Overgeneralised engineering claim and absolute — high confidence.**  
   > “A fully loaded ship needed less than a metre of water to float.”

   Draught varied by vessel and load; the museum gives approximately one metre for the large warship Skuldelev 2. “Less than a metre” is not a universal specification. The following “It could be run straight onto any beach” likewise needs a suitable shore and conditions. Distinguish shallow-draught raiding vessels from a single ship supposedly suited to every ocean, river and portage. [Source: Viking Ship Museum, Skuldelev 2 specifications](https://www.vikingeskibsmuseet.dk/besoeg/udstillinger/de-fem-vikingeskibe/skuldelev-2-det-store-langskib).

10. **Archaeological evidence made to prove too much — high confidence.**  
    > “It was the earliest known meeting between people from Europe and people of the Americas, whose ancestors had parted on the far side of the world tens of thousands of years before.”

    Following the tree-ring explanation, “It” makes the 1021 woodcutting sound like a dated encounter. The study dates **Norse activity in Newfoundland**, not a meeting with Indigenous people. Separate the archaeological date from the encounters reported in the sagas. The preceding “The date is known to the year” also needs to specify the activity, rather than the settlement’s founding or Leif’s voyage. [Source: the dating study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03972-8).

11. **False absolute about knowledge of America — high confidence.**  
    > “Nobody in the rest of Europe took any notice.”

    Adam of Bremen described Vinland in the 1070s, using information associated with the Danish court. This also qualifies “For centuries the only evidence that it had happened was the stories that Icelanders told”: a medieval continental account existed. The voyages failed to initiate sustained European colonisation, but they were not entirely unnoticed outside Iceland. [Source: study discussing Adam’s account](https://doi.org/10.3138/cjh-2019-0028).

12. **Outdated archaeological claim — high confidence.**  
    > “A single Viking helmet has been found in one piece, and it is a plain round cap of iron with a guard for the eyes.”

    Gjermundbu was recovered in fragments and reconstructed. The Yarm helmet has also been authenticated as an early medieval helmet, probably Viking Age; the Gjermundbu museum explicitly withdrew the claim that its helmet was the only one. “One piece” is wrong, and the uniqueness claim is outdated. [Source: University of Oslo’s Museum of Cultural History](https://www.khm.uio.no/english/news/the-viking-helmet-from-yarm.html).

13. **Overstatement about Iceland’s institutions — high confidence on taxation; medium on the intended scope.**  
    > “Iceland had no army, no tax collectors and no police.”

    This paragraph presents an arrangement lasting more than three centuries, but Iceland introduced a compulsory tithe in 1096/1097. Absence of a royal executive is not absence of organised revenue collection throughout the Commonwealth. Restrict the claim to the early period and the absence of a central state apparatus. [Source: Icelandic National Archives, tithe](https://ordabelgur.skjalasafn.is/kb/tiund/).

14. **An inference presented as an exclusive rule — high confidence.**  
    > “They borrow words like *they* only from people they marry.”

    Pronoun borrowing supports sustained, intimate language contact; it does not establish marriage as a necessary condition. Bilingual communities and language shift can transmit core vocabulary. Intermarriage is a plausible contributor here, but “only” turns an effective observation into an unsupported universal law, contrary to *style.md*. [Source: research on Norse-derived vocabulary and language contact](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/43CD58F6C21B26FD00126D904B28B652/S1360674320000465a.pdf/old_norsederived_lexis_in_multilingual_accounts_a_case_study.pdf).

15. **Incorrect present-tense absolute — high confidence.**  
    > “Scandinavia has no silver mines.”

    Sweden has the historic Sala silver mine, among others. The intended point concerns the Vikings’ dependence on imported silver, not a permanent absence of Scandinavian silver deposits or mines. Give the claim its Viking-age boundary. [Source: Sala Silver Mine’s history](https://www.salasilvergruva.se/historia-och-fakta/).

16. **Reader orientation: the narrative’s central map is missing.**  
    > “A ship could be rowed up a northern river, dragged a few kilometres across the watershed on log rollers, and floated down the Dnieper to the Black Sea and Constantinople, or down the Volga to the Caspian and the lands of the caliph in Baghdad.”

    Chapters 27 and 30 have established Constantinople and Baghdad, but not these connecting routes. “Watershed” is unexplained, and the sentence compresses two distinct journeys into one. Explain the divide between river systems and make clear that reaching the Caspian does not mean sailing directly into Baghdad. The map planned in the outline would do substantial work here.

17. **Reader orientation: too many unnamed kingdoms and kings at the climax.**  
    > “The English king caught him by surprise and killed him, and the survivors needed only twenty-four ships to go home.”

    Name Harald Hardrada and Harold Godwinson when introducing them. They are the principal actors in the closing sequence, and Harold appears again against William. Likewise, naming Wessex when introducing Alfred would help readers follow the earlier territorial division. These names satisfy the style guide’s exception for names needed to follow the story.

18. **Reader orientation: an existing landmark is unnecessarily disguised.**  
    > “They stood on duty through the long services in the great church of Holy Wisdom, and at least one of them was bored.”

    Chapter 27 has already introduced Hagia Sophia. Identify this as Hagia Sophia, with “Holy Wisdom” as its translated name, so the reader recognises the building. The boredom is an imagined motive, not something the surviving inscription establishes; it should not carry the explanation for the graffiti.
