## Factual flags

1. **Major — agricultural states could field mounted archers.**

   > “Put the horse, the dried food and the bow together, and the result is a kind of army that no farming country could raise.”

   “Could raise” makes this false. Agricultural states could recruit and train mounted archers, including men of steppe origin. The chapter itself subsequently describes Egypt’s rulers doing precisely that. The defensible distinction is that pastoral life supplied an unusually large pool of already trained riders, rather than that agricultural states could not raise comparable cavalry. [Source: Museum With No Frontiers, “The Mamluk System.”](https://islamicart.museumwnf.org/exhibitions/isl/the_mamluks/exhibition.php?page=2&theme=1)

2. **Major — birth continued to matter in military appointments.**

   > “The officers were chosen for what they could do, and not for their birth.”

   Genghis promoted capable followers, but this did not eliminate family standing and kinship from the command structure. His relatives held commands, and recruitment into the prestigious imperial guard explicitly privileged commanders’ sons and brothers. Ability and loyalty could overcome low birth; birth did not become irrelevant. [Source: *The Secret History of the Mongols*, sections on guard recruitment and organisation.](https://www.altaica.ru/SECRET/lib/de%20Rachewiltz_The%20Secret%20History%20of%20the%20Mongols%20%28%D0%BF%D0%B5%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%BE%D0%B4%2C%20Papers%20on%20Far%20Eastern%20History%29.pdf)

3. **Minor — the new wall sentence contradicts the chapter’s own explanation.**

   > “Everything that made the horsemen dangerous in open country was useless in front of a wall.”

   A few sentences later, the horsemen destroy relief armies and isolate cities. Mobility and mounted fighting therefore remained useful to siege operations, even though they could not themselves breach substantial walls. The correct distinction is between surrounding a city and breaking its defences. Source: the chapter’s own sentence, “The horsemen destroyed the enemy’s field armies and cut a city off from help, and the engineers then opened it.”

4. **Major — Mohi is misdescribed as the same charging-knights trap as Legnica.**

   > “Both battles were won by the same trick.”

   > “When the knights’ heavy horses were blown and their line had come apart, the Mongols turned and closed in from the sides.”

   At Mohi, the decisive action involved fighting over the Sajó crossing and surrounding the Hungarian camp. Feigned withdrawal featured in the campaign, but the account here substitutes a single pursuit-and-counterattack scenario for the battle’s actual sequence. The two battles need distinct descriptions, or a broader statement about Mongol coordination and manoeuvre. [Source: Laszlovszky and colleagues, reconstruction of the Battle of Muhi in *Hungarian Historical Review*.](https://hunghist.org/83-articles/564-2019-2-laszlovszky)

5. **Major — the Mongols returned after Ain Jalut.**

   > “Two years later, a Mongol army was beaten in a great battle and did not come back to avenge it.”

   Mongol forces returned to northern Syria almost immediately and were defeated near Homs in December 1260. Further campaigns followed during decades of Mongol–Mamluk warfare. Ain Jalut ended that occupation of Syria; it did not end attempts to recover it. This is a different problem from the earlier check’s already-fixed “first great defeat” claim. [Source: Reuven Amitai, “Hulāgu Khan,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*.](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hulagu-khan/)

6. **Major — access to roads is not a general right to postal services.**

   > “The relay stations, the tablets and the merchants’ right to use them added up to something new.”

   Merchants’ use of the official relay system was conditional and restricted. Möngke prohibited merchant use; it was not a standing entitlement enjoyed by merchants generally throughout the period described. Distinguish access to trade routes from authorised use of government horses, provisions, and stations. [Source: Willem Floor, “Čāpār,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*.](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/capar-or-capar-turk/)

7. **Minor — the first-target chronology remains unfixed.**

   > “The first target was north China, which lay just across the Gobi desert and was the richest country any Mongol had heard of.”

   The next sentence identifies this target as Jin territory. Campaigns against Western Xia preceded the Jin invasion of 1211; the chapter itself subsequently acknowledges the earlier siege. The earlier check flagged this, but the sentence remains unchanged. Western Xia should precede Jin in the sequence. [Source: *The Cambridge History of the Mongol Empire*, introductory chronology.](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811084/24899/excerpt/9781108424899_excerpt.pdf)

8. **Minor — the rainfall interval is still shifted by the wording.**

   > “The rings show that the fifteen years after 1211 were the longest run of wet years that central Mongolia had seen in more than a thousand.”

   The geographical overstatement identified previously has been fixed. The remaining date problem is “after 1211”: the study’s fifteen-year interval is **1211–1225 inclusive**, not the fifteen years following 1211. [Source: Pederson and colleagues, “Pluvials, droughts, the Mongol Empire, and modern Mongolia.”](https://www.pnas.org/content/pnas/111/12/4375.full.pdf)

9. **Minor — the empire’s extent conflates invasion with lasting rule.**

   > “It ran from Korea to Hungary and covered about a sixth of the land on earth.”

   In context, this describes territory held under Kublai. Hungary had been invaded and temporarily occupied in 1241–42, but remained outside the Mongol Empire. “To Hungary” needs to mean its frontier explicitly, or the western endpoint should refer to territory actually under Mongol rule. [Source: Christopher Atwood, *Encyclopedia of Mongolia and the Mongol Empire*, discussion of central Europe.](https://turkistanilibrary.com/sites/default/files/encyclopedia_of_mongolia_and_the_mongol.pdf)

## Clarity flags

- **Signposting:**  
  > “Historians still argue about the reason, and there are three candidates.”  
  > “The first is politics.”  
  > “The second is grass.”  
  > “The third is walls.”

  This turns the paragraph into an announced checklist. The explanations themselves are useful; the numbered introductions conflict with the prohibition on signpost sentences.

- **Announcement before explanation:**  
  > “The victors were the soldiers who ruled Egypt, and they were soldiers of an unusual kind.”

  “An unusual kind” announces interest without explaining it. The next sentence supplies the substance.

- **Vague novelty and padding:**  
  > “The relay stations, the tablets and the merchants’ right to use them added up to something new.”

  “Something new” postpones the point, and “The effect was that” immediately restarts the explanation. Beyond the factual access problem above, this is a weak transition.

- **Ambiguous arithmetic:**  
  > “Ten of these made a hundred, ten hundreds made a thousand, and ten thousands made a division of ten thousand.”

  “Ten thousands” can mean ten groups of a thousand or several groups of ten thousand. “Ten units of a thousand” would make the intended arithmetic clear.

- **Unidentified twin city:**  
  > “Even so, for five years his army sat in front of the twin fortress cities of Xiangyang, which guarded the route south, and could not get in.”

  The plural “cities” receives only one name. Readers need to understand that this means Xiangyang and its neighbouring fortress city, Fancheng.

- **Repeated wording:**  
  > “The means were horses, and the number of horses on the steppe depends on the weather.”

  “Means” repeats the preceding sentence’s ending; “horses” repeats within this sentence and again in the next paragraph’s grass–horses explanation. The causal bridge is useful, but the repetition is conspicuous.

- **Exception-breaking absolute:**  
  > “It can move no faster than the carts do.”

  An army could detach cavalry or advance ahead of its baggage. “Can move no faster” is stronger than the preceding, appropriately qualified “usually.”

- **Overbroad everyday absolute:**  
  > “Everything about this life happened on a horse.”

  The following account includes milking, processing food, and moving a house by cart. The line exaggerates beyond its own illustration.

- **Caption punctuation:**  
  > “A Mongolian herder on a small horse, with a pack camel and a flock, on the grassland of Mongolia today..”

  Double full stop.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-references, or chapter-number cross-references.

## Overall

The revision improves some connections, but it is not fact-neutral: several bridges introduce absolutes, new causal claims, or institutional entitlements. The most consequential corrections concern Mohi, the return after Ain Jalut, military appointments, and merchant access to relay stations. Most earlier fixes hold; the first-target chronology and rainfall interval still need attention. No files were edited.
