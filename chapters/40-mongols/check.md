1. **Factual error; internal contradiction — high confidence.**  
   > “The effect was that, for about a century, a person could cross Asia under a single authority.”

   Mongol rule facilitated transcontinental travel, but there was no century of unified authority. The empire fractured after 1260—as the chapter itself says—and wars between its successor states disrupted trade. In 1263–64, rival khans even massacred merchants serving the other side. Describe travel through related Mongol states, with periods of cooperation and conflict. [Source: *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Mongols.”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mongols-index/mongols-i-the-people/)

2. **Factual error and overstated explanation — high confidence on the return; disputed causation.**  
   > “Whatever weighed most, they did not come back, and western Europe was spared by its distance and its lack of grass.”

   They invaded Hungary again in 1285. Nor does acknowledging disagreement earlier in the paragraph justify ending with distance and grass as the settled explanation. Military resistance, fortifications and Mongol politics also matter. [Source: Laszlovszky and colleagues, research on the Hungarian invasions.](https://real.mtak.hu/92415/1/HHR_2018-3_Laszlovszky_Pow_Romhanyi_Ferenczi_Pinke.pdf)

   > “The great khan had died in Mongolia, and the princes with the army had an election to attend.”

   This presents a disputed explanation as an established obligation that caused their departure. Batu did not return to Mongolia for the election. [Source: Büntgen and Di Cosmo, discussion of the competing explanations.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4881396/)

3. **Doubtful rule; exception-breaking absolute — high confidence.**  
   > “A city that opened its gates would be plundered and taxed, and its people would live.”

   Submission generally improved a city’s chances; it did not guarantee survival. Merv surrendered after resistance and negotiations, yet its population was massacred. Previous rebellion, resistance and commanders’ decisions complicate the proposed binary. State the incentive to surrender without promising immunity. [Source: account of Merv’s surrender and massacre, with references to the medieval narratives.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Merv_%281221%29)

4. **Factual error — high confidence.**  
   > “Two years later, the Mongols were beaten for the first time in a great battle, at a place in Palestine called Ain Jalut.”

   Jalal al-Din defeated a substantial Mongol army at Parwan in 1221. Ain Jalut in 1260 was a consequential defeat, not their first major battlefield defeat. [Source: Christopher Atwood, *Encyclopedia of Mongolia and the Mongol Empire*, “Parwan, Battle of.”](https://turkistanilibrary.com/sites/default/files/encyclopedia_of_mongolia_and_the_mongol.pdf)

5. **Misleading central explanation; contradiction with chapter 36 — high confidence.**  
   > “Someone else has to grow his food and haul it to him, and an army on campaign drags behind it a long tail of carts.”

   This follows “In a farming country” but makes one supply arrangement universal. [Chapter 36 explicitly says](chapters/39-americas/chapter.md:37): “Armies marched on foot and carried their own food.” Mongol armies themselves also used baggage trains and supplies from agricultural populations. Their advantage was reduced dependence on cumbersome supplies in suitable conditions, not a categorical separation between self-feeding horsemen and wagon-dependent farmers. [Source: Timothy May, *The Mongol Art of War*, discussion of logistics.](https://dokumen.pub/the-mongol-art-of-war-1844154769-9781844154760.html)

6. **Overgeneralisation; tension with chapter 34 — high confidence.**  
   > “The knights of Europe fought as individuals, each seeking a worthy opponent.”

   European knights also fought in organised formations and coordinated forces. The reader has already encountered the Templars and Hospitallers living under rules and forming a standing army in [chapter 34](chapters/37-crusades/chapter.md:125). This contrast turns differences in discipline and tactics into a misleading opposition between duellists and soldiers. Describe the failures of the particular armies at Legnica and Mohi instead of attributing one behaviour to European knights generally.

7. **Disputed environmental claim presented as fact — high confidence that qualification is needed.**  
   > “The winter had also been wet, and the Hungarian plain, which is the last and smallest patch of steppe on the way west, had turned to marsh and could not feed their horses.”

   A cold, snowy winter and subsequent thaw are relevant, but the claim that the plain became unable to support the horses is a contested reconstruction. Research challenging it finds no evidence for a basin-wide collapse in food production and questions the proposed military effects. Present this as one explanation, not the agreed factual foundation beneath the debate. [Source: Pinke and colleagues, “Climate of doubt.”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629221/)

   > “The pattern was the same at every edge.”

   The chapter’s own examples break this absolute: the Mongols crossed the Kyzylkum and ultimately conquered southern China. Terrain imposed different constraints; it did not produce one uniform frontier outcome.

8. **Factual error — high confidence.**  
   > “The blue-and-white china that later filled the cupboards of the world was invented in the Mongol century, from a Persian colour and a Chinese clay.”

   Chinese blue-and-white ceramics existed in the Tang period: the ninth-century Belitung wreck—the ship introduced in chapter 33—contained examples. Yuan production brought a major flowering and commercial expansion, not the first invention. [Source: Asian Civilisations Museum curator Kan Shuyi, “Sunken Treasure.”](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/sunken-treasure-a-ninth-century-shipwreck/story)

9. **False priority claim; style-breaking absolute — high confidence.**  
   > “It was the first attempt to write the history of the whole known world, and it could have been made in few other places.”

   Universal histories long predated Rashid al-Din. Diodorus Siculus attempted a history encompassing the inhabited world in the first century BCE. Rashid al-Din’s exceptionally broad coverage of Eurasian peoples is the defensible distinction. [Source: *Oxford Classical Dictionary*, “Diodorus.”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548730850?login=false)

10. **Factual overstatement; internal contradiction — high confidence.**  
    > “Merchants were soon allowed to use the roads as well, since the Mongols had no craftsmen and no farms of their own and wanted everything that trade could bring.”

    By this point, the narrative has described Mongol-controlled workshops and the conquest of enormous agricultural territories. Rubruck also records Mongol lords receiving flour and millet from their southern villages. Distinguish pastoral households’ demand for manufactured goods from the resources of their empire. [Source: Rubruck’s account, section on diet.](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html)

    > “On the steppe there were no specialists.”

    This is also contradicted by the chapter’s subsequent “blacksmiths’ sons.” Military participation did not eliminate specialised occupations.

11. **Chronological confusion — high confidence.**  
    > “The first target was north China, which lay just across the Gobi desert and was the richest country any Mongol had heard of.”

    The following sentence identifies this target as Jin territory, but campaigns against Western Xia preceded the Jin invasion of 1211. The dam episode in the next paragraph belongs to that earlier sequence. Name Western Xia and place its siege before the Jin campaign. [Source: *The Cambridge History of the Mongol Empire*, introductory chronology.](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811084/24899/excerpt/9781108424899_excerpt.pdf)

12. **Reader continuity problem with chapter 32.**  
    > “The Jin capital fell in 1215, after a blockade that starved it.”

    Chapter 32 ends with the Mongols taking Kaifeng in 1233. Here the capital is near modern Beijing. Both events can be correct, but readers need the missing connection: the Jin moved their court from Zhongdu to Kaifeng in 1214, and resistance continued after Zhongdu fell. Otherwise this looks like a conflicting date for the conquest already described. [Earlier passage.](chapters/32-gunpowder/chapter.md:135) [Historical chronology.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_the_Jin_dynasty)

13. **Geographical imprecision — high confidence.**  
    > “At Merv and Nishapur, in the east of Iran, the people were led out onto the plain and killed, and the walls were pulled down.”

    Nishapur is in modern Iran; Merv is in modern Turkmenistan. Both belonged to historical Khorasan. If “Iran” means the wider historical region, that meaning needs explaining; otherwise use modern locations or explain Khorasan. [Source: the archaeological Merv project.](https://maxvanberchem.org/en/11-archeologie/46-the-medieval-city-of-merv)

14. **Errors in the opening eyewitness scene — high confidence.**  
    > “There was a woman from the French city of Metz who cooked him a dinner, a nephew of an English bishop, and a number of Germans who were mining gold and making weapons.”

    Rubruck does not identify the bishop as English. The German miners and weapon-makers were at Bolat: he heard about them but did not visit them. The sentence incorrectly assembles reported people into an encounter. [Source: Rubruck’s account.](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html)

    > “In another quarter of the town lived craftsmen from China, and in a third, merchants from Persia.”

    Rubruck describes two quarters, Chinese artisans and Muslim merchants; “third” and the specifically Persian identification are unsupported. [Same source.](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html)

15. **Incorrect setting and unsupported visual detail — high confidence on location; medium on height.**  
    > “What astonished him was what he found in the courtyard of the palace.”  
    > “There stood a tree made of silver, as tall as the hall.”

    Rubruck places the tree inside the palace entrance, not in its courtyard. He supplies no height matching the hall. Keep the documented setting and omit the height unless another source supports it. [Source: Rubruck’s description of the palace fountain.](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html)

16. **Doubtful food-processing detail — medium-high confidence.**  
    > “Mares were milked, and the milk was drunk fresh, dried into hard curds, or fermented into a mildly alcoholic drink.”

    This assigns the dried curds specifically to mare’s milk. Rubruck distinguishes fermented mare’s milk from hard dried curds made after processing cow’s milk. Separate the dairy products rather than attributing all three to mares. [Source: Rubruck’s account.](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html)

17. **Overstatement of scientific evidence — high confidence.**  
    > “The rings show that the fifteen years after 1211 were the wettest that Mongolia had seen in more than a thousand years.”

    The study reconstructs moisture conditions in central Mongolia and identifies 1211–1225 as an exceptionally persistent wet interval. It does not establish a uniform rainfall record for all Mongolia. “From 1211 to 1225” also avoids shifting the interval by saying “after 1211.” [Source: Pederson and colleagues, *PNAS*.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3970536/)

18. **False technological absolute — high confidence.**  
    > “Urgent news could travel more than two hundred kilometres in a day, a speed that nothing on land would beat until the railway.”

    News travelled much faster through optical telegraph networks before steam railways. Chappe’s Paris–Lille line operated in 1794. Keep the impressive relay speed; remove the universal record claim. [Source: Musée des Arts et Métiers, Chappe telegraph.](https://www.arts-et-metiers.net/musee/modele-telegraphe-optique-systeme-chappe)

19. **Reader chronology problem at the ending.**  
    > “The road that carried the goldsmith east and the monk west stayed open for a few decades more.”

    “More” has no stable starting point. The preceding paragraphs move through the 1330s, Kublai’s death in 1294 and developments roughly a century later. The flea ending invites readers to anticipate plague, but they cannot tell what decade they are in. Anchor the closing scene to a date or an unmistakable earlier event.

20. **Unexplained terminology and institutions.**  
    > “At the Mongol court in Persia, a vizier who had trained as a doctor was ordered to write the history of the Mongols.”

    “Vizier” needs its first-use explanation: a chief minister.

    > “The victors were the soldiers who ruled Egypt, most of whom had been born on the steppe themselves and sold as boys into military slavery.”

    Readers last encountered Egypt under Saladin. They need one bridging sentence explaining how an army recruited through slavery became Egypt’s ruling power. Their origins alone do not explain that transition.

21. **Direct breaks of the prohibition on signpost sentences.**  
    > “Put these together and an unusual thing follows.”

    This announces a conclusion instead of stating it.

    > “The Mongols had gathered them from the two ends of Asia in under fifty years, and the story of how they did it begins with what Mongolia does not have.”

    The second clause announces the narrative’s construction. Move directly into the grassland explanation. I found no clear hedging stacks, explicit chapter-number cross-references or literal “this chapter” references; the problems here are signposting and the specific absolutes identified above.
