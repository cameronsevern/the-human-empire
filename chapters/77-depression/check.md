Read the requested files; no files edited. Findings below are ordered by seriousness. “High confidence” means a clear error or well-supported correction; “moderate” means the wording goes beyond what the evidence establishes.

1. **Factual error — the ending misidentifies Nazi voters.**  
   > “In the country that the slump had hit hardest, six million unemployed had made their choice in the summer of 1932, at the ballot box.”

   This implies that Germany’s unemployed collectively chose Hitler. Research instead associates unemployment especially with Communist voting. The Nazis won about 37% in July 1932; Hitler became chancellor by appointment in January 1933. “Hit hardest” also needs a specified measure. Name Germany and the Nazis: the unnamed country and unnamed choice leave an inexperienced reader guessing. **High confidence.** Sources: [research on austerity and Nazi voting](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/gem/nazi_austerity.pdf), [US Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-nazi-rise-to-power).

2. **Factual error — Hoover’s spending record is reversed.**  
   > “As its tax revenues fell, it therefore cut spending, and in 1932, at the bottom of the slump, it raised taxes.”

   Federal outlays rose from approximately $3.1 billion in fiscal 1929 to $4.7 billion in 1932, before falling slightly in 1933. Hoover pursued budget economies and raised taxes, but this sentence presents falling expenditure as the general pattern. The economic trough was in 1933. **High confidence.** Sources: [official historical budget tables, Table 1.1](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BUDGET-2000-TAB/html/BUDGET-2000-TAB.htm), [Federal Reserve history](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/great-depression).

3. **Factual error — deposit insurance arrives months too early.**  
   > “Within the month Congress had passed a law under which the government insured every ordinary bank deposit.”

   The law was signed on **16 June 1933**, not within March. Insurance began on **1 January 1934**, initially covering up to **$2,500 per depositor** at participating banks. This matters because the paragraph wrongly makes insurance part of the immediate March restoration of confidence. **High confidence.** Sources: [FDIC timeline](https://www.fdic.gov/90years), [FDIC history](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/publications/brief-history-of-deposit-insurance/book/brief-history-deposit-insurance.pdf).

4. **Doubtful claim and broken absolute — Germany’s recovery.**  
   > “The fastest recovery was Germany's, where a new government from 1933 spent without limit on motorways and then on weapons, and had abolished unemployment by 1938.”

   “Fastest” depends on the measure: Japan regained its previous industrial-production peak in 1933, Germany in 1936. Germany still recorded approximately **429,000 unemployed in 1938**. “Spent without limit” is another unsustainable absolute. Describe rapid recovery and the near-elimination of *registered* unemployment, with an identified comparison if retaining the superlative. **High confidence on unemployment; moderate on the ranking.** Sources: [Christina Romer’s comparative study](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~cromer/Reprints/The%20Nation%20in%20Depression.pdf), [contemporary League of Nations survey](https://deriv.nls.uk/dcn23/1903/3888/190338883.23.pdf).

5. **Factual error and internal contradiction — failed-bank deposits did not all disappear.**  
   > “In three years, nine thousand American banks failed, and the savings in them vanished.”

   The familiar total covers **1930–1933**, four calendar years. Deposits became inaccessible and suffered losses, but liquidation often returned part of the money. Earlier, the chapter itself says the Bank of United States’ customers recovered part of their savings. Distinguish frozen deposits from permanent losses. **High confidence.** Source: [FDIC history, bank-suspension and depositor-loss tables](https://www.fdic.gov/system/files/2024-08/brhist.pdf).

6. **Doubtful causal claim — the crash could hurt people without shares.**  
   > “The crash itself need not have ruined anybody except the people who owned shares.”

   Falling consumption, reduced investment and impaired lending could destroy jobs and businesses belonging to non-shareholders even without a banking catastrophe. The defensible point is that the crash need not have produced a prolonged depression. The chapter’s subsequent account of car-factory layoffs also undermines this sentence. **High confidence.** Source: [Federal Reserve account of the crash’s effects](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/stock-market-crash-of-1929).

7. **Doubtful characterization — Hoover’s response was not simply minimal government.**  
   > “He and nearly everyone who advised him believed that they knew what a government should do in a slump, and it was very little.”

   Hoover promoted public construction, pressed employers to maintain wages and created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation to support lending institutions. Those interventions proved insufficient, but omitting them makes his policy position misleadingly simple. **High confidence on the actions; moderate on the characterization of advisers’ beliefs.** Source: [Hoover Presidential Library](https://hoover.archives.gov/exhibits/great-depression).

8. **Factual overstatement — bank runs did recur.**  
   > “Bank runs of the old kind ceased, and the United States has hardly seen one since.”

   Insurance greatly reduced runs by insured depositors; it did not remove the underlying mechanism. Silicon Valley Bank lost over $40 billion in deposits in one day in 2023. The chapter needs to distinguish protection of insured deposits from the disappearance of runs. **High confidence.** Source: [Federal Reserve review of SVB](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2023-april-svb-executive-summary.htm).

9. **Factual overstatement — Brazil still sold coffee.**  
   > “Brazil, which grew most of the world's coffee, could find no buyers for it at any price, and for years the government bought up the crop and destroyed it.”

   Brazil continued exporting coffee. The government destroyed surplus stocks to restrict supply and support prices; it did not destroy the entire crop because demand had vanished. “No buyers … at any price” also breaks the rule against vulnerable absolutes. **High confidence.** Source: [research on the coffee market and destruction programme](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/political-economy-of-commodity-cartel-formation-the-case-of-coffee-19301940/D3CAC0CBA5528066373F444161DA4FEA).

10. **Contradiction with chapter 62 — earlier demands for government action disappear.**  
    > “Before 1929, a slump was regarded as something like the weather.”

    Chapter 62 already describes government-created employment: “In Paris the new government set up public workshops for the unemployed…” It also presents competing explanations of slumps and political campaigns to change economic conditions. The Depression strengthened expectations of government responsibility; it did not create them from nothing. **High confidence.** Internal source: [chapter 62, line 92](chapters/62-marx/chapter.md:92).

11. **Factual error — the trading-volume comparison.**  
    > “Sixteen million shares were dumped in a day, three times the previous record, and the machines that printed the prices ran hours behind.”

    The previous Thursday had already seen approximately **12.9 million** shares traded. Tuesday’s approximately 16 million was about a quarter higher, not three times the record. **High confidence.** Source: [PBS crash timeline](https://cgi.pbs.org/wgbh/amex/crash/timeline/timeline2.html).

12. **Chronological error — Germany’s stabilizing currency came in 1923.**  
    > “Germany was rescued in 1924 by a new currency and by American loans.”

    The Rentenmark was introduced on **15 November 1923** and ended hyperinflation. The Reichsmark and the Dawes lending arrangements followed in 1924. The sentence merges stabilization with the subsequent financing settlement. **High confidence.** Source: [German Federal Archives](https://weimar.bundesarchiv.de/WEIMAR/DE/Content/Dokumente-zur-Zeitgeschichte/1923-11-15_waehrungsreform.html).

13. **Factual overstatement — “highest tariff” is not secure.**  
    > “In June 1930 Congress passed the highest tariff in the country's history, raising the duty on some twenty thousand imported items.”

    By average duties on dutiable imports, rates under the 1828 tariff reached approximately 61.7%, exceeding the 1932 Smoot–Hawley-era peak of 59.1%. “One of the highest” avoids a false superlative; the measure also needs distinguishing from duties averaged across *all* imports. **High confidence.** Sources: [historical trade-policy research](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-policy-history/article/institutional-foundation-of-us-trade-policy-revisiting-explanations-for-the-1934-reciprocal-trade-agreements-act/8646C9C702E567BF3D10D41A2FE4EFB8), [US International Trade Commission table](https://www.usitc.gov/documents/dataweb/ave_table_1891_2017.pdf).

14. **Quotation attribution — Mellon’s advice needs its reporting source.**  
    > “Liquidate labour, he advised, liquidate stocks, liquidate the farmers, liquidate real estate.”

    This famous wording comes from **Hoover’s later memoirs**, rather than an independently established verbatim record of Mellon speaking. “Hoover later recalled his advice…” supplies the necessary attribution without a discussion of source reliability. The outline explicitly recognized this distinction. **High confidence about provenance; uncertain about Mellon’s exact words.** Source: [Hoover’s memoirs, volume III](https://hoover.archives.gov/sites/default/files/research/ebooks/b1v3_full.pdf).

15. **Doubtful causal compression — the 1937 relapse.**  
    > “That year, thinking the emergency over, he cut spending to balance the budget, and the economy promptly fell back into recession.”

    Fiscal contraction contributed, but new payroll taxes and monetary contraction also matter. Their relative contributions remain debated. The sentence makes spending cuts sound like a complete, settled explanation. **High confidence that the explanation is incomplete; moderate on the causal weights.** Source: [Federal Reserve history of the 1937–38 recession](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/recession-of-1937-38).

16. **Misleading timescale — three million conservation workers.**  
    > “He put three million young men to work in the forests, planting trees and cutting firebreaks.”

    Approximately three million served across the CCC’s **nine-year existence**, not immediately in 1933. Following “tried everything at once,” the sentence invites the wrong reading. Add the programme’s duration. **High confidence.** Source: [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/maca/learn/historyculture/civilian-conservation-corps.htm).

17. **Misleading description — Roosevelt concealed the extent of his disability.**  
    > “The winner was the governor of New York, Franklin Roosevelt, a wealthy and cheerful man who had been paralysed from the waist down by polio and concealed the fact with steel leg braces.”

    His history of polio was public knowledge. He managed appearances to obscure the extent of his mobility limitations and dependence on a wheelchair; braces alone did not conceal “the fact.” **High confidence.** Source: [National Park Service’s account](https://www.nps.gov/places/fdr-memorial-prologue.htm).

18. **Factual qualification — exchanging a single banknote for gold.**  
    > “Anyone who held a banknote could, in principle, take it to the central bank and exchange it for a fixed weight of gold.”

    This describes the principle more broadly than actual interwar arrangements allowed. Britain’s restored standard permitted redemption in bars of approximately **400 troy ounces**, not ordinary small-note exchanges for coins. Keep the fixed-gold-value explanation, but avoid suggesting universal retail convertibility. **High confidence.** Source: [British Treasury explanation](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/683458/FOI2018-00037_Bradbury_Pound.pdf).

19. **Reader comprehension — the central mechanism lacks a step.**  
    > “The central bank raised interest rates, borrowing became dear, businesses cut back, wages and prices fell, and the gold flowed home.”

    Why does gold return? Explain that higher interest can attract foreign funds, while lower domestic spending and prices can reduce imports and encourage exports. Without that bridge, the gold standard’s role—the chapter’s central argument—remains mysterious.

20. **Reader comprehension — the central bank appears without its defining role.**  
    > “The central bank accordingly stood by while a third of the banks failed, and at one point raised interest rates to defend the currency.”

    Chapter 55 introduced the Bank of England as a lender to government, not the modern central bank’s ability to lend emergency money to commercial banks. Explain that power before blaming its non-use. Otherwise readers cannot understand what “stood by” means or why a central bank differs from the Bronx bank.

21. **Reader comprehension — deposits becoming money needs explanation.**  
    > “A country's money is mostly bank deposits, and by 1933 a third of the American money supply had ceased to exist.”

    The opening taught readers to picture savings being lent out. Now money seemingly disappears. Explain that spendable account balances count as money, and that loan repayment without replacement lending, cash hoarding and bank closures shrink those balances. Distinguish this contraction from the permanent depositor losses in item 5.

22. **Style — an absolute undermines the banking explanation.**  
    > “If everyone comes at once, no bank on Earth can pay.”

    The accurate point is that an ordinary bank cannot meet every withdrawal **from its cash reserves alone**. Emergency borrowing or outside support can change the outcome—the very remedy the chapter later says officials should have supplied. “No bank on Earth” breaks the absolute rule and obscures that distinction.

I found no clear hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit body cross-references, and no direct contradiction with chapters 55 or 72. I would not flag the chapter’s confident criticism of austerity or defence of intervention merely for taking a side.
