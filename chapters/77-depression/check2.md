## Changed facts

No existing number, name, date or quotation was removed or replaced. However, the revision adds factual claims and strengthens several existing ones. The substantive changes are below; simple sentence splits and explanations already implicit in the original are excluded. “No equivalent” means the sentence is newly added.

1. **The bank’s name caused customers’ mistaken belief.**

   Old: “Most of its hundreds of thousands of customers were immigrant families who worked in the clothing trade, and many had opened accounts in the belief that it belonged to the government.”

   New: “The name had done its work on them: many had opened accounts in the belief that the bank belonged to the government.”

   The belief is unchanged; its cause is now asserted.

2. **Gold-standard adjustment becomes automatic.**

   Old: “A government that printed too much would see people turn in its notes for gold, and when the gold ran low it would have to stop.”

   New addition: “The discipline worked by itself.”

   This strengthens an account of a constraint into a claim about automatic operation.

3. **The international lending arrangement guaranteed payment while functioning.**

   Old: “The money went round and round, and the whole arrangement depended on Americans continuing to lend.”

   New: “The money went round and round, and as long as it kept moving, everyone could pay what they owed.”

   “Everyone could pay” is an additional claim.

4. **Borrowers retained the gains on their leveraged investments.**

   Old: “While prices rose, this was a machine for getting rich.”

   New: “While prices rose, this was a machine for getting rich, because the investor kept the whole gain on the borrowed money as well as on his own.”

   This adds an explanation of leverage; it describes gross gains before borrowing costs.

5. **Delayed prices prevented sellers knowing their proceeds.**

   Old: “Sixteen million shares were dumped in a day, a record that stood for almost forty years, and the machines that printed the prices ran hours behind.”

   New: “Sixteen million shares were dumped in a day, a record that stood for almost forty years, and the machines that printed the prices ran hours behind, so that sellers could not tell what their shares were fetching.”

   The consequence is newly explicit.

6. **Most families lost nothing directly in the crash.**

   Old: “Only a small minority of Americans owned shares.”

   New: “Only a small minority of Americans owned shares, so most families lost nothing in the crash itself.”

   The new clause adds a claim about losses, rather than ownership alone.

7. **Most bankers shared Mellon’s position.**

   Old: “His secretary of the treasury, one of the richest men in America, put the orthodox view in its purest form.”

   New: “His secretary of the treasury, one of the richest men in America, put the orthodox view, the one most bankers then held, in its purest form.”

   “Most bankers” introduces a claim about the prevalence of this view.

8. **Gold prevented central-bank action.**

   Old: “The second was that the dollar must remain tied to gold.”

   New: “The second was that the dollar must remain tied to gold, and that loyalty tied the hands of the central bank.”

   Later, old: “The record bears him out, and it also shows how much the gold standard was to blame.”

   New: “It also shows how much the gold standard was to blame, since a country that was defending its gold could not lower its interest rates or spend freely.”

   The revision adds a substantially stronger constraint. See factual flag 1.

9. **Import restrictions keep domestic factories busy.**

   Old: “Each of these policies made sense for one household, one bank or one country acting alone.”

   New addition: “A country that keeps out imports keeps its own factories busy.”

   This introduces an economic outcome, rather than merely explaining policymakers’ reasoning. See factual flag 5.

10. **Austria was the first failure in the chain.**

    Old: “In May 1931 the largest bank in Austria admitted that it was insolvent.”

    New addition: “The first link to snap was in Austria.”

    The revision introduces a chronological priority claim whose geographical scope is unclear.

11. **Short-term debts could be recalled, and lenders recalled them.**

    Old: “The panic jumped to Germany, where the banks owed vast short-term sums to foreigners.”

    New addition: “Short-term debts can be called in at short notice, and frightened lenders called them in.”

    This adds both a contractual explanation and an account of lenders’ actions.

12. **Germany’s subsequent measures were demanded by gold.**

    Old: “To keep its currency on gold, it raised interest rates sharply, cut the pay of civil servants and reduced unemployment benefits.”

    New preceding sentence: “The German government then did what the gold standard demanded.”

    This reinforces the existing explanation and makes the sequence more explicit. See factual flag 2.

13. **Brazil destroyed coffee specifically to prevent a further price fall.**

    Old: “Brazil, which grew most of the world's coffee, had far more than it could sell, and for years the government bought up the surplus and destroyed it.”

    New: “Selling the surplus would only have driven the price lower still, so for years the government bought it up and destroyed it.”

    The policy’s purpose is newly explicit and consistent with the corrected account.

14. **The Soviet example became an argument against a specified economic system.**

    Old: “To a great many people in the West, standing in line for soup, that looked like an argument.”

    New: “To a great many people in the West, standing in line for soup, that looked like an argument against the whole system of private banks and free markets.”

    The object of the argument is newly specified.

15. **Fear itself causes bank failure.**

    Old: No equivalent.

    New: “The sentence went straight to the cause of the runs, since a bank fails when its customers fear that it will.”

    This adds an unconditional causal statement.

16. **Deposit insurance removed the individual incentive to run.**

    Old: “Runs on American banks by small savers all but ceased.”

    New preceding sentence: “A small saver no longer had to race his neighbours to the counter, because his money was safe even if the bank failed.”

    This adds the mechanism and an assurance of protection; the preceding coverage limit supplies an important qualification.

17. **A new relative date is supplied for Britain leaving gold.**

    Old: “He took the dollar off gold.”

    New: “He took the dollar off gold, as Britain had done two years before.”

    This is reasonable calendar-year rounding: September 1931 to spring 1933.

18. **Classical economists prescribed waiting and expected quick re-employment.**

    Old: “Economists of the old school said that slumps cured themselves in the long run.”

    New: “Economists of the old school said that slumps cured themselves in the long run, and that governments should wait.”

    Old: “If workers are idle, wages fall until it pays to hire them.”

    New: “If workers are idle, wages fall until it pays to hire them, and so the idle are soon at work again.”

    The additions introduce a policy prescription and a speed claim. “Soon” also sits awkwardly beside “in the long run.”

19. **Nobody blamed governments for earlier slumps.**

    Old: “Before 1929, most people regarded a slump as something like the weather.”

    New: “Before 1929, most people regarded a slump as something like the weather: it came, it did damage, and nobody thought to blame the government for it.”

    This reintroduces an absolute after the earlier wording had been softened. See factual flag 3.

20. **The three mechanisms reinforced one another.**

    Old: “They agree on more than they admit.”

    New: “They agree on more than they admit, since shrinking money, falling spending and the defence of gold all fed one another.”

    This adds a substantive—and defensible—explanation for the claimed agreement.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — gold constrained monetary easing; it did not make it impossible.**

   > “It also shows how much the gold standard was to blame, since a country that was defending its gold could not lower its interest rates or spend freely.”

   The new categorical prohibition is wrong. The New York Fed reduced its discount rate to 1½ per cent in May 1931 while the United States remained on gold. The Fed also undertook substantial monetary expansion in 1932. Gold could severely constrain easing, especially during reserve losses, but did not universally prevent it. Sources: [New York Fed’s 1931 annual report](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/annual-report-federal-reserve-bank-new-york-467/seventeenth-annual-report-federal-reserve-bank-new-york-year-ended-december-31-1931-17976/fulltext), [Federal Reserve account of the 1932 expansion](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/banking-panics-1931-33).

2. **Major — Germany’s post-crisis policy is confused with continued gold convertibility.**

   > “The German government then did what the gold standard demanded. To keep its currency on gold, it raised interest rates sharply, cut the pay of civil servants and reduced unemployment benefits.”

   Following the July bank closures, Germany imposed exchange controls, effectively leaving the international gold standard while maintaining its official parity. Austerity continued, but describing the subsequent package simply as keeping the currency “on gold” obscures that distinction. It also conflicts with the chapter’s chart, which identifies July 1931 as Germany’s departure. The correct distinction is between defending the mark’s official value and maintaining free gold convertibility. Source: [European Central Bank historical study](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecbwp1478.pdf).

3. **Major — the new “nobody” claim restores a previously corrected error.**

   > “Before 1929, most people regarded a slump as something like the weather: it came, it did damage, and nobody thought to blame the government for it.”

   Governments were blamed for economic distress long before 1929. During the 1890s depression, Cleveland himself blamed legislation passed under the previous administration; unemployed marchers demanded federal public works and relief. The Depression strengthened expectations of government responsibility; it did not introduce political blame for slumps. This flag concerns the newly added clause, not the earlier correction to “most people.” Source: [Miller Center, Cleveland’s domestic affairs](https://millercenter.org/president/cleveland/domestic-affairs).

4. **Minor — Keynes’s quotation is placed in the wrong original exchange.**

   > “Economists of the old school said that slumps cured themselves in the long run, and that governments should wait. In the long run, Keynes replied, we are all dead.”

   The words are authentic, but “replied” misrepresents their immediate context. Keynes wrote them in *A Tract on Monetary Reform* in 1923 while discussing the quantity theory of money and the inadequacy of long-run proportional relationships as guides to current conditions. They were not originally a reply to the particular claim about self-correcting unemployment presented here. Source: [Keynes, *A Tract on Monetary Reform*, p. 80](https://www.hetwebsite.net/het/texts/keynes/keynes1923tract.pdf).

5. **Minor — restricting imports does not necessarily keep a country’s factories busy.**

   > “A country that keeps out imports keeps its own factories busy.”

   Protection can help producers competing with imports, but it can also raise costs for domestic factories using imported materials and components. The country-wide result is therefore not guaranteed, even before retaliation. The correct claim concerns protection for particular producers, or policymakers’ intended result. Evidence of the competing mechanisms: [Federal Reserve research on manufacturing tariffs, input costs and employment](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2019086pap.pdf).

6. **Minor — instalment buying and non-mortgage household debt were not new in the 1920s.**

   > “Most of these goods were bought on a new plan.”

   > “For the first time, ordinary families were deep in debt for things other than a house, and felt prosperous.”

   These are inherited sentences, not new errors, but the earlier check did not identify them. Instalment selling had spread through sewing machines, furniture and other household purchases during the nineteenth century. Singer’s “dollar down, dollar a week” selling was established by the 1890s. The 1920s greatly expanded consumer credit; they did not introduce the practice. Source: [Harvard Business School’s history of instalment selling](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/credit/credit4b.html).

## Clarity flags

1. **New signposts conflict with the style sheet.**

   > “A few years later, that lesson would matter a great deal.”

   > “The reasoning was simple.”

   > “In practice, one lesson has stuck.”

   Each announces significance or an explanation instead of supplying it. The following sentences already do the work.

2. **The Austria transition has an unclear scope.**

   > “The first link to snap was in Austria.”

   The preceding paragraph has already said the American lending circuit stopped, and the opening describes an American bank failure in 1930. Specify that Austria begins this account of the **1931 European banking crisis**, rather than apparently starting the whole collapse.

3. **“Short-term” is confused with “callable.”**

   > “Short-term debts can be called in at short notice, and frightened lenders called them in.”

   A debt falling due soon is not necessarily repayable whenever the lender demands. Readers need the distinction between withdrawing callable funds and refusing to renew loans as they mature.

4. **The banking explanation slips back into an absolute.**

   > “The sentence went straight to the cause of the runs, since a bank fails when its customers fear that it will.”

   Fear can trigger withdrawals that overwhelm a bank’s available cash; failure is not automatic. The chapter itself explains emergency support and the restoration of confidence.

5. **“The eighth evening” loses its reference point.**

   > “On the eighth evening he spoke on the radio again…”

   With the new paragraph break and intervening bank holiday, readers may count from the closure rather than the inauguration. The intended date is 12 March 1933.

6. **The liquidation definition does not explain the quotation’s most puzzling term.**

   > “To liquidate something is to sell it off for whatever it will fetch.”

   Applied to “liquidate labour,” this sounds like selling workers. Explain the intended economic meaning—allowing employment, wages and failing businesses to collapse—rather than giving only the asset-sale meaning.

7. **Some additions repeat information without adding a useful step.**

   > “The money that had gone round from America to Germany, to Britain and France, and back again, stopped moving.”

   This repeats the immediately preceding statement that the debt circle had broken and American banks stopped lending. Likewise, “He also put the idle to work” immediately precedes “He sent young men to work in the forests…”

8. **There is conspicuous repeated wording.**

   > “…those who come to withdraw on an ordinary day. On an ordinary day that is enough…”

   The repeated phrase draws attention to the prose rather than the explanation.

9. **Several surviving absolutes remain vulnerable.**

   > “The cure for a loss of gold was always the same…”

   > “After it, voters in every democracy held the government responsible for whether they had work, and no government has been allowed to forget it.”

   > “No central banker since has knowingly let the banking system go down.”

   These turn broad historical tendencies into universal claims. The last also leaves “go down” undefined and asserts knowledge of every central banker’s intentions.

10. **The ending withholds a necessary name.**

    > “…the party that promised to sweep the whole system away had become the largest in parliament in the summer of 1932.”

    The earlier factual error about unemployed voters has been fixed. The remaining clarity problem is that a reader without historical background may not know this means the Nazi Party.

No hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit body cross-references stood out.

## Overall

The revision generally improves the explanations, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. Several bridges introduce stronger claims, especially about gold, government responsibility and economic mechanisms. Correct those and trim the signposts; the chapter’s chosen interpretation can remain confident. No files were edited.
