1. **Factual error; central premise — high confidence.** “Arabia had no king, no tax collector, no police and no courts, and the question of what kept anyone safe had one answer, which was kinship.”

   This describes the whole peninsula as stateless. Southern Arabia had kingdoms, followed in the sixth century by Aksumite and Persian rule; eastern Arabia also experienced Persian government. Limit the claim to the absence of a central state in the western Arabian setting. [Cambridge history, discussion of sixth-century Arabia](https://cristoraul.org/english/readinghall/thirdmillenniumlibrary/Medieval-History-Chapters/PDF/NCMedH_1-500-700.pdf).

2. **Faulty inference from evidence — high confidence.** “The pages therefore come from his own lifetime or from the dozen years after it.”

   Radiocarbon dating dates the animal’s death, not the writing. Parchment could have been stored before use, so the result does not establish that the text was copied by 645. The range also begins before Muhammad’s conventional birth date. Say that the *parchment* dates close to his lifetime; distinguish that from the manuscript’s date. [University of Birmingham’s manuscript FAQ](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/facilities/cadbury/birmingham-quran-mingana-collection/birmingham-quran/faqs).

3. **Factual overstatement; internal contradiction — high confidence.** “A widow, an orphan, a slave or a stranger had nobody who would pick up a sword on their account, and could be cheated or beaten at no risk.”

   Those statuses did not automatically remove kinship or patronage protection. Muhammad is the chapter’s own counterexample: an orphan protected by his grandfather and uncle. Khadija likewise appears as a wealthy widow with considerable independence. The defensible claim concerns people **without effective protectors**, not all widows and orphans. [Encyclopaedia Iranica on Muhammad’s care by Abu Talib](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ali-b-abi-taleb/ali-b-abi-taleb-i-life/). The heading “A man without a clan” also misdirects readers before the narrative reaches his loss of protection.

4. **Misrepresentation of the Medina agreement — high confidence.** “It depended on a declaration that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is his messenger, and anyone at all could make it.”

   The preceding subject is membership in the agreement’s community. Its Jewish participants retained their religion; they did not have to make this declaration. The charter also retained tribal groups and their existing compensation obligations. Distinguish the Muslim religious community from the broader political agreement, and describe belief as extending allegiance beyond kinship rather than simply replacing it. [Medina charter, especially clauses 3–11 and 25](https://constitution.org/1-Constitution/cons/medina/con_medina.htm).

5. **False absolute supporting the argument — high confidence.** “A feud of that kind had no way to stop, since each side owed the other a death.”

   Pre-Islamic feuds could end through negotiated compensation and arbitration. Muhammad’s achievement was not the invention of a previously unavailable means of settlement. Say that this feud had resisted settlement. [Joseph Schacht, *An Introduction to Islamic Law*, discussion of the pre-Islamic background](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/Library/Schacht%2C%20J%20-%20An%20Introduction.pdf).

6. **Overstated account of the sources — high confidence.** “The detailed story of Muhammad's life comes from biographies composed by Muslim scholars more than a century after he died, out of reports that had been handed down by word of mouth for four or five generations.”

   The surviving extended biographies are late, but their antecedents were not exclusively oral. Earlier written material includes letters attributed to ʿUrwa ibn al-Zubayr, preserved in later works. The correction is that the biographies drew on earlier **oral and written** reports. [Görke and Schoeler, *The Earliest Writings on the Life of Muḥammad*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/earliest-writings-on-the-life-of-muhammad/2D4419CF178C7984D24ECD92806C73F0/listing).

7. **Disputed historical claim presented as settled — high confidence about the dispute.** “He left no son, and he had named no successor.”

   “No surviving son” is sound. The second clause adopts one interpretation of a contested succession history: Shia tradition understands the declaration at Ghadir Khumm as naming Ali; Sunni tradition generally does not. This is not objectionable merely because it takes a historical position, but the chapter supplies no basis for settling the dispute. “He left no generally acknowledged successor” states the political situation without doing so. [Encyclopaedia Iranica on Ali and the succession](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ali-b-abi-taleb/ali-b-abi-taleb-i-life/).

8. **Another disputed tradition presented as universal — high confidence about the dispute.** “His uncle, the head of his clan, never accepted the new teaching, but he refused to withdraw his protection from his nephew, and to harm Muhammad would therefore have meant a feud with the whole clan.”

   Abu Talib’s unbelief is the predominant Sunni account; Shia traditions affirm his faith. His protection is the relevant historical point and does not require resolving his religious allegiance. This deserves attribution or omission, particularly after the opening promises “the Muslim tradition” as though it were a single account. [Shia traditions concerning Abu Talib’s faith](https://al-islam.org/mujam-ahadith-al-shia/chapter-faith-abu-talib).

9. **False absolute, contradicted by the tradition being followed — high confidence.** “They were unable to touch Muhammad himself.”

   Clan protection deterred attacks but did not make him physically untouchable. A report in Bukhari describes an attacker throttling him while he prayed, until Abu Bakr intervened. Describe the risk of retaliation rather than immunity from assault. [*Sahih al-Bukhari* 3678](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/62/28).

10. **Chronological error — high confidence within the conventional chronology.** “For some ten years the stalemate lasted.”

    The preceding narrative starts public preaching after three private years, conventionally around 613. Khadija and Abu Talib died around 619: roughly six years later, not ten. About ten years fits the period from the first revelations, which is a different starting point. [Marshall Hodgson’s chronology in *The Venture of Islam*](https://sites.la.utexas.edu/mhc/files/2009/10/hodgson-venture146-195.pdf).

11. **Misstatement caused by word order — high confidence.** “They limited a man to four wives and told him to take one only if he could not treat several fairly.”

    “Take one only if” makes inability to treat several fairly a condition for marrying one. The intended meaning is **take only one if** he feared unequal treatment. That is what the verse says. [Qur’an 4:3](https://quran.com/an-nisa/3).

12. **False absolute — high confidence.** “Muhammad marched on Mecca with ten thousand men, and the town did not resist.”

    The conquest met limited resistance: Khalid’s column fought its way in. “Little resistance” preserves the point without erasing the fighting. [W. Montgomery Watt, *Muhammad: Prophet and Statesman*, p. 205](https://www.emaanlibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/Watt-Muhammad-Prophet-and-Statesman-1961.pdf).

13. **Doubtful identification of a witness — high confidence about the attribution problem.** “A Christian writing in Palestine within a few years of Muhammad's death mentions a prophet who has appeared among the Arabs, and an Armenian bishop a generation later knows that he was a merchant named Muhammad who taught his people about the God of Abraham.”

    The Armenian history is conventionally called “Sebeos,” but its identification with that bishop is insecure; modern scholarship commonly treats it as anonymous. “An Armenian chronicler” keeps the useful evidence without making the disputed identification. [Stephen Shoemaker, *A Prophet Has Appeared*, discussion of “Sebeos”](https://dokumen.pub/a-prophet-has-appeared-the-rise-of-islam-through-christian-and-jewish-eyes-a-sourcebook-9780520971271.html).

14. **Chronological disorientation, especially after chapter 27 — high confidence.** “To the north lay the two great empires, Rome and Persia, which were then fighting the longest and most destructive of all their wars, and which paid Arab chiefs on the desert's edge to guard their frontiers.”

    The last explicit setting is “the sixth century,” followed shortly by Muhammad’s birth. The intended final Roman–Persian war began in **602**. Anchor this sentence to the years when Muhammad began preaching. Otherwise readers may place the war around 570 or understand it as the immediate continuation of chapter 27’s ending. [Hodgson’s comparative chronology](https://sites.la.utexas.edu/mhc/files/2009/10/hodgson-venture146-195.pdf).

15. **Ambiguous comparison with chapter 25 — high confidence about the reading problem.** “They also condemn a practice that the Arabs shared with the Romans.”

    The next sentence identifies burial alive, whereas [chapter 25](chapters/25-christianity/chapter.md) describes infant exposure: babies left outdoors. If the shared practice means infanticide broadly, say so. As written, the connection can suggest that burying unwanted daughters alive was the Roman practice already described. This is an ambiguity, not a demonstrated contradiction between the chapters.

16. **Reader loses the distinction between a rule and its observance.** “The area of peace grew with every agreement, and the tribes still outside it found themselves facing a larger and larger alliance that did not fight among itself.”

    The explanation moves from a prohibition on internal raiding to a claim that internal fighting did not occur. The agreement establishes duties; it does not establish perfect compliance. Its provisions for murder and serious disputes make that distinction particularly relevant. Keep the explanation about mutual protection and obligations. [Medina charter, clauses 21 and 42](https://constitution.org/1-Constitution/cons/medina/con_medina.htm).

17. **Unexplained historical term.** “In addition, the people who gathered round him were mostly young, and many were poor, clients of other clans or slaves, and the message they were hearing was that the rich would answer for their riches.”

    “Clients” sounds like customers to a general reader. Explain here that these were people dependent on another clan’s patronage or protection. This breaks the rule requiring historical terms to be explained on first appearance.

18. **Self-reference and signposting.** “What follows is the story as the Muslim tradition tells it, which is the only detailed version there is.”

    “What follows” announces the chapter’s own procedure, contrary to the prohibition on self-reference and signpost sentences. The necessary qualification about the evidence can be stated directly without announcing the coming narrative. “The Muslim tradition” also obscures the divergent accounts noted above.

19. **Signpost sentence.** “The earliest of them are short, rhythmic and urgent, and their message can be stated briefly.”

    The first clause describes the recitations; the second announces an impending summary. Remove the announcement and let the message follow.

I found no clear direct contradiction with chapters 14, 25 or 27, no unmistakable hedging stacks, and no explicit chapter cross-references. Nor did I find an unambiguous narrator assertion settling supernatural truth: the declarations about God and judgment are framed as the recitations’ message, and the final declaration is attributed to Abu Bakr. Items 7–8 concern contested historical traditions, rather than confidence alone or necessarily a profession of religious belief. No files were edited.
