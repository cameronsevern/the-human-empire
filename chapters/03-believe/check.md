## 1. Factual flags

1. **“Nobody works that hard for a corpse” / “who had gone somewhere that fine clothes and spears would be useful” / “a grave … says that the person has gone somewhere.”**  
   Elaborate burial demonstrates that the dead mattered to the living; it does not establish belief in an afterlife. Grief, remembrance, social status and obligations to the deceased can also explain costly funerals and grave goods. An afterlife interpretation is plausible, but the chapter repeatedly presents it as something the evidence proves. **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

2. **“These were not rich people with time to waste … every hour spent drilling ivory was an hour not spent finding food.”**  
   Hunting and gathering does not imply continuous subsistence pressure or an absence of surplus time and resources. The wealth and social organization represented by the Sunghir burials remain debated, and the beads could have accumulated during the owners’ lives. The passage builds its argument on an unsupported picture of people sacrificing desperately needed food-gathering time. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

3. **“An old Neanderthal man, toothless and crippled with arthritis … could not have chewed his own food for years … Somebody had fed him.”**  
   The La Chapelle-aux-Saints individual had lost many teeth, but was not completely toothless. His pathology does not establish that he could not chew or obtain food independently; assistance is a reasonable interpretation, not a demonstrated necessity. “Crippled” also overstates what can securely be inferred about his mobility. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

4. **“The painters worked by the light of small stone lamps … Nobody could have stood back to admire the result.”**  
   Palaeolithic cave users employed torches and fires as well as fat lamps. Lighting experiments and archaeological evidence do not support the assertion that viewing paintings from a distance was impossible. Restricted illumination would affect how pictures appeared, but does not establish that they could not be contemplated. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

5. **“With a man’s body and arms” / “others cared enough to … hide it at the back of a cave.”**  
   The Löwenmensch combines human and feline features, but its human sex is disputed. Its discovery location also does not establish deliberate concealment: placement, storage, deposition and subsequent disturbance are different possibilities. Both details turn interpretations into events the narrator claims to know. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

6. **“Among peoples who had been out of touch with one another for tens of thousands of years.”**  
   The societies being compared were not all isolated survivals from the Palaeolithic. Hunter-gatherer populations participated in migration, exchange and contact with neighbouring societies, including farmers and herders. Similar beliefs can reflect inheritance, transmission or independent development; the claimed isolation cannot establish their antiquity. **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

7. **“Every such people has someone whose job is to deal with the unseen.”**  
   Specialist healers and ritual practitioners are widespread, but not universal in the form described. Ritual knowledge and dealings with spirits can be distributed among ordinary members of a community rather than assigned to a distinct specialist. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

8. **“There is no great god in the sky who made the rules and watches to see whether you keep them” / “The watching, judging god arrives much later, together with kings and cities.”**  
   Some hunter-gatherer traditions include creator or high gods with moral concerns, and other supernatural beings can enforce social conduct. The origins and spread of moralizing gods—and their relationship to political complexity—remain disputed. A categorical sequence from morally indifferent spirits to urban, judging gods is not established. **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

9. **“Hungry people do not keep up anything so expensive for tens of thousands of years unless it gives something back.”**  
   Persistence and cost do not demonstrate that a practice provides compensating benefits. Cultural practices can persist through inherited convention, unequal benefits, coercion or as by-products of other capacities. The sentence treats an adaptive explanation of religion as a necessary conclusion. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

10. **“A band that believed together shared more, quarrelled less and held together better … the believing bands were the ones that survived.”**  
    Experiments on synchrony and comparisons of historical communes do not establish this evolutionary history. Religious practices can support cooperation, but the claim that differential survival of believing bands explains their prevalence is a contested hypothesis; neither reduced quarrelling nor the implied elimination of nonbelieving bands is demonstrated. **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

11. **“Nobody walked those distances to fetch a shell. The shells moved from hand to hand along chains of friends, exactly as beads still move in the Kalahari.”**  
    Distant material sources demonstrate movement, not a unique mechanism of movement. Direct procurement, seasonal mobility, marriage-related movement and exchange can all contribute. Gift partnerships are a useful analogy, but archaeological distance alone cannot establish that particular system or rule out long journeys. **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

12. **“Figures of women with heavy breasts and hips, with no feet and no faces.”**  
    Palaeolithic female figurines vary substantially. Some are slender, some have feet, and some have facial features; the Siberian examples are especially poorly represented by this uniform description. These are tendencies in part of the material, not defining characteristics of the whole distribution. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

13. **“Those who carved them never met and could not have understood one another’s speech … they must have shared something of what it meant.”**  
    We cannot reconstruct the makers’ individual encounters or mutual linguistic intelligibility. Similar forms suggest connections or shared conventions in some cases, but do not require identical or overlapping meanings across the entire geographical and chronological range. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

14. **“This solved a problem that no other animal has solved. A chimpanzee that meets a strange chimpanzee from another troop will flee or attack.”**  
    Chimpanzee encounters between communities are often hostile, but “flee or attack” is not an exceptionless rule, particularly around female transfer. More decisively, bonobos can associate and cooperate across community boundaries. Human symbolic cooperation is distinctive in scale and form, but peaceful dealings with outsiders are not exclusively human. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

15. **“In 1994 a German archaeologist walked over it … and began to dig.”**  
    Klaus Schmidt recognized the site’s significance in 1994; the excavation programme began in 1995. The sentence merges those events into one year. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

16. **“The people who built them were hunters. They had no farms.”**  
    Hunting wild animals is well supported, but it does not exclude cultivating plants. Göbekli Tepe belongs to a period of developing cultivation and changing subsistence practices; the extent of cultivation among its builders remains debated. Absence of domesticated livestock cannot establish absence of farming. **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

17. **“Every pillar was … hauled several hundred metres by ropes and muscle.”**  
    Quarrying occurred on the limestone plateau around the site, and transport distances differed. Neither a journey of several hundred metres for every pillar nor the precise hauling equipment is established. These are plausible reconstructions presented as documented particulars. **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

18. **“That takes hundreds of people. They had to come together from camps scattered over a wide country … because they believed in the same thing strongly enough to give months of their lives to it.”**  
    Labour estimates depend on methods and organization; hundreds of simultaneous workers are not a demonstrated requirement. Evidence for domestic occupation also challenges the picture of an exclusively ceremonial destination supplied by distant camps. The workers’ origins, recruitment, duration of service and common religious motivation cannot be reconstructed this precisely. **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

19. **“Göbekli Tepe turns that order upside down. Here the great shrine came first.”**  
    The interpretation of Göbekli Tepe as a shrine preceding settlement and farming is contested. Its monumental buildings existed within a settlement context, and sedentary communities elsewhere in Southwest Asia already predated fully developed agriculture. The site does not establish a simple replacement sequence in which temples came first and generated villages and farming. **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

20. **“Thirty kilometres away is a range of hills … scientists compared the DNA of farmed wheat from all over the world … the closest match was the wild wheat of those hills.”**  
    This appears to refer to research connecting domesticated **einkorn** with wild populations around Karaca Dağ, generally described as roughly 90 kilometres from Göbekli Tepe, rather than 30. The finding concerned a particular wheat lineage, not farmed wheat collectively, and does not establish that provisioning Göbekli Tepe caused its domestication. **Confidence: medium. Severity: major.**

21. **“Hundreds of people cannot gather on one ridge season after season … without stripping the country of game. Sooner or later someone has to find another way of feeding the crowd.”**  
    Such gatherings do not inevitably exhaust local game: that depends on population size, duration, animal movements, provisioning territory and other foods. The implied progression from ceremonial gatherings to resource depletion to agriculture is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated ecological necessity or established history of this site. **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

## 2. Clarity flags

- **“About thirty-four thousand years ago … in what is now Russia.”** Name Sunghir. Without the site name, readers cannot readily distinguish this specific burial evidence from a composite reconstruction.

- **“In a cave in Israel” / “In the same caves.”** The unexplained shift from singular to plural obscures whether the burial, ochre and perforated shells came from the same cave, different caves or the same archaeological layer.

- **“Nothing in it can be dug up. However, the things it made them do can.”** The referent of “it” is slippery: the imagined world, the shared belief or the mental picture. The intended distinction is between beliefs and their material traces.

- **“The world is full of persons.”** Explain that “person” here means a being understood to have intentions and social relationships; an unfamiliar reader may take it to mean a human being in disguise.

- **“Human food consists entirely of souls.”** The statement needs a brief explanation that eating animals was understood as taking beings with spiritual lives, creating obligations or danger.

- **“A game in which each can either share or cheat.”** A concrete description of what participants gave, kept or contributed would make the experimental evidence intelligible. “Share” and “cheat” are not self-explanatory experimental categories.

- **“The religious ones lasted far longer.”** Clarify what surviving means: remaining a communal organization, retaining members or continuing to occupy a settlement. Those are different outcomes.

- **“Beliefs and symbols were what held that larger thing together.”** “That larger thing” could mean the seasonal gathering, an enduring regional network or a common cultural identity. Specify which relationship the figurines are supposed to demonstrate.

## 3. Overall

The chapter repeatedly treats evidence of symbolic behaviour as proof of particular supernatural beliefs.  
Its account of recent hunter-gatherer societies combines diverse traditions into an overly uniform ancestral religion.  
The proposed links between religion, cooperation and group survival are plausible hypotheses presented as established history.  
The Göbekli Tepe ending depends on disputed claims about subsistence, settlement, labour and the origins of cultivation.
