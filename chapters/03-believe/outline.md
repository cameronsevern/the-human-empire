## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Early humans believed in things they could not see: that the dead go somewhere, that animals and places have spirits, that the world was made by ancestors and runs by rules. They left no words, but they left graves, paintings and carvings that cost enormous effort and fed nobody. Shared belief did practical work. It bound a band together, and it let people who had never met trust each other, so that the web of cooperation grew from dozens to thousands. Before anyone farmed, belief was already strong enough to move ten-ton stones.

## The story

1. **Thirteen thousand beads.** Answer first: they believed in things they could not see. Then the proof. About 34,000 years ago at Sungir, in Russia, a man and two children were buried under red ochre with some thirteen thousand beads carved from mammoth ivory, each bead about an hour's work. That is years of labour put into the ground with the dead. No animal does anything like it, and nobody does it without believing something.

2. **The dead.** Burial is the oldest sign. People were laid in graves with red ochre and shells a hundred thousand years ago, and Neanderthals buried their dead too. What a grave implies: the body still matters, the person has gone somewhere, and the living owe them something.

3. **Paintings in the dark.** Deep in caves, far from daylight and from where anyone lived, people painted animals: lions, rhinos and horses at Chauvet in France 36,000 years ago, and a wild pig in Indonesia older still. They pressed their hands to the rock and blew pigment around them. They made flutes from bird bones. And they carved a figure with a man's body and a lion's head, a thing that has never existed. Here is the "talk about things that are not there" from chapter 1, made solid.

4. **A world full of persons.** What did it mean? The best guide is the beliefs of foraging peoples recorded in recent centuries, which are remarkably alike across continents. Animals, rivers, storms and mountains are persons with wills of their own. The hunted animal must be thanked. The dead are still around. Some people can travel to the spirit world in a trance and come back with a cure or a warning. There are no priests, no temples, and no god who watches whether you are good. Those come later, with kings and cities.

5. **Why belief pays.** A shared story does work. Singing, dancing and moving in time together make people trust each other. A rule said to come from the ancestors is obeyed without anyone having to enforce it. A hard initiation proves that a young man is committed. A band that believes together shares more, quarrels less and holds together in a bad year. Belief also answers the questions a big-brained animal cannot help asking: why did my child die, where did she go.

6. **Friends you have never met.** Belief and its symbols reached beyond the band. Seashells are found hundreds of kilometres inland, passed from hand to hand. Among the Ju/'hoansi every adult keeps gift partners in distant camps, and can go to them when the local food fails. Bands gathered in the good season to trade, arrange marriages, dance and settle scores. Small carved figures of women are found, in the same style, from France to Siberia. People who shared beads, songs and stories could recognise each other as the same kind of people. This is the first time humans could cooperate with strangers, and everything larger in the book is built on it.

7. **The hill of stones.** End at Göbekli Tepe in Turkey, built about 11,600 years ago. Rings of stone pillars taller than a man, the largest weighing more than ten tons, carved with foxes, snakes, scorpions and vultures. The builders were hunters. They had no farms, no pottery, no metal and no draft animals. Hundreds of people had to gather, be fed and work together for months, and what they built was not a house. The temple came before the town. And a short walk away, wild wheat grows on the hills.
