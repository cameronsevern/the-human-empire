## Factual flags

1. **Minor — “Around them they placed spears carved from mammoth tusk, bracelets, and the teeth of hundreds of foxes.”**  
   This confuses the number of teeth with the number of animals. The published inventory records hundreds of fox **canines**, including more than 250 in one belt; it does not establish hundreds of individual foxes. The correct description is “hundreds of fox teeth.” [Trinkaus and Buzhilova, *Antiquity*](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2017.223).

2. **Minor — “The oldest of those traces are graves.”**  
   In context, “those traces” includes material evidence of symbolic thought, such as ornaments. Shell beads from Bizmoune Cave date to at least 142,000 years ago, preceding the roughly 100,000-year-old graves introduced here. Graves are among the early traces, not the oldest known symbolic objects. [Sehasseh et al., *Science Advances*](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abi8620).

3. **Minor — “No one ever saw a whole wall at once.”**  
   The addition of torches fixes the earlier lighting omission, but this remaining claim contradicts experimental evidence about their reach. Reconstructed torches illuminated spaces almost six metres across; visibility depended on wall size, distance and lighting arrangements. Limited lighting did not make seeing an entire wall universally impossible. [Medina-Alcaide et al., *PLOS ONE*](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250497).

4. **Major — “This solved a problem that defeats our closest relatives.”**  
   Narrowing the following example to male chimpanzees does not fix this broader statement. Bonobos are also our closest living relatives, and wild bonobos cooperate across group boundaries, including grooming, food sharing and forming coalitions. Human symbolic networks differ in scale and mechanism; cooperation beyond one’s group does not defeat all our closest relatives. [Samuni and Surbeck, *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37972165/).

5. **Minor — “There are no temples.”**  
   This contradicts the chapter’s own later account: “Here the great shrine came first, raised by people who still hunted for a living.” As written, the first sentence excludes ritual buildings from forager societies, while the ending makes one central to the argument. The consistent fact is that hunting and gathering could coexist with substantial communal ritual architecture. [German Archaeological Institute’s Göbekli Tepe project](https://www.dainst.org/projekt/-/project-display/260972).

6. **Major — “That takes a great many hands, far more than one band could supply.”**  
   Removing “hundreds” has not fixed the claimed physical necessity. Published engineering estimates put pillar transport within the capacity of much smaller teams and challenge the assertion that a large outside workforce was required. Construction required coordinated labour; its scale does not establish that one local community was insufficient. [Banning, *Open Archaeology*, section 3.3](https://doi.org/10.1515/opar-2022-0317).

7. **Major — “Here the great shrine came first, raised by people who still hunted for a living.”**  
   In the preceding sequence—farming, villages, temples—“came first” implies the shrine preceded settlement. Excavations have identified domestic buildings and activities alongside the monumental architecture. The excavation institute now describes Göbekli Tepe as a settlement with a pronounced ritual component. Hunting remains compatible with that finding; a shrine-before-village sequence does not. [German Archaeological Institute](https://www.dainst.org/projekt/-/project-display/260972).

## Clarity flags

- **“Nothing in it can be dug up. However, the things it made them do can…”**  
  “It” shifts between a worldview and its imagined contents. Actions cannot literally be excavated either; their material traces can.

- **“Several things about these paintings are strange, and each one rules out an easy explanation.”**  
  This announces forthcoming points, contrary to the style sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences. The following “First…” already supplies the structure.

- **“However, there is another way in.”**  
  Another announcement rather than an explanation. The next paragraph directly introduces the ethnographic comparison and can carry the transition.

- **“Set strangers to walk in step or sing in time for a few minutes, then give them a game in which each can either share or cheat…”**  
  “Share or cheat” leaves the experiment unclear. Briefly explain what participants could contribute or keep.

- **“The religious ones lasted far longer, and the religious ones that demanded the most of their members…”**  
  “The religious ones” repeats awkwardly. “Lasted” also needs a clear meaning: continued operating as communal organizations.

- **“Beliefs and symbols were what held that larger thing together.”**  
  “That larger thing” could mean the temporary gathering or an enduring network of bands. The distinction matters to the argument.

- **“The Ju/'hoansi of the Kalahari show how it works.”**  
  Readers have already met the San, but are not told how the Ju/'hoansi relate to them. A brief identification would prevent the impression of an unrelated new example.

## Overall

The chapter has a clear narrative and several earlier corrections now work. Its confident interpretations need not be softened merely for being interpretations. The substantive remaining problems are the Göbekli Tepe settlement and labour claims, the ape comparison, and several smaller factual or internal inconsistencies. No files were edited.
