# 48. Why did the factory run on coal?

*Britain, about 1700 to 1850, with links to the American South and India*

## A terrible machine

In 1712 an ironmonger named Thomas Newcomen built a machine beside a coal mine in the English Midlands. It was the size of a house. It boiled water, and used the steam to rock a huge wooden beam up and down, a dozen times a minute, pumping water out of the mine.

By any sensible measure it was a terrible machine. Of every hundred shovelfuls of coal fed into it, about ninety-nine did nothing but warm the sky. Almost anywhere else on Earth it would have been a joke. At a coal mine it was a bargain, because a mine is heaped with small broken coal that is hard to sell.

That is how the age of steam began: with a machine so wasteful that it was mostly useful to the people digging up its fuel. For more than a century afterwards, waterwheels still did more of Britain's work than steam did.

The mine owners who bought the thing were not dreaming of a new age. They had a wet hole in the ground. The coal near the surface had been dug out long before, and the deeper the miners followed the seam, the more water they had to lift and the further they had to lift it. The usual answer was horses. Teams of them walked in a circle all day, turning a drum that hauled up buckets on a rope. A deep, wet mine could keep dozens of horses at work, and horses eat whether the mine is making money or not. Plenty of good coal lay under water because nobody could afford to reach it.

Newcomen's engine ate something cheaper than oats. It ate the rubbish of the mine itself.

To see why that mattered so much, forget the engine for a moment and think about where people had always got their energy.

Before coal, almost all of it came from sunlight that had fallen in recent years. Plants caught the light and turned it into grain, grass and wood. People ate the grain. Horses and oxen ate the grass and gave back muscle. Wood, which is a few decades of sunshine stacked up in a trunk, cooked the dinner and smelted the iron. Wind and falling water are sunlight too, at one remove, since the sun drives the weather.

This arrangement had a ceiling, and the ceiling was land. A field can grow wheat for bread, or hay for horses, or trees for the fire, but not all three at once. A kingdom that wanted more iron had to burn more charcoal, which meant more woodland, which meant less farmland, which meant less food. Societies could grow richer for a while by farming better and trading more. Then they ran out of acres. Every great civilisation before 1700 had lived under that ceiling, however clever its people were.

England had already started to cheat. London was one of the largest cities in Europe, and there was nothing like enough woodland near it to keep its hearths warm. So Londoners burned coal, shipped down the coast from the mines around Newcastle. It was dirty and it stank, and visitors complained about the air, but it was cheap. By 1700 Britain was digging roughly three million tons of coal a year, and coal supplied about half of all the energy the English used.

Coal is sunlight as well, but very old sunlight: forests that grew and fell hundreds of millions of years ago, pressed into black rock. Burning it was like finding a second country buried under the first one, a country made entirely of firewood.

So when Newcomen arrived there was already a big coal industry, and it had a water problem. His engine did not create the appetite for coal. It served it. What was new was something else. For as long as people had lit fires, fire had given them heat. This machine took heat and turned it into steady movement. A lump of coal could now do the work of a horse.

## The weight of the air

The odd thing about the steam engine is that steam did not push it. Air did.

Picture a huge bicycle pump standing on end, with its top open to the sky. That is the cylinder, and inside it a piston slides up and down. Underneath sits a boiler, which is little more than a giant kettle. A chain runs from the top of the piston up to one end of a great wooden beam, balanced in the middle like a seesaw. From the other end of the beam, heavy rods hang down the mine shaft to the pumps at the bottom.

The weight of those rods pulls their end of the beam down, which lifts the piston to the top of the cylinder. As it rises, steam from the boiler flows in underneath and fills the space. Then a jet of cold water is sprayed inside. The steam collapses at once into a few drops of water, and where there was a cylinder full of steam there is now almost nothing at all.

Above the piston is the whole atmosphere, miles of air pressing down. Below it is next to nothing. So the air shoves the piston down, hard. The piston drags its end of the beam down with it, the far end swings up, and the rods lift a load of water out of the mine. Then steam is let in again, the rods sink, the piston rises, and the whole thing repeats, a dozen times a minute, with a great deal of creaking and hissing.

*[Diagram: the Newcomen engine, one cycle in four steps]*

That was the trick. The steam was only a way of making emptiness, so that the weight of the air could do the work.

It was also the flaw. Every squirt of cold water chilled the whole iron cylinder, and the next puff of steam had to heat it up again before it could fill it. Most of the coal went on warming and re-warming a metal tube. Nobody at the mine minded very much. The coal was lying around in heaps.

Who thought of such a thing? Not a scientist. Newcomen sold iron goods in Dartmouth, a small port in the south-west of England, and he spent years tinkering with his engine alongside a local plumber.

Learned men had in fact been excited about air and emptiness for two generations. An Italian had shown that air has weight, and wrote that "We live submerged at the bottom of an ocean of air". A German mayor had fitted two metal bowls together, pumped the air from between them, and invited teams of horses to pull them apart. They could not. A Frenchman had even built a small model in which condensing steam drew a piston down a tube.

Whether the ironmonger knew any of this is not known. A story that he wrote to one of the great scientists in London was told long after his death, and nobody has been able to find the letters. He may have read about the experiments. He may have worked it out at his own bench, with pipes and solder and a lot of failures.

And the science of heat itself was missing. Nobody could yet say how much work a fire ought to be able to do, or why Newcomen's engine did so little of it. That rule was worked out in the following century, by people who sat and puzzled over engines that were already running. We usually imagine that science discovers and engineers then build. Here the machine came first and the theory followed, more than a hundred years behind.

## Where coal cost money

Newcomen's engine spread. Within a couple of generations hundreds had been built, and most of them stood where you would expect, at coal mines, burning the stuff nobody wanted.

But coal mines were not the only wet holes in Britain. Far in the south-west, in Cornwall, miners dug tin and copper from hard rock beside the sea, and their mines flooded too. Cornwall had no coal at all. Every ton came by ship from Wales, and it was expensive. The Cornish bought Newcomen engines because they had no choice, and then watched them eat the profits. Here, at last, was a place where waste hurt.

The man who did something about it lived at the other end of the country. James Watt made scientific instruments for the University of Glasgow. In the winter of 1763 to 1764 he was handed a small model of a Newcomen engine that the university used for teaching and that would not run properly. He mended it. Then he could not stop thinking about how much steam it swallowed.

He saw what the mine owners had not needed to see. The engine was asking one piece of iron to do two opposite jobs. The cylinder had to be hot, so that steam could fill it, and a moment later cold, so that the steam would collapse. Hot, cold, hot, cold, twelve times a minute. Nearly all the fuel went into that quarrel.

Two years after mending the model, Watt had his answer, and it was simple enough to say in five words. Do the cooling somewhere else. He added a second, smaller chamber beside the cylinder, kept cold all the time, and joined the two with a pipe and a valve. When the valve opened, the steam rushed across into the cold chamber and collapsed there. The main cylinder never had to be chilled, so it could be wrapped up and kept hot all day.

*[Diagram: Watt's separate condenser beside the Newcomen cylinder]*

An idea is not an engine. It took Watt eleven more years to sell one. He ran out of money, his first backer went bankrupt, and for a time he earned his living surveying canals. The stubbornest problem was the cylinder itself. His design only worked if the piston fitted snugly, and nobody in Britain could make a large iron tube that was truly round inside. Newcomen's builders had stuffed the gaps with rope and poured water on top.

The answer came from the weapons trade. In 1774 an ironmaster built a new machine for boring out the barrels of cannon, one that held its cutting tool rigid at both ends so that it cut straight and true. A cannon barrel and an engine cylinder are much the same thing, an iron tube that must be smooth and round. The same machine bored Watt's cylinders, and the piston finally fitted.

By then Watt had found a partner, a Birmingham manufacturer named Matthew Boulton, who made buttons and buckles and silverware and had the two things Watt lacked: money and cheerfulness. Boulton understood exactly what they had. When the writer James Boswell visited his works in 1776, Boulton told him, "I sell here, Sir, what all the world desires to have, – POWER."

The new engine burned between a quarter and a third of the coal that an ordinary Newcomen engine needed for the same work. It was still wasteful. Most of the heat still went up the chimney. But it had cut the fuel bill by more than half, and Boulton turned that into one of the cleverest price tags in the history of business. He did not charge for the engine's worth. He charged for the coal it saved: every year the customer paid a third of the value of the fuel he was no longer burning.

At a coal mine that offer meant little, because the coal being saved was nearly worthless. In Cornwall it was irresistible. Cornwall became the first big market for the Watt engine, and within a few years the old Newcomen engines there had almost disappeared.

So far, though, every one of these engines did the same single thing. It rocked a beam up and down. That is perfect for a pump, and for seventy years pumping was nearly all that steam was asked to do. If you wanted to grind corn or turn a lathe, you needed something that went round, and the thing that went round was a waterwheel. A few mill owners found a comical way of joining the two. They bought an engine and used it to pump water from below their waterwheel back up to the top, so that the same water could run over the wheel again.

Boulton saw that the mines would not need new pumps for ever, while the country was filling with mills. He nagged his partner, and in the 1780s Watt worked out how to make the rocking beam turn a shaft. Now an engine could drive machinery directly, anywhere a cart or a barge could bring coal.

But what machinery? An engine that turns a shaft is only useful if there are rooms full of machines waiting to be turned. As it happened, somebody had just invented those, for a quite different reason, and without giving steam a thought.

## The mill on the river

It started with a craze for pretty cloth.

India made the best cotton cloth in the world. It was light, it was washable, and it came printed in bright colours that did not run. When trading ships began bringing it to Britain in quantity, people who had spent their lives in itchy wool and stiff linen went mad for it. The makers of wool and silk went mad in a different way. They petitioned, they rioted, and in the early 1700s they got Parliament to ban most printed cotton cloth.

The ban did not kill the taste for cotton. It handed British makers a challenge: produce something like the Indian article at home. In Lancashire, in the damp north-west of England, weavers were already making a mixed cloth of linen and cotton that the law allowed, working at home on hand looms with raw cotton imported by sea. They wanted to do better, and they could not, because of thread.

Cloth is thread woven together, and spinning thread by hand is painfully slow. A woman at a spinning wheel drew out a wisp of cotton fibres with her fingers, let the wheel twist it, and wound it on, one thread at a time. It took several spinners to keep one weaver supplied. And English fingers could not make cotton thread as fine and strong as Indian fingers, which had been at it for a very long time.

So the tinkerers went to work on spinning. One early machine let a single spinner turn a handle and work a row of spindles at once. The machine that mattered most copied the fingers themselves. The loose rope of cotton passed between pairs of rollers, each pair turning faster than the one before, so that the cotton was stretched thinner and thinner as it went. Then a whirling spindle twisted it tight and wound it up. No skill was needed. A child could mind it, and children did.

The man who made a business of this machine was Richard Arkwright, a former barber and wig-maker with a hard head for money. His machine was too heavy to turn by hand, and that turned out to be the making of it. In 1771 he built a tall stone building beside a fast stream at Cromford, in a Derbyshire valley, and put a waterwheel in it. One wheel turned shafts and belts that ran up through the floors, and those drove hundreds of spindles at once.

Cromford was not the first building to hold many workers and one source of power. A silk mill in nearby Derby had done that years before. But Cromford set the pattern for cotton, and cotton cloth was about to become the country's biggest export.

The pattern was this. The machines ran when the wheel ran, and the wheel could run day and night, so the mill worked two shifts of twelve hours each. The workers, many of them children, arrived by the clock and left by the clock. They did not own the tools or the cotton or the thread. They did not decide how fast to work; the machine decided. Their job was to feed it, to join threads when they snapped, and to keep up.

Then came a third machine, which combined ideas from the two earlier ones and spun finer thread than either. It was called the mule, since it was a cross between them. The early mules still needed a skilled man to run them, but they could make thread fit for the most delicate cloth. Between the early 1780s and the 1820s the price of ordinary cotton thread fell to about an eighth of what it had been. Cotton, once a luxury, became what working people wore.

All of this, the machines and the clock and the children, ran on rivers. The factory had been invented, and coal had nothing to do with it.

Arkwright's imitators lined the valleys of northern England with mills. Around 1800, nearly twenty years after Watt's engine learned to turn a shaft, steam still supplied only about a fifth of the power in British industry. Most of the rest was falling water. Steam did not draw level until about 1830.

The reason was simple. For a mill with a good stream, water was generally the cheaper choice. A river sends no bill. An engine had to be bought, and then fed with coal every day of its life.

So why did the mill owners leave the river?

Because a river is where it is. The best falls of water were in narrow valleys in the hills, a long way from anywhere, and hardly anyone lived there. An owner who built in such a place had to build a village too, with houses and a shop and a chapel, and then persuade families to move to it. Some of the early valley mills were staffed with orphans sent by the cartload from city workhouses.

A river also keeps its own hours. In a dry summer the wheel slowed, and in a hard winter it froze. The mill upstream could hold back the water in its pond just when the mill downstream needed it. And around Manchester, the heart of the cloth trade, there was not enough falling water to go round.

An engine had none of these habits. It could be set down in the middle of a town already full of people who needed work, so that if one family would not take the wage, another was waiting at the gate. It ran when the owner said so, at the speed the owner chose, in drought and frost alike. And it asked for no agreement with the neighbours.

Coal had become easier to get, too. In 1761 a duke had opened a canal from his coal mines straight into Manchester, and the price of coal in the town fell by about half. Soon canals were being dug all over the country, largely to move coal.

It did not have to go this way, at least not so fast. Engineers showed that water could be made reliable. At Greenock in Scotland, one of them built a reservoir in the hills and a channel more than five miles long to carry the water down to the town. It opened in 1827, and it turned the wheels of cotton mills, sugar refineries, a paper mill and a foundry. Similar schemes were drawn up for the rivers of Lancashire. They were not built. They would have required mill owners to share the cost, share the water and accept a timetable for using it, and men who competed with each other on every other day of the week were not willing.

Some historians think the owners chose steam mainly because it gave them the whip hand over their workers. Others point out that an engine which ignores the weather is simply a better machine. Both are true, and the owners did not need to choose between the reasons. Water could have carried the cotton industry a good deal further than it did. It could not have gone much beyond that. Nobody has ever run a railway on a millstream.

So the mills came down from the hills. Manchester, which was already a cloth town, had about 23,000 people when Arkwright was building at Cromford. Eighty years later it had about 300,000, and a skyline of chimneys. Visitors came from across Europe to stare at it, the way people now visit a city of skyscrapers, and went home impressed and appalled. The factory had found its fuel.

## Along the thread

Follow a single thread of cotton in those years and you meet four people who would never meet each other.

*[Map: the cotton thread, from the American South to Liverpool and Manchester and out to export markets, about 1850]*

Begin in the mill, with a child. In 1832 a committee of Parliament, assembled to make the case for a shorter working day, heard from a young man named Matthew Crabtree. He had gone to work at the age of eight, around 1820, in a Yorkshire mill that made woollen blankets. It was wool and not cotton, but the hours and the discipline were of the same kind.

His ordinary day ran from six in the morning to eight at night, with an hour off at noon. When trade was brisk it ran from five to nine. He lived about two miles from the mill. Asked what happened if he arrived late, he answered: "I was most commonly beaten."

*[Evidence: a page of testimony from the 1832 committee; a view of a mule-spinning room]*

The beating was not random cruelty, and Crabtree explained why with some fairness to the men who did it. The machine turned out its work at a steady rate. The adult in charge had to keep pace with the machine or lose his place, and the children had to keep pace with him. Late in the day, when small children grew drowsy, the strap was how the pace was kept. The committee wanted to hear exactly this and asked its questions accordingly. But hours like these were the ordinary rule, in kind mills and cruel ones alike. The wheel or the engine did not tire, and every idle minute of an expensive machine was money lost.

Next along the thread stood a man who had never set foot in a mill. At first the mills made thread, not cloth. Somebody still had to weave it into cloth, and that was the handloom weaver, a skilled man working at his own loom in his own cottage, often with his family helping. For a while the machines made him prosperous. Cheap thread poured out of the mills, the weavers could not turn it into cloth fast enough, and their wages were the envy of other working men.

Good wages drew people in. Weaving plain cloth was not hard to learn, and soon there were far too many weavers, nearly a quarter of a million of them. Then trade turned bad. In 1806 a cotton weaver could earn about twenty shillings a week. By 1820 he earned less than half of that, and it went on falling.

All this happened before the power loom was common. A loom driven by an engine had been patented long before, but it worked badly for decades. In the 1820s it was finally made practical, and it finished the job that overcrowding had begun. By the middle of the century fewer than fifty thousand handloom weavers were left. Many of them had hung on for years at starvation wages rather than send themselves, or their children, through the factory gate. They were not being stupid. They knew what they would be giving up: the right to start work when they chose.

Now follow the thread backwards, to where the cotton grew. Cotton will not grow in Britain. In Arkwright's day it came from the Caribbean, from Brazil, from the eastern Mediterranean. Then, in 1793, an American built a simple machine, the cotton gin, that pulled the sticky seeds out of the kind of cotton that grows well across the inland South of the United States. That had been slow hand work. With the gin, cotton could be planted across the whole South.

The planters had the land, much of it taken from the Native peoples who lived on it. They got the labour by buying human beings. In 1790 there were about 700,000 enslaved people in the United States. By 1860 there were nearly four million, and a great many of them had been marched or shipped south-west to the cotton fields, away from their families. By the middle of the century about three-quarters of all the cotton that Britain imported came from the American South, and most of that was grown and picked by slaves.

One of them left a description. Solomon Northup was a free black man from the North who was kidnapped and held in slavery for twelve years. He picked cotton, and afterwards he wrote down how the day ended. Each picker's basket was carried to the gin-house to be weighed. Each picker had a weight he was expected to bring in, and was whipped if he fell short. If he brought in more, his target might be raised the next day. However tired he was, Northup wrote, "a slave never approaches the gin-house with his basket of cotton but with fear."

A weighing scale and a whip, in other words, did in the cotton field what the clock and the strap did in the mill. The two systems were not the same. The mill child went home at night and would one day be free to leave. But they were joined by the same thread, and each made the other profitable. The mills could not have grown as they did without cheap cotton, and the plantations would have had far fewer buyers without the mills.

The fourth person lived at the other end of the thread, in India, where the whole business had started. For centuries Indian spinners and weavers had clothed much of the world. Now the copy came home to ruin the original. First they lost their customers abroad. In the 1790s India still sent more than two million pieces of cotton cloth a year to Britain; by the 1830s it sent fewer than 300,000, held back by British duties and undersold by British machines. Then Lancashire's thread and cloth began arriving in India's own markets, carried by a British trading company that had made itself the ruler of much of the country.

Indian weavers proved hard to kill off, and for decades more they went on making most of the cloth that Indians wore. The hand spinners, mostly women earning a little money at home, were less lucky. There is no competing with a machine that never tires and is never paid.

That leaves the people who were supposed to be the winners: ordinary British workers. Here is the awkward fact. For the first two generations, they did not clearly gain.

Between 1780 and 1840 the amount each British worker produced rose by nearly half. Over the same sixty years the wages of an ordinary worker, measured by what they could buy, rose by about an eighth. Nearly all the difference went to the people who owned the mills, the mines and the land. The rate of profit roughly doubled.

Wages are not the whole of a life, either. The new towns grew faster than anyone could build drains for them. Families crowded into cellars and back-to-back houses, drank from wells beside cesspits, and breathed coal smoke. A baby born in the middle of Manchester around 1840 could expect, on average, to live fewer than thirty years. Children born in those decades grew up shorter than the generation before them.

The gains came. From about the 1830s wages began to climb, and by 1900 they had more than doubled. The grandchildren of the mill hands ate better, lived longer and owned more than any working people before them. But that was no comfort to the grandparents, who had lived their whole lives in the gap.

## Why here?

Why did all this happen on a damp island off the edge of Europe?

It is not an obvious place. Britain in 1700 was a middling power. China was vastly larger and, for most of history, more inventive. Song China, seven centuries earlier, had coal, iron in huge quantities, printed books and busy markets, and no age of steam followed. India made better cloth. France had more people and fine scientists. The Dutch were richer, and better at trade and banking.

Historians have argued about this for a century and do not agree. The argument is worth hearing, because each side has noticed something real.

The first idea is about prices. British workers were among the best paid in the world, and British coal was very cheap. A machine that burns coal to save labour is a fine bargain in such a place and a silly one where hands are cheap and fuel is dear. On this view the engine and the spinning machine were invented in Britain because Britain was where it paid to invent them. The trouble is that the people the spinning machines replaced were women and children, and they were paid very little.

The second idea is about people. Britain was unusually full of tinkerers: blacksmiths, clockmakers, millwrights, instrument makers. They read pamphlets, went to lectures, wrote to one another, and shared a belief that nature could be understood and put to use. More to the point, they could turn a sketch into a machine that worked. Newcomen, Watt and the man who bored the cylinders were all of this kind. The trouble here is that the cotton inventors, who mattered most for the economy, mostly had little to do with learned societies, and belief in useful knowledge was fashionable across Europe, in places where no factories appeared.

The third idea is luck. Britain's coal lay close to the surface, close to its towns and close to water that could float it to market. China had plenty of coal, but most of it lay in the north and north-west, far from the rich cities of the lower Yangzi, and when the Song lost the north to invaders they lost their iron country with it. Britain was lucky overseas as well. Its colonies and trading partners sent it cotton, sugar and timber in quantities it could not have grown at home. By one reckoning, replacing the cotton Britain imported in 1830 with wool would have needed more sheep pasture than all the farmland and pasture in the country.

The fourth idea is rules. In 1688 Parliament pushed out one king and brought in another on its own terms. After that a British monarch could not simply seize property or refuse to repay a loan. People could build a mill or lend to a canal company with some confidence that they would keep the proceeds, and the government itself could borrow cheaply. That surely helped. But the Dutch had much of this earlier, and a full century separates 1688 from the cotton mills.

The fifth idea is empire and slavery. Britain's wealth, on this view, was built on other people's backs. The slave plantations supplied profits to invest and the raw cotton itself. The colonies were markets that could not refuse British goods. The navy protected the trade, and taxes at the ports kept Indian cloth out while the mills grew up. All of that happened. The dispute is over how much it weighed. The profits of slavery were modest beside the whole British economy, though not beside the sums invested in industry. And Spain, Portugal and France had colonies and slaves as well, and no factories to show for them.

None of these ideas is enough alone, and it is not hard to see why. Take them away one at a time. Coal was necessary, and plainly it was not sufficient, since other countries had coal and went on living as before. Skilled mechanics without cheap fuel would have made excellent clocks. Cheap fuel without a reason to save labour would have gone on heating houses. And cotton mills without an ocean of slave-grown cotton and a world of customers would have been a curiosity in a few Derbyshire valleys.

What Britain had was all of it, in one place, at one time: the fuel, the people able to build machines, a reason to bother, and an empire's worth of cotton and customers. That is less satisfying than a single cause. It is also why the thing happened once, and then had to be copied.

People who like to argue about whether history is driven by resources or by ideas can each claim this story. Each would be half right. A country sitting on coal with no one able to build an engine stays poor. A country full of ingenious mechanics with nothing to burn stays under the old ceiling. It took the rock and the mind together.

## Buried sunlight

Once an engine could turn a wheel, it was only a matter of time before someone put the engine on wheels.

That needed a smaller, fiercer kind of engine than Watt's, and Watt, a cautious man, disliked the idea. A Cornish engineer built one anyway. In 1825 a railway opened in the north-east of England to carry coal from the mines to a port, with steam locomotives hauling the wagons. Five years later a line joined the cotton mills of Manchester to the docks at Liverpool, and it was the first railway to link two cities with steam trains running to a timetable.

Notice what the first of those trains was for. The steam engine had been born at a coal mine, to help dig coal. Now it went onto rails to carry coal. The more coal was dug, the cheaper it was to build and run engines, and the more engines there were, the more coal was wanted. Britain dug roughly three million tons a year in 1700. By 1850 it dug forty or fifty million.

That loop is what broke the old ceiling. Every society before this one that grew rich had, sooner or later, run short of land to grow its energy on. Britain now drew its energy from underground, and the supply looked endless. People still needed fields for bread and for cotton. But the fire that drove their machines no longer competed with their dinner. So the growth did not stop after a generation or two, as it always had before. It went on, and it is still going on.

It is easy to make this sound more sudden than it was. In 1850 most British workers were not in factories. Farming was still the largest single occupation. Far more people worked in old trades that the new machines had not touched than worked in factories, and there were still horses everywhere. A visitor who avoided a handful of smoky towns could have crossed the country and thought little had changed. The old world lasted a long time alongside the new one.

And there was a cost that no one at the time could have counted. Coal is carbon that plants took out of the air over millions of years. Burning it puts the carbon back, in a few decades. The amount of it in the atmosphere had been steady for a very long time. In the 1800s it began to climb. The chimneys of Manchester were where that began, and the burning has not stopped.

Newcomen's customers wanted a dry mine. Arkwright wanted cheap thread. None of these people set out to change how humans live on the planet, and together they did. By the middle of the nineteenth century a society had machines that could make more than anyone had imagined, and crowds of people in a few smoky towns who tended them from six in the morning. Who would get the wealth the machines made, and who would decide the price of a day's work, were now the questions. The machines could not answer them.
