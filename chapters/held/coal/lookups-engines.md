## Section 1: Opening

**1a. Horse cost / number of horses at a named mine.** Verdict: uncertain.
- Only figure found: 500 horses "constantly employed" at a colliery at Griff, Warwickshire. It comes from Desaguliers, quoted in Lardner's *The Steam Engine*, and refers to the patentees' proposals to drain that colliery (Lardner dates it to "the latter end" of 1712). The Dictionary of National Biography says Desaguliers's accuracy has been "somewhat discredited". I found no independent confirmation and no annual cost figure for horses at any mine.
- Recommendation: use it, if at all, as "Desaguliers reported", not as documented fact.
- Sources: Lardner via https://textbase.scriptorium.ro/lardner/the_steam_engine/chap_ii/37 (as quoted in search result); DNB https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography,_1885-1900/Newcomen,_Thomas
- Also: Smeaton's own comparison of engines is in fuel duty, not in horse cost. Nothing here gives a horse-vs-engine cost ratio.

**1b. Newcomen, ironmonger of Dartmouth; engine of 1712 near Dudley Castle; date, site, depth, water per minute.** Verdict: confirmed for date and place; depth and rate rest on one secondary source.
- 1712, Conygree Coalworks at Tipton, "near Dudley Castle", Staffordshire, with partner John Calley. Generally accepted as the first *successful* engine; one source says it may have been preceded by an engine near Wolverhampton. Nuvolari 2004 says there is "some evidence" of an earlier engine at Wheal Vor, Cornwall, 1710, but Cornish adoption only began in earnest in the 1720s (Rolt and Allen 1997). Newcomen was born in Dartmouth c.1663/64 and was an ironmonger.
- Depth and rate: a popular history (Wallace, ch. 2, "The Newcomen Engine") gives the 1712 Dudley engine as 21 in. cylinder, lifting water from 153 ft, 12 strokes a minute, 10 imperial gallons a stroke (about 120 gallons a minute). Single secondary source, no citation seen; I could not confirm against the 1719 Barney engraving or a scholarly source. Treat as "reported".
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Newcomen ; https://imeche.org/news/news-article/raising-water-by-fire ; https://thehopkinthomasproject.com/TheHopkinThomasProject/TimeLine/Wales/Steam/ThomasNewcomen/Wallace/Wallace.htm

**1c. Savery patent 1698, "The Miner's Friend"; poor lift; bursting; Newcomen worked under Savery's patent.** Verdict: confirmed, with two dates to keep straight.
- Patent granted 2 July 1698 (14 years). A 1699 Act of Parliament extended it by 21 years, to 1733 (the "Fire Engine Act"). "The Miner's Friend; or, An Engine to Raise Water by Fire" is Savery's *book* of 1702, not the 1698 advertisement; the outline's "advertised as" is fine if you mean the book.
- Lift limit: suction means the pump must sit no more than about 30 ft above the water. Bursting: at York Buildings pressure of roughly 8-10 atmospheres blew the joints; at Broad Waters, Wednesbury, steam "rent the whole machine to pieces."
- Newcomen: Savery's patent forced Newcomen into partnership; by 1712 they marketed his engine under it. Savery died 1715; the rights passed to a joint-stock "Proprietors of the Invention for Raising Water by Fire" with Newcomen on the committee. Savery's patent ran out in 1733, four years after Newcomen died (1729).
- Sources: WP-Savery; Nuvolari 2004; https://thehopkinthomasproject.com/TheHopkinThomasProject/TimeLine/Wales/Steam/ThomasNewcomen/Wallace/Wallace.htm

## Section 2: The organic economy

**2a. British coal output about 1700 and share of European output; London coal imports.** Verdict: output range given; European share and London tonnage not found.
- Output c.1700: popular summaries give about 2.7 million tonnes (Drax, Our World in Data series built on Flinn vol. 2); a course chapter says about 3 million tons. Wrigley counts consumption, about 2.2 million tons for England in 1700 per one secondary citation. So: "roughly 3 million tons" is safe; I could not open Flinn's table itself.
- Share of European output: not found. The claim "Britain produced 80 percent of Europe's coal" appears in one non-scholarly source; the stronger-looking claim "burned more coal than the rest of Europe combined" is in a teaching chapter without citation. Do not state a percentage.
- Wrigley figures, as reproduced in a secondary paper: coal supplied about half of all energy used in England and Wales in 1700-09 and about 79% by 1800-09.
- London coal imports 1700: not found. Flinn, Nef and Wrigley would have it. Related facts found: by 1700 the east-coast coal trade took roughly half the tonnage of the merchant navy (secondary); London received more coal than any other port in the world; about 600 colliers carried about 1.35 million tons a year to London by 1800 (one secondary source). Caution: chaldron units differ (Newcomen chaldron c.2.65 tons, London chaldron c.1.4 tons), which is why 1700 tonnages vary.

Calley's background is also unclear (glazier or grazier, per different accounts).

## Section 4: Mechanism

**4a. About twelve strokes a minute; typical horsepower.** Verdict: strokes confirmed; typical horsepower not found.
- Nuvolari 2004: strokes were limited to about 10-12 a minute (before Watt's cataract regulator); WP-Newcomen: "around 12 times per minute." Power scaled with cylinder area.
- Horsepower: no authoritative "typical" figure found. One related datum: von Tunzelmann estimates 4-6 hp as the size above which a Newcomen engine paid better than a Savery engine (Nuvolari 2004, citing von Tunzelmann 1978, pp. 47-48). That is a threshold, not a typical output. A blog's "about 40 hp for the Dudley engine" is unsourced; do not use. Mark as uncertain.

**4b. Thermal efficiency, "under 1 percent".** Verdict: confirmed for the typical engine; improved engines about 1.4 percent.
- Duty (foot-pounds of water lifted per bushel of coal): average of Newcastle-area Newcomen engines in 1769 was 5.59 million; Smeaton's improved engine (1772) 9.45 million; Cornish Newcomen average in 1779 was 7.1 million. Using von Tunzelmann's rule (efficiency about 0.15 percent times duty in millions), that is about 0.8 percent for the 1769 average and about 1.4 percent for Smeaton's engine. (Nuvolari 2004, footnote 26-27, citing von Tunzelmann 1978 pp. 67-70.)
- Other figures: Britannica says about 1 percent; a study of surviving engines puts individual cases at about 0.3 to 0.6 percent. Watt's engine is given about 2 percent (Britannica).
- Wording suggestion: "less than 1 percent for the typical engine in 1769; at best a little over 1 percent."
- Sources: Nuvolari 2004; https://www.britannica.com/technology/energy-conversion/Developments-of-the-Industrial-Revolution ; https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/17581206.2018.1463660

**4c. Cornwall had water to pump and no coal; Newcomen engine counts by 1730 and by 1775 or 1800.** Verdict: Cornwall claim confirmed; counts are a range, and they disagree.
- Cornwall: coal came by sea from Wales and was extremely expensive; fuel inefficiency "hampered" Newcomen use there (Nuvolari 2004). Newcomen engines in Cornwall from the 1720s, even if there was an earlier one at Wheal Vor (1710 or 1715).
- By 1733 (patent expiry): about 100 built under the Proprietors' monopoly (Nuvolari 2004); about 125 (Wikipedia); 110, of which 14 abroad (another source). Use "about 100 to 125."
- By 1775: about 600 built (Wikipedia; some already worn out).
- By 1800: figures are not comparable: Nuvolari's Kanefsky-based database has 1,369 engines of known type for 1760-1800 (all types); Wikipedia has over 2,200 engines built in the eighteenth century, about 450 of them Watt; Kanefsky and Robey's casual estimate is 2,400 to 2,500; Boulton and Watt built 496 (Wikipedia) or 451 (Jennifer Tann's count, which cut Kanefsky and Robey's 478). Say "roughly 2,000 to 2,500 engines of all types by 1800, of which under 500 were Boulton and Watt."
- Related: Watt and Cornwall. Boulton and Watt erected 49 pumping engines in Cornwall between 1777 and 1801 (Nuvolari). One source says all but one of Cornwall's Newcomen engines had been displaced by 1783. Wikipedia says Watt's first commercial engines (1776) worked "mainly" in Cornish mines; I could not confirm that, and I believe (not verified here) the very first were in Staffordshire/Shropshire. Safer: "Cornwall became the first big market."
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Newcomen ; WP-Newcomen ; Nuvolari 2004 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boulton_and_Watt ; https://thehopkinthomasproject.com/TheHopkinThomasProject/TimeLine/Wales/Steam/JamesWatt/Kelly/Watt.htm ; Kanefsky and Robey 1980, "Steam engines in eighteenth-century Britain: a quantitative assessment", Technology and Culture 21 (not opened; cited in search results)

## Section 5: Watt and after

**5a. Watt dates.** Verdict: confirmed.
- 1763-64 repairs University of Glasgow Newcomen model; 1765 conceives separate condenser; 1769 patent (No. 913); 1775 partnership with Boulton and Parliament extends the patent to 1800; 1800 patent expires, Watt retires. Source: WP-Watt.

**5b. Wilkinson boring.** Verdict: confirmed (1774), with one nuance.
- Wilkinson built a boring machine in 1774 (patent for boring iron cannon, No. 1063, 1774; later quashed 1779). Its cutter shaft ran through the cylinder and was supported at both ends, unlike older cantilever borers. It made the cylinder for Boulton and Watt's first commercial engine; Wilkinson got an exclusive contract to supply cylinders. The outline's "1774-75" is fine. Nuance: the link to gunmaking is real, but the cylinder boring was a modified cannon-boring design; Wikipedia calls it possibly the first machine tool. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilkinson_(industrialist)

**5c. Royalty terms; fuel saving "two-thirds to three-quarters".** Verdict: royalty confirmed; fuel saving is a range and depends on the comparison.
- Royalty: one third of the value of the coal saved compared with a Newcomen engine of the same power (WP-Watt; Britannica 1911 says "usually claimed a sum equivalent to one-third of the saving"). Savings were calculated against a hypothetical equivalent Newcomen engine.
- Saving: Britannica 1911: Watt's engine used "no more than a fourth" of the fuel (a 75 percent saving). Maurice Kelly: one third of the fuel of an equivalent atmospheric engine (a 67 percent saving). Duty figures (Nuvolari 2004): average Newcomen 5.6 million; Smeaton-improved 7-10 million; first Watt pumping engines 18 million; later 26 million; Cornwall 1792 about 32-35 million. So against an average 1769 Newcomen engine the saving was about 70-80 percent; against Smeaton's best it was less (about 45 to 65 percent). Outline's "two-thirds to three-quarters" holds against the ordinary Newcomen engine.
- Sources: https://thehopkinthomasproject.com/TheHopkinThomasProject/TimeLine/Wales/Steam/JamesWatt/Kelly/Watt.htm ; https://kwx.liehendr.com/content/wikisource_en_all_maxi_2022-09/A/Page%3AEB1911_-_Volume_28.djvu/434 ; Nuvolari 2004

**5d. Boulton to Boswell, "I sell here, Sir, what all the world desires to have — POWER."** Verdict: confirmed.
- Wording as given by Wikiquote from Boswell's *Life of Samuel Johnson* (1791): "I sell here, Sir, what all the world desires to have, – POWER." Visit to Boulton's Soho works on 22 March 1776 (per search summary; Wikiquote page did not name Soho). One scholarly reference gives Life, vol. 2, p. 32 (Hill-Powell edition). Boswell is the only contemporary source; some reprints vary comma/dash and "desires/desire". The outline's rendering matches Wikiquote's. Caveat: I did not view a scan of Boswell's text, so check against the Hill-Powell edition or a Project Gutenberg copy before printing.
- Sources: https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Matthew_Boulton ; https://engines.egr.uh.edu/talks/powersir

**5e. Rotary motion: sun-and-planet, double-acting, governor.** Verdict: confirmed.
- 1781 patent for sun-and-planet gear (gets round a rival patent on the crank); 1782 double-acting and expansive engine patents; 1784 parallel motion; 1788 centrifugal governor. Nuvolari 2004 says it was Boulton who pushed Watt in the early 1780s to produce rotary motion. Source: WP-Watt; Nuvolari 2004.

**5f. Patent to 1800; did it delay better engines?** Verdict: dates confirmed; debate exists, one side documented.
- Extension to 1800 confirmed (WP-Watt). The delay argument: Boldrin and Levine (*Against Intellectual Monopoly*, 2008) say the 1769 and especially the extended 1775 patent delayed adoption and that improvers (Bull, Trevithick, Woolf, Hornblower) were held back until expiry. Nuvolari and Sean Bottomley are the usual scholarly counterweights, but I did not retrieve their arguments, so I cannot summarise them. Say "debated" and cite Boldrin and Levine for the pro-delay side only.
- Sources: https://didattica.unibocconi.it/mypage/upload/8056-BOLDRIN_E_LEVINE120081001153614.20061024 ; https://mises.org/library/james-watt-monopolist (advocacy source; use with care)

**5g. Trevithick and high pressure.** Verdict: confirmed, with a correction to the sequence.
- Trevithick was already working on high-pressure steam from 1797 (Ding Dong mine, where Boulton and Watt later served an injunction); he avoided a condenser partly to avoid Watt's patent. Puffing Devil road locomotive 24 December 1801; patent for high-pressure engine 24 March 1802; Penydarren locomotive 21 February 1804 (hauled 10 tons of iron and about 70 men); the Cornish boiler and engine c.1812. A Trevithick engine exploded at Greenwich in 1803 (four killed), which Boulton and Watt used against high pressure. So "after it lapsed" is right for commercial use, but the work began before 1800.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Trevithick ; WP-Watt

**5h. Bridgewater canal 1761; "halved the price".** Verdict: confirmed with caveat.
- Opened Worsley to Manchester in 1761; first boat over the Barton Aqueduct 17 July 1761. Wikipedia: price of coal in Manchester fell by about half within a year. The duke had promised a delivered price of no more than 4d per cwt (112 lb). One Wikipedia-derived source says "nearly two-thirds". I could not confirm the commonly quoted 7d to 3.5d. Use "about half".
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgewater_Canal ; https://bridgewatercanal.co.uk/?p=106 (via search summary)

**5i. Installed steam horsepower about 1800, 1830, 1870; steam overtaking water in cotton.** Verdict: figures found; outline's broad point confirmed with detail.
- Kanefsky (1979) estimates of power capacity installed, mainly mining and manufacturing, as given in Nuvolari 2004, Table 1.1:
  - 1760: steam 5,000 hp, water 70,000, wind 10,000
  - 1800: steam 35,000 (20.6%), water 120,000 (70.6%), wind 15,000
  - 1830: steam 160,000 (47%), water 160,000 (47%), wind 20,000
  - 1870: steam 2,060,000 (89.6%), water 230,000 (10%), wind 10,000
  Nuvolari warns these are installed capacity not use, and probably overstate steam for 1760-1830 because small water and wind units were missed. His reading: steam only became general from the 1840s. So "around 1800 most British factories still ran on water" is confirmed (steam about 21 percent of capacity); in the aggregate, steam only matched water around 1830 and dominated by 1870.
- Cotton: Wikipedia ("Cotton mill") says from about 1820 the stationary steam engine became the normal power source for cotton mills; by 1860 Lancashire mills used about 300,000 hp, only about 18,500 hp of it water. I did not find a source naming a single crossover year for cotton, so "1820s-30s" is consistent but not sharply dated. Von Tunzelmann 1978 (not opened) is the standard source; Bottomley (Economic History Review 2025) argues steam use was wider than earlier estimates, especially in manufacturing; a Glasgow-led 2024 study (PNAS Nexus) finds waterpower was abundant across most of Britain, with Greater Manchester the exception, which pushed Manchester mills to steam.
- Sources: Nuvolari 2004; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_mill ; https://www.gla.ac.uk/news/headline_1091714_en.html ; https://ehs.org.uk/article/stationary-steam-power-in-the-united-kingdom-1800-70-an-empirical-reassessment/

Safe wording: "began to climb in the 1800s." Do not say 1750.

## Section 10: Alternative

**10a. Robert Thom, Shaws Water Works, dates, why the larger schemes were not built.** Verdict: Greenock confirmed; the Lancashire part rests on Malm's abstract only.
- Robert Thom (1774-1847), Scottish civil engineer, owner of Rothesay Cotton Mills on Bute, where he ran a water-power system (operating by 1820). Shaws Water Company incorporated 10 June 1825 with capital of £30,000; scheme opened 16 April 1827. Great Reservoir, later renamed Loch Thom; aqueduct is the Greenock Cut (5.5 miles). Commissioned by Sir Michael Shaw Stewart. It supplied drinking water and power: wheels drove a paper mill, woollen and cotton mills, ropeworks, sugar refineries, an iron foundry and shipbuilding works. Watt and Rennie had earlier said such a scheme was impractical. The aqueduct became obsolete in 1971. Thom's later schemes for Edinburgh (1829) and Glasgow (1835-41) were largely unbuilt; one at Paisley appears to have been executed.
- Lancashire: Andreas Malm, article in *Environmental History* (2014, doi 10.1093/envhis/emt106; abstract at Lund) describes Thom as a champion of water and critic of steam and traces the fate of Lancashire reservoir plans. Per the abstract: water was consistently the cheaper power, but the schemes required manufacturers to coordinate their energy use, accept planning and share construction costs; under free competition these demands were "ultimately unfeasible," so the largest schemes were never built. This is Malm's argument, not settled fact; the main counter is the 2024 Glasgow finding that water was abundant almost everywhere except Greater Manchester (see 5i). I could not read the article body, so I cannot name individual Lancashire plans or dates.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loch_Thom ; https://canmore.org.uk/site/202205/loch-thom-compensation-reservoir ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Thom_(engineer) ; https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/4250817 ; https://www.gla.ac.uk/news/headline_1091714_en.html
