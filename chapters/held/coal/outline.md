# Chapter 48 — outline, round 2

**Title:** Why did the factory run on coal?
**Line under the title:** Britain, about 1700 to 1850, with links to the American South and India.
**Length:** 5,000 to 7,000 words.

This outline is a story in ten movements. The movements are not section headings: the writer chooses four to seven headings of their own that come from the story. The style sheet (`style.md` at the repository root) governs voice. The three `lookups-*.md` files hold the checked facts. Any figure, date or quotation in the chapter must appear in a lookup file and must not be marked UNVERIFIED there. Where this outline and a lookup disagree, the lookup wins.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

For all of history people lived on recent sunlight: crops, fodder, wood, wind, falling water. That put a ceiling on every society, because every use of energy competed for land. Britain got past the ceiling by burning sunlight stored underground, and it happened in a roundabout, almost accidental way. The first engine was a terrible machine that paid only because coal mines had fuel to waste. The factory was invented separately, for cotton, and ran on water. The two were joined late and slowly. The result made a few people rich quickly, made most people's lives harder for two generations, tied together strangers on three continents, and started the burning that has not stopped.

## The story

1. **A terrible machine.** Open on Newcomen's engine of 1712 beside a coal mine in the English Midlands. Mines flooded as they went deeper, and horses walking in circles could not keep up. The engine pumped water. It wasted about ninety-nine parts in a hundred of its fuel. It paid anyway because a coal mine has heaps of small broken coal that is hard to sell.

2. **Living on sunlight.** Step back. Before this, nearly all the energy people used had been captured by plants in the recent past. Land for bread, land for horse feed and land for firewood were the same land. A society could get richer for a while and then hit the limit. England had already begun to cheat: London heated itself with coal shipped from Newcastle, so by 1700 there was a big coal industry with a water problem. The engine served an industry that already existed. What was new was turning heat into motion.

3. **How it worked, and who understood it.** One clear passage on the mechanism (see "The mechanism" below). Then the surprising part: it was built by an ironmonger, not a scientist. Natural philosophers had shown that air has weight and that a vacuum is powerful, but whether Newcomen knew their work is not known. And the science of heat came afterwards: the rule for how much work heat can do was worked out in the following century by people studying engines that already ran. Here the machine came first and the theory followed.

4. **Where coal cost money.** Hundreds of Newcomen engines were built, mostly at coal mines. The tin and copper mines of Cornwall also needed pumping and had no coal; every ton came by sea. That is where waste hurt. James Watt, an instrument maker in Glasgow repairing a model engine, saw that the fuel went into reheating the cylinder on every stroke, and added a separate cold chamber to condense the steam so the cylinder could stay hot. It took years to build one that worked, partly because nobody could bore a cylinder accurately enough until a cannon-maker's boring machine did it. His partner Matthew Boulton charged customers a share of the coal they saved. Boulton's line to Boswell, if used, must be quoted exactly from the lookup.

5. **From pumping to turning.** For seventy years engines only rocked up and down, which suits a pump. Some mill owners used them to pump water back uphill to run over a waterwheel again. In the 1780s Watt's engine was adapted to turn a shaft directly. Now it could drive machinery. But there was not yet much machinery to drive, which leads to cotton.

6. **The factory was invented without it.** Britons loved printed Indian cotton cloth so much that Parliament restricted it to protect wool and silk. British makers set out to copy a product India made better. The bottleneck was spinning thread. A run of machines broke it, and the decisive step was Arkwright's mill at Cromford: hundreds of spindles driven by one waterwheel, with workers, many of them children, on a clock. That was the pattern for the cotton mills that followed, and it ran on a river. Describe a spinning machine in three or four sentences at most. Name Arkwright; name other inventors only if the story needs them.

7. **Why leave the river?** Around 1800 water still powered most mills, and waterwheels did more of Britain's work than steam into the 1830s. Water was cheaper. But a river is where it is: in a valley, short of workers, low in a dry summer, frozen in a hard winter, and shared with the mill upstream. An engine could be put in a town full of people who needed work, and it ran when the owner chose. Canals brought the coal. Engineers showed that reservoirs could make water reliable, and one such scheme was built at Greenock, but owners would have had to share rivers and move workers to the hills. Manchester, already a cloth town, became the smoky city the world came to stare at. This is the place for the counterfactual, in a paragraph, woven in: water could have carried cotton further than it did; it could not have run railways.

8. **The people along the thread.** Follow one thread of cotton and meet four people. The child in the mill: hours, the pace set by the machine, the overseer; one short quotation from Matthew Crabtree's evidence to Parliament. The handloom weaver, a skilled man working at home, whose earnings fell for decades before the power loom finished his trade. The enslaved picker in the American South: most of the cotton Lancashire spun by the middle of the century was grown by enslaved people; one short quotation from Solomon Northup on the daily weighing. The Indian spinner and weaver, who lost first their export markets and then, for spinners, much of their work at home. Then the awkward fact: for the first two generations ordinary British workers did not clearly gain. Output per worker rose, wages barely moved until about the 1830s, and a child born in Manchester could expect a far shorter life than one born in the countryside. The gains came later.

9. **Why here?** Why Britain, and not China, India, France or the Netherlands? Song China had coal, iron, printing and markets seven centuries earlier. Historians do not agree, and the honest way to tell it is as four or five ideas in plain words, no more than a short paragraph each, with no scholar's name in the body:
   - Workers in Britain were expensive and coal was cheap, so a machine that swapped coal for labour paid there before it paid elsewhere. The objection: the women and children who actually did the spinning were poorly paid.
   - Britain was full of tinkerers: smiths, clockmakers, millwrights and instrument makers who believed nature could be put to use and could turn an idea into a machine that worked.
   - Luck of geography: Britain's coal lay near its industry and near water transport, and its colonies and trade supplied cotton, sugar and timber that would have taken vast areas of British land to produce. China's coal lay far from its richest region.
   - Rules: after 1688 Parliament limited the king, property was fairly safe and the state could borrow cheaply. The objection: the Dutch had much of this earlier.
   - Empire and slavery: profits, captive markets, raw cotton, a navy to protect the trade and tariffs to keep Indian cloth out. The objection: the profits were modest beside the whole economy, and other empires had colonies without factories.
   Then the narrator's view, stated plainly: none of these was enough alone. Coal was necessary and plainly not sufficient, since others had coal. It took fuel, people able to build machines, a reason to bother, and an empire's worth of cotton and customers, in the same place at the same time. This is also evidence in the book's running dispute over whether resources or ideas drive history: here both were needed.

10. **What it started.** Engines went onto rails, first to carry coal. Coal output multiplied many times over. For the first time a society's growth was not held down by the amount of land it could plant, and it kept growing for generations. Old ways lasted alongside the new: in 1850 most British workers were not in factories. And the carbon from all that buried sunlight went into the air. End on an image or an open question about who would get the wealth the machines made and who would decide the price of a day's work. Do not announce the next chapter.

## The mechanism

Tell it in the order it happens, in one or two paragraphs. A boiler makes steam. The steam fills an upright cylinder under a piston, like a huge bicycle pump standing on end. Cold water is sprayed in. The steam turns back into a few drops of water and leaves almost nothing behind, a partial vacuum. The weight of the air above then pushes the piston down. The piston pulls one end of a rocking beam, and the other end lifts the rods that work the pumps down the shaft. Steam is let in again, the weight of the pump rods pulls the piston back up, and it repeats. The point to land: steam does not push. The atmosphere does. The flaw: every stroke chills the whole cylinder, and the next puff of steam has to heat it again.

For Watt, say only what he changed and why it saved fuel. Leave out the governor, the sun-and-planet gear, double action and patents unless one sentence needs them.

## Wording settled after the independent check of round 1

The first drafts were checked by a second model. These points fix what it found. Follow them.

- Deeper mines meant more water to lift and further to lift it. Do not say water always comes in faster with depth, or that there was a depth no horses could manage.
- "Recent sunlight" or "sunlight of recent years", not "that year's sunlight". Wood takes decades to grow.
- The 99-percent waste applies to early engines. Later Newcomen engines did a little better.
- Coal relaxed the land limit on energy. It did not abolish all limits. Food and fibre still needed land.
- Newcomen engines were used mostly at coal mines, not only there. Cornwall used them and paid heavily for fuel. Small coal was hard to sell, not impossible to sell.
- Do not say the engine "needed" the science of the vacuum. It works by air pressure; what Newcomen knew of the science is unknown.
- On heat: do not say nobody knew anything about heat. Say the theory of how much work heat can yield came later, from studying engines.
- Watt reduced the waste greatly; he did not end it. Use the fuel saving given in the lookup.
- The boring machine came from cannon-making. Do not say governments paid for it.
- Cromford was not the first factory of any kind; a silk mill at Derby came earlier. It set the pattern for cotton.
- On the ban on Indian cloth: Parliament banned most printed cotton cloth in the early 1700s, and Lancashire's mixed linen-and-cotton cloth stayed legal. No more detail than that.
- If the spinning mule is mentioned, say it combined ideas from the two earlier machines. Do not describe its carriage. Skilled spinners still ran the early mules.
- One spinning-productivity figure at most, quoted with the same units as the lookup. No figure for Indian hand spinning.
- "Cotton gin", not "saw gin". No bale counts or bale weights.
- Not all cotton was grown by enslaved people. Say "most of the cotton Lancashire spun by mid-century".
- The 1832 committee heard testimony; it was not sworn. Crabtree was describing the years around 1820. The committee was assembled to make the case for a shorter working day; say so in a clause.
- Crabtree worked as a child in a Yorkshire woollen (blanket) mill, not a cotton mill. Say so. The hours and discipline were of the same kind as in cotton.
- For life in Manchester use the life-expectancy figure in the lookup. Compare it with the countryside or the nation only if the lookup gives the comparison figure.
- The Derby silk mill and the practice of using engines to pump water back over a waterwheel may be mentioned without dates or figures.
- Handloom weavers' earnings were falling before power looms were common, from too many weavers and bad trade. The power loom finished the job.
- The Liverpool and Manchester line (1830) was the first to link two cities with steam trains running to a timetable. Do not say it relied on locomotives alone.
- The people along the cotton chain "would never meet". Do not say none of them knew the others existed.
- Manchester was already a textile town before steam.
- Skip the dispute over why cotton-picking rates rose, the dispute over whether Watt's patent held back progress, and the Factory Act's detailed terms.
- No horse counts for any named mine.
