## 1. Factual flags

Based only on `chapters/52-coal/draft-A.md` and my existing knowledge; I have not checked external sources. Confidence refers to the proposed correction, and “major” marks an error or overstatement that affects a central explanation or scholarly argument.

1. **“The coal that remained lay deeper, and every extra metre of depth meant more water to lift.”**  
   This makes a variable geological problem sound universal. Accessible shallow coal was not generally exhausted across Britain, and water inflow depends on rock permeability, faults and connections to aquifers, not simply depth; greater depth does increase the distance water must be raised.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

2. **“In 1700 almost everything people did was powered by that year's sunlight”; “For the first time, a society's supply of energy did not compete with its supply of food.”**  
   The first statement conflicts with the chapter’s own estimate that coal already supplied roughly half of English and Welsh energy around 1700. The second exaggerates the completeness and novelty of the escape: coal substantially relaxed dependence on agricultural land, while food, fodder, timber and other organic inputs remained important. Present the organic economy as a model and the transition as gradual.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

3. **“Nobody in 1712, or in 1780, knew what heat was. There was no concept of energy … That rule was first worked out in 1824 … [by] Sadi Carnot.”**  
   This conflates several developments. Eighteenth-century investigators had substantial knowledge of heat, including Joseph Black’s work on latent and specific heat, although no accepted modern thermodynamic theory existed. Carnot established a theoretical limit on heat-engine efficiency while using caloric theory; the mechanical equivalent of heat and the general conservation of energy emerged principally in the 1840s.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

4. **“The atmospheric engine needed the science of the vacuum.”**  
   An engine’s operation obeying a scientific principle does not establish that its inventor needed prior scientific knowledge of that principle. The chapter previously acknowledges that transmission of the relevant science to Newcomen is unproven, then treats that dependence as established in its conclusion.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

5. **“A Newcomen engine turned less than a hundredth of its fuel into work.”**  
   This is defensible for early or ordinary engines, but not as an unqualified statement about the entire engine type. Improved atmospheric engines exceeded 1 percent efficiency, as the chapter itself acknowledges in discussing Smeaton. Specify the period or performance benchmark.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

6. **“He had little reason to care about efficiency”; “waste hardly mattered.”**  
   Cheap fuel reduced the incentive to economize, but did not eliminate it. Fuel handling, firing labour, boiler capacity and maintenance still cost money, and improvements could increase pumping capacity as well as reduce coal consumption. The sustained improvement of atmospheric engines makes the blanket claim misleading.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

7. **“The Newcomen engine … made sense only beside a coal mine.”**  
   This is directly contradicted by its use in Cornish metal mines, which the chapter describes. Atmospheric engines also served other pumping applications. Cheap pithead coal made them especially attractive, but sufficiently valuable drainage could justify expensive fuel.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

8. **“The obstacle was metalworking”; “nobody could make one.”**  
   Accurate cylinder boring was a critical obstacle, but the wording reduces Watt’s development problems to one cause and makes existing capability sound nonexistent. Financing, sealing, condenser performance and construction difficulties also mattered; full-size experimental engines preceded Wilkinson’s improved cylinders.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

9. **“Governments paying for accurate guns had … paid for the tool that made the efficient steam engine possible.”**  
   Wilkinson’s connection with cannon manufacture establishes a technological connection, not direct government financing of his invention. The chapter needs evidence for that financial claim; otherwise say that techniques developed for cannon boring enabled more accurate engine cylinders.  
   **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

10. **“In 1782 he patented the double-acting engine, in which the cylinder was closed at both ends.”**  
    This implies that closing the cylinder’s top arrived with double action. Watt’s earlier single-acting pumping engines already used a closed top, with steam admitted above the piston, helping keep the cylinder hot. Double action meant alternating admission and condensation on both sides, not the first adoption of a closed cylinder.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

11. **“Up to this point every engine did one thing: it rocked a beam”; “Steam reached the mills only after Watt cut its fuel use … [and] it was made to turn a shaft.”**  
    Savery’s machine had no beam, as the chapter has already explained. More consequentially, steam engines powered mills indirectly before Watt’s rotative engine by pumping water back to a waterwheel, and other engineers developed rotative arrangements. Watt greatly improved practical factory power; he did not create its only possible route.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

12. **“The engine now held a steady speed without anyone touching it”; “smoothly at a constant speed.”**  
    Watt’s centrifugal governor regulated speed within limits; it did not maintain literally constant speed as loads changed. A flywheel also mattered in smoothing the engine’s cyclic torque, but is absent from this explanation.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

13. **“The Act of 1721 … [had] exemptions … for fustian.”**  
    This compresses a consequential legal sequence. The clear protection for British linen-warp, cotton-weft fabrics is associated with the Manchester Act of 1736, which clarified their position following the earlier prohibition. Distinguish the original exemptions from that later change.  
    **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

14. **“Crompton combined the rollers of the water frame with the moving carriage of the jenny.”**  
    The jenny had a moving clasp or drawing mechanism, but its spindles remained stationary. The mule’s moving spindle carriage was a distinctive arrangement. Explain the combination as roller drafting with intermittent drawing and twisting, rather than implying that Crompton transferred an existing spindle carriage from the jenny.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

15. **“One source of power can turn a hundred [spindles] as easily as ten.”**  
    A common drive can operate many spindles, but additional spindles require additional power and impose greater loads on shafts and gearing. The economy lies in shared power transmission and reduced labour per spindle, not in operating ten times as many without additional effort.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

16. **“135 [working hours] on an automatic mule in 1825.”**  
    This appears to give mature automatic-mule performance to the year of Roberts’s first patent. The chapter subsequently dates his successful version to 1830. Verify what machine, yarn count and date the productivity estimate actually represents before presenting it as achieved automatic production in 1825.  
    **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

17. **“Usable versions [of the power loom] came in the 1810s and commercial success in 1822.”**  
    Successful commercial power weaving developed incrementally, including improvements associated with William Horrocks before the 1810s. Roberts’s 1822 loom was an important advance, not a defensible single starting date for commercial success—especially when the next sentence records 14,000 power looms in 1820.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

18. **“Nearly 4 million [bales] in 1860, a bale by then weighing about 225 kilograms.”**  
    Bale weights varied considerably, and imposing a roughly 500-pound bale on the 1860 crop is questionable; historical series often use different actual or standardized weights. Specify the crop year and the bale convention used by the output series rather than attaching this conversion to all contemporary bales.  
    **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

19. **“British goods [were] … about 35 percent [of India’s cloth market] … which means Indian spinners and weavers still clothed most Indians.”**  
    The inference combines two different occupations. Indian handloom weavers increasingly used imported machine-spun yarn, so the survival of Indian weaving does not demonstrate comparable survival of Indian hand spinning. Domestic cloth-market share cannot establish both.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

20. **“[The Sadler Committee] took sworn evidence”; “what was said under oath.”**  
    Ordinary House of Commons select committees did not generally possess the power to administer oaths in 1832; that general power came later. Unless a specific exceptional authority can be demonstrated for this committee, describe Crabtree’s statements as testimony, not sworn testimony, and correct the image caption too.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

21. **“[The 1833 Factory Act] barred children under nine from working in them.”**  
    Silk mills were an important exception to the minimum-age prohibition. Although “most textile mills” provides some qualification, the subsequent rule reads as uniform across the mills covered by the act. State the exception explicitly.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

22. **“The power loom then became a practical machine. By 1820 a weaver’s weekly earnings had fallen…”**  
    The sequence makes power-loom competition appear to explain the whole early collapse in handloom earnings. Much of that decline occurred while power weaving remained limited; labour supply, entry into weaving, trade conditions and postwar disruption also mattered. Separate the earlier wage decline from the later decisive displacement by power looms.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

23. **“A machine … is worth developing only where labour is dear and fuel cheap”; “energy … was cheaper there than anywhere.”**  
    These are stronger claims than Allen’s comparative argument warrants. Profitability depends on relative factor prices, capital costs, productivity and the particular machine; the hand-powered jenny did not require cheap coal. British coalfield districts also cannot stand for every British location or establish a worldwide minimum energy price.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

24. **“If the labour being replaced was cheap, the motive for replacing it has to be found elsewhere.”**  
    Cheap labour can still be worth mechanizing if productivity gains are large enough. Humphries and Schneider challenge the evidence and calibration of the high-wage explanation for spinning; their findings do not logically eliminate labour saving as an incentive.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

25. **“Small improvements made by people who gained little from them.”**  
    This attributes a general lack of reward without evidence. Craftsmen and manufacturers could gain through wages, reputation, contracts, promotion or profits without obtaining patents or becoming famous. Mokyr’s argument about useful knowledge should not be equated with predominantly unrewarded invention.  
    **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

26. **“[Wage studies] … puts the divergence well before 1800.”**  
    Earlier wage differences challenge strong claims of parity, but do not by themselves settle the dating of the Great Divergence. Urban male wage series, household living standards and regional output per person are different measures, and their geographical representativeness is disputed. Present this as evidence for earlier divergence, not a conclusive dating.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

27. **“The ghost-acre figures assume that British farming could not have improved and that cotton could only have been replaced by wool.”**  
    This turns limitations of a counterfactual calculation into unnecessarily absolute assumptions attributed to Pomeranz. A calculation using contemporary yields and wool equivalents need not assert that improvement or other substitution was impossible. Also, estimates of actual land embodied in imports answer a different question from estimates of British land needed to replace them.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

28. **“The overseas half … is Pomeranz’s own.”**  
    Pomeranz’s distinctive contribution was his synthesis and comparative argument, not the invention of the idea that overseas resources relieved European land constraints. That idea and the “ghost acreage” tradition predate his work.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

29. **“The Liverpool and Manchester Railway … [was] the first to rely on steam locomotives alone. Railways began as a way of moving coal and cotton.”**  
    The Liverpool and Manchester used stationary-engine rope haulage on the steep Liverpool approaches, so “locomotives alone” is inaccurate. Earlier railways and wagonways served several mineral and industrial trades; cotton belongs particularly to this railway’s commercial setting, not to the origin of railways generally. Describe its pioneering integrated passenger-and-freight steam operation more precisely.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

30. **“None of these people had met, and most did not know the others existed.”**  
    This is an unsupported assertion about everyone in the illustrative supply chain. Merchants met suppliers and customers, and workers could know that their materials and markets were overseas. The defensible point is that most participants had no direct personal relationship with people at distant stages of production.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

## 2. Clarity flags

- **“One stroke of the engine has four steps.”** The account describes a complete cycle, including the piston’s return and working strokes. Use “cycle” here and distinguish it from a single stroke.

- **“When the engine is at rest their weight … lifts the piston.”** This requires the pressure beneath the piston to have been restored. Explain that admitting steam removes the pressure imbalance so the heavy pump rods can descend; otherwise the reader may wonder why their weight does not defeat the working stroke too.

- **“The far end hauls up the pump rods and a load of water with them.”** Explain that the rods operate pumps down the shaft. This wording invites readers to imagine a bucket attached to the beam, and the absence of pump valves makes it unclear why the water does not simply fall back.

- **“Almost nothing is left in the cylinder.”** Liquid condensate, injection water and some air remain. Explain briefly how water drains away and air is removed, while preserving the useful distinction between a partial vacuum and a completely empty chamber.

- **The separate-condenser description and diagram.** The reader needs to know that the condenser remains cold, the working cylinder remains hot, and valves isolate the condenser during steam admission. The diagram also needs Watt’s closed cylinder top, rather than suggesting that attaching a cold vessel to the illustrated open-top Newcomen engine completes the conversion.

- **“The average … duty … was 5.6 million.”** Repeat the full unit at least once: pounds of water raised one foot per bushel of coal. Explain that conversion to thermal efficiency additionally requires assumptions about the coal’s mass and heating value.

- **The sun-and-planet and double-acting passage.** The account omits how a chain suspension capable of pulling becomes a connection capable of transmitting force in both directions. A brief explanation of Watt’s linkage and the flywheel would complete the mechanical story.

- **“Drawing out and twisting several threads together.”** A novice may read this as combining several threads into one. Say that the jenny produced several separate yarns simultaneously, and distinguish drawing, twisting and winding onto spindles.

- **The jenny, water frame and mule sequence.** Explain what each machine’s yarn was useful for: the jenny’s comparatively weak yarn principally for weft, the water frame’s stronger yarn for warp, and the mule’s capacity for fine yarn. Define warp and weft; otherwise the reason several spinning technologies coexisted remains obscure.

- **“100 pounds … of fine yarn.”** Yarn fineness strongly affects spinning time. Without a common yarn count and a definition of whose working hours are counted—including assistants—the productivity figures look more directly comparable than the chapter establishes.

- **The cotton-picker weights and the bale weights.** Northup’s daily picking quota concerns seed cotton before ginning, whereas commercial bale weights concern ginned lint. State this distinction so readers do not compare the two as equivalent quantities.

- **“By their estimate, the labour cost of making cotton goods…”** Identify whether the estimate concerns spinning, weaving or an entire cloth-production process, and whether it holds cloth quality constant. “Cotton goods” is too broad for the numerical comparison.

- **“The same estimates look different measured against investment instead of income.”** Give the date or period and clarify whether the numerator is slave-trading profits alone or includes plantation profits. Otherwise the percentages appear interchangeable despite potentially different coverage.

- **“A study published in 2024”; “One recent reassessment”; “Others dispute this reading.”** Name the authors and identify the disputed proposition. This matters particularly where an unnamed study supplies the principal counterargument to a named scholar.

- **“Spain had more colonies than Britain.”** “More” could mean territory, population, number of jurisdictions or value of trade, and the relevant date is unspecified. It also does not tell the reader which proposed mechanism—profits, markets, raw materials or state support—the comparison tests.

## 3. Overall

The chapter needs substantive correction before publication, particularly in its account of heat science, factory power and the spinning machines.  
It presents several competing explanations, but overstates Allen’s necessary conditions, treats uncertain scientific influence on Newcomen as established, and presents some criticisms of Pomeranz as more decisive than they are.  
The discussions of institutions, empire and slavery identify relevant mechanisms, although their counterarguments need clearer dates, denominators and connections to the claims being challenged.  
Readability is weakened where simple language conceals missing mechanical steps or makes unlike quantitative measures appear comparable.  
I count **13 major and 17 minor factual flags**, with the separate clarity flags excluded from those totals.
