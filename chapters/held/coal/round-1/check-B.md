## 1. Factual flags

Assessment based only on the specified draft and my existing knowledge; no outside sources consulted. “Major” means a claim materially affects the chapter’s explanation or representation of a scholarly argument; “minor” means a localized error or overstatement.

1. **“The further down they went, the faster the water came in”; “Below a certain depth no number of horses could keep up.”**  
   Water inflow depends on geology, aquifers and fractures, not simply depth. Greater depth increases the work needed to raise each unit of water; horse drainage generally encountered economic and practical limits, not a universal depth beyond which it was impossible.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

2. **“Nearly all the energy any society used came from the sunlight of the past year or so.”**  
   Fuelwood embodies years or decades of growth, and peat much longer periods. Wrigley’s distinction concerns dependence on organic production and land, rather than energy literally harvested within approximately one year.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

3. **“Over ninety-nine of every hundred parts of the coal’s energy went up the chimney or into warming the cylinder and the cooling water.”**  
   This follows a statement that Smeaton’s engines achieved 1.4 percent efficiency, which implies losses of 98.6 percent. Restrict the greater-than-99-percent statement to the earlier engines for which it applies; the chapter repeatedly treats it as characteristic of all Newcomen engines.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

4. **“It could not be sold”; “The fee was worth paying only where coal was dear, which meant Cornwall”; “made sense only where coal was almost free.”**  
   These turn a relative-cost explanation into an exclusive rule. Slack had low or sometimes negligible market value, but was not universally unsaleable; atmospheric engines also operated where fuel cost money, including Cornwall. Watt engines had markets outside Cornwall, and their value depended on fuel savings, useful output, reliability and other costs—not simply whether coal was “free” or “dear.”  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

5. **“Its walls never cooled”; “Nothing had to be reheated at every stroke.”**  
   Watt greatly reduced the repeated cooling and reheating of the working cylinder; he did not eliminate heat loss, temperature variation or cylinder condensation. These sentences describe an idealization as an achieved physical condition.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

6. **“A machine for boring cannon in which the cutting tool was carried on a bar supported at both ends, running right through the casting.”**  
   This appears to conflate Wilkinson’s cannon-boring machinery with the arrangement used for open-ended engine cylinders. A cannon barrel has a closed breech, so a bar cannot simply pass through it in the same manner. The transfer of precision-boring techniques is sound, but the exact mechanical description needs checking.  
   **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

7. **“Against Smeaton’s best improved engines … roughly half to two-thirds”; “the Watt engine reached about 2 percent.”**  
   The efficiency figures supplied do not support these savings. Replacing a 1.4-percent-efficient engine with a 2-percent-efficient one reduces fuel consumption by approximately 30 percent, not 50–67 percent. Different generations or operating conditions may explain the discrepancy, but they must be identified.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

8. **“So the first factories did not run on coal. They ran on water”; “Cromford as the first factory in the modern sense.”**  
   Water dominated important early mechanized textile factories, but “the first factories” is too broad. Earlier centralized manufacturing establishments used several power arrangements, and Lombe’s Derby silk mill predates Cromford while meeting the stated criteria of concentrated machinery, common power and organized labour. Describe Cromford as a formative early cotton factory.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

9. **“From 1721 … banned wearing or using printed or painted cotton cloth at all, foreign or domestic. Cloth woven with a linen warp and a cotton weft … was allowed.”**  
   The legislation had exemptions and implementation dates that this compresses away. More importantly, permission for British printed linen–cotton mixtures is associated with the Manchester Act of 1736; the treatment of unprinted fustians and printed mixed fabrics should not be collapsed into one blanket exception dating from 1721.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

10. **“The mule … combined the roller of the water frame with the moving carriage of the jenny.”**  
    The mule combined roller drafting with intermittent drawing and spinning associated with the jenny, but its moving spindle carriage was a distinctive arrangement. In the jenny, the spindles remained stationary while the operator moved the clasp or drawing mechanism. The present wording suggests the same carriage was transferred between machines.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

11. **“The workers’ job shrank to feeding, watching and mending broken threads.”**  
    This is premature for the machinery just described. Before effective self-acting mules, mule spinners performed skilled operations controlling the carriage, drawing, twisting and winding; even later machinery did not immediately eliminate skilled male mule spinning. The sentence obscures an important source of worker bargaining power and the reason manufacturers sought self-acting machinery.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

12. **“50,000 hours for the best Indian hand spinners … whose yarn is not exactly comparable, but which shows the order of the change.”**  
    An untraced figure for a different yarn cannot establish the magnitude of a productivity comparison. Yarn fineness and quality strongly affect labour requirements. The direction of technological change is secure, but this numerical comparison does not demonstrate its scale without comparable products and labour definitions.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

13. **“In 1793 Eli Whitney built a saw gin … in which wire teeth on a turning drum pulled the fibre through slots.”**  
    Whitney’s original device is usually distinguished as a wire-toothed or spike gin; the subsequent saw gin used circular toothed saws and is associated particularly with Hodgen Holmes. The passage describes Whitney’s mechanism while giving it the later mechanism’s name.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

14. **“Nearly 4 million bales, each of about 227 kilograms, in 1860.”**  
    Antebellum bales were not uniformly standardized at 500 pounds. The commonly cited 1859–60 crop is approximately 4.8 million contemporary bales, whereas a total expressed in standardized 500-pound equivalents will differ. Specify the crop year and whether these are actual bales or standardized equivalents.  
    **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

15. **“The labour that grew and picked it was enslaved”; “cotton, a fibre grown abroad by enslaved people and sold in markets opened by force.”**  
    Enslaved labour dominated the expanding American export supply and was fundamental to British cotton manufacturing, but not all cotton was slave-grown. Indian and other supplies involved different labour systems, and British cotton exports also entered markets through ordinary commercial competition. The categorical wording erases variation already acknowledged elsewhere in the chapter.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

16. **“A published record of sworn evidence.”**  
    House of Commons select committees generally lacked the power to administer oaths in 1832; general authority came later in the nineteenth century. The Sadler testimony should be called recorded parliamentary evidence, not sworn evidence. The later factory inspectors’ explicit oath-taking power is a separate matter.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

17. **“His testimony describes the 1820s.”**  
    On the draft’s own dates, Crabtree was 22 in 1832 and worked at the mill from age eight for four years, approximately 1818–1822. His evidence concerns the late 1810s and early 1820s.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

18. **“Then the power loom arrived.… By the same series, weavers’ money earnings fell by more than half by 1820.”**  
    This sequence makes power looms appear to explain the initial earnings collapse, although the chapter itself reports relatively few power looms before 1820. Handloom earnings were already falling under pressures including expanding labour supply, competition and postwar conditions. Power weaving subsequently intensified and completed displacement; it should not carry the whole causal explanation.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

19. **“Acemoglu and Robinson argue that 1688 gave England institutions open to everyone.”**  
    This misstates their concept of inclusive institutions. Their argument concerns constraints on power, broader participation and incentives for investment and innovation, not universal inclusion after 1688. Political and economic exclusions persisted by sex, property, religion and imperial status.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

20. **“[Williams] made two claims”; “The first claim has been tested hardest.”**  
    Williams’s argument also encompasses the wider contribution of Atlantic slavery and commerce to capitalism, not merely identifiable profits financing factories. His explanation of abolition through economic change has itself been subjected to exceptionally extensive testing and criticism, especially over the alleged decline of the British Caribbean. Ranking the financing claim as the one “tested hardest” is doubtful.  
    **Confidence: medium. Severity: minor.**

21. **“Parthasarathi argues instead that British policy decided the contest”; “policy decided the matter.”**  
    This reduces his explanation to protection and imperial coercion. His account also emphasizes comparative wages, ecological pressures, technological responses and state support. Policy is important to his argument, but presenting it as a simple alternative to productivity misrepresents how those mechanisms interact.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

22. **“The Americas … supplied cotton, sugar and timber that Britain could not have grown.”**  
    Britain could grow timber, and did. The argument concerns the acreage and opportunity costs of meeting demand domestically, not climatic impossibility for all three commodities. Separate tropical crops from timber.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

23. **“The dispute is unresolved”; “Neither side disputes … that the gains went to the owners.”**  
    The treatment of Baptist and Olmstead–Rhode risks presenting their causal explanations as equally supported. Increased coercion alone does not establish the source of the measured productivity increase, and Baptist’s handling of quantitative evidence has attracted substantial methodological criticism. Moreover, the claim that gains went to owners needs qualification: enslaved workers were denied control over the gains, but lower prices could also benefit manufacturers and consumers.  
    **Confidence: medium. Severity: major.**

24. **“Traditional trades untouched by the new machines.”**  
    Occupational totals cannot establish that trades were untouched by mechanization. Workers outside factories could use machine-made inputs or tools and experience changes in transport, prices and demand. Attribute the occupational findings to Broadberry and colleagues without turning them into a finding of technological isolation.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

25. **“The Liverpool and Manchester Railway … was the first to rely entirely on steam locomotives.”**  
    Its Liverpool terminal arrangements included stationary-engine rope haulage, notably through the Wapping tunnel. Its pioneering role is better described in terms of a major intercity railway providing regular steam-hauled passenger and freight services; “entirely” is inaccurate.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

26. **“A society’s energy use no longer competed with its food supply for land”; “Coal had no such ceiling until the air itself became one.”**  
    Coal greatly relaxed the land constraint on energy supply; it did not abolish competition for land or all resource constraints. Food, fodder, timber and textile fibres still required land, while coal faced geological, extraction, transport and economic limits. The chapter’s central explanation should be framed as a large relaxation of a constraint, not its complete disappearance.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

27. **“Two things are not speculative. Water could have carried the textile industry further than it did.”**  
    The existence of additional technically usable water power does not establish how much further textile production could economically have expanded with it. Location, seasonal reliability, reservoirs, capital costs and labour supply make this a counterfactual inference—the very issue the preceding paragraphs acknowledge as disputed.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

28. **“None of them knew the others, and each depended on the rest.”**  
    This is an illustrative composite, not an established historical relationship. Some participants could know one another, and a displaced Bengali weaver was not necessarily an input into, or reciprocal beneficiary of, the particular cloth supply chain described. Distinguish production dependencies from the competitive effects of the same trading system.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

29. **“Manchester was a market town”; “the world’s first industrial city.”**  
    Manchester was already an important textile manufacturing and commercial centre by the 1770s, so “market town” understates its existing economy. “First industrial city” is a conventional characterization rather than an uncontested chronological fact; it depends on the definition of industrialization and the treatment of earlier manufacturing cities.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

## 2. Clarity flags

- **Newcomen’s pump:** “The pump rods lifted a load of water up the shaft” leaves the actual pump unexplained. Briefly describe how a pump piston and one-way valves raise water, potentially through successive stages. Otherwise readers may imagine the rods lifting buckets or the engine sucking water directly from the bottom.

- **Atmospheric pressure:** “A piston … carries roughly two tonnes of air on its top face” can suggest a literal load sitting on the piston. Say that atmospheric pressure exerts a downward force equivalent to approximately two tonnes’ weight, opposed by pressure underneath.

- **Savery’s suction limit:** Separate suction lift from pressure delivery. Approximately nine metres is a practical suction limit; the theoretical sea-level limit is about ten metres. Explain that the second stage could push water higher, but required hazardous pressures.

- **Watt’s closed cylinder:** The account closes the cylinder’s top without explaining what replaces the atmosphere above the piston. For the early single-acting Watt engine, steam above the piston supplied pressure while condensation created low pressure below it.

- **Watt’s condenser:** The cold-water bath description omits the direct cold-water injection used in the usual jet condenser. Distinguish this from surface condensation, and explain that the air pump removed both accumulated water and non-condensable gases.

- **Double acting:** “Steam pushed the piston in both directions” should explain that steam admission and connection to the condenser alternated between the cylinder’s ends. Otherwise it appears to contradict the subsequent statement that Watt’s engines relied on a vacuum.

- **Rotary drive:** The sun-and-planet description needs a diagram or one further sentence about how the planet wheel’s orbit turns the output shaft. Explain also that a flywheel helped smooth the engine’s uneven impulses; the governor regulated speed rather than creating rotary motion.

- **Spinning machines:** Readers need the practical distinction between their products: the jenny initially produced relatively weak yarn chiefly suitable for weft, the water frame produced stronger warp yarn, and the mule combined strength with greater fineness. This makes the sequence intelligible as more than increasing spindle counts.

- **“Too heavy for human muscle”:** Machine weight is not the relevant explanation. Arkwright’s machinery required sustained mechanical power at useful operating scale; early arrangements included horse power before water-powered mills.

- **Yarn counts and productivity:** Define the cotton count’s standard length if retaining the technical discussion, and identify whether the labour figures include assistants and preparatory work. “A single mule” and “one spinner” are not interchangeable measures of labour productivity.

- **“A slubber”:** This was a worker engaged in preparing fibres into loose strands, with supervisory responsibilities in the described arrangement—not simply a generic synonym for overlooker. Define “cardings” as well.

- **Power statistics:** Explain that installed horsepower measures capacity, not annual energy delivered. Identify what supplies the remainder when water is seven-tenths and steam one-fifth, and keep Britain, the United Kingdom, Lancashire and Greater Manchester geographically distinct.

- **Indian market shares:** The change from about 10 percent in 1850 to 35 percent in the late 1850s needs a clear indication of whether the estimates use comparable definitions and denominators. “Pieces” of exported cloth also vary in size and cannot automatically be treated as equal quantities.

- **Wages and living standards:** Specify whether wage series represent men, particular occupations or households. Low urban life expectancy demonstrates severe health conditions, but does not by itself establish a deterioration over time or settle the real-wage debate.

- **The scholarly framework:** “Historians largely agree on the facts set out above” is confusing after several explicitly disputed measurements and causal claims. Distinguish the broad chronology from contested estimates and explanations.

- **Traceability:** “One popular history,” “one account,” “a 2020 recalculation,” and the unnamed Glasgow study leave important claims impossible to identify precisely within the draft. Give author–date references, especially for numerical claims; the source list also lacks identifiable entries for several scholars discussed in the text.

## 3. Overall

The broad chronology is recognizable, but categorical claims about fuel costs, factories, labour and the disappearance of resource limits repeatedly exceed the evidence described.  
The engineering narrative needs corrections to the condenser, spinning mule and gin, together with reconciliation of its fuel-saving figures.  
Allen, Mokyr and Pomeranz receive identifiable arguments and objections, while Acemoglu–Robinson and Parthasarathi are reduced to stronger and simpler claims than they make.  
The slavery discussion needs clearer separation of established coercion, disputed productivity mechanisms and the distribution of economic gains, while non-specialist readability suffers where machinery and numerical comparisons remain underexplained.
