# Chapter 48 — Why did the factory run on coal?

**Place and years:** Britain, about 1700 to 1850, with links to the American South and Bengal.

**The question:** Why did an industrial economy powered by coal appear first in Britain, and why then?

**Working answer to test:** Early engines were wasteful but ran where coal was almost free. Why industrialization began in Britain is one of the most argued questions in economic history, and the chapter lays out the main explanations.

## 1. Opening: water in the mine

- Open at a British coal or metal mine around 1700. The shallow seams are worked out, the shafts go deeper, and water seeps in faster than it can be lifted.
- The existing answer: buckets and chain pumps driven by horses walking in a circle, or by a waterwheel where a stream allowed. State what a team of horses cost to keep and how deep they could drain. **[CHECK: a documented example of the cost or number of horses at a named mine]**
- The first engine: Thomas Newcomen, an ironmonger from Dartmouth, and his engine of 1712 at a colliery near Dudley Castle. **[CHECK: date, site name, depth and volume of water raised per minute]**
- Before him, Thomas Savery's pump, patented 1698 and advertised as "The Miner's Friend." It could not lift water far and tended to burst. **[CHECK: dates, and that Newcomen had to work under Savery's patent]**
- Close the opening on the question: this machine wasted nearly all its fuel. Why did anyone buy it?

## 2. The world before: an economy that ran on sunlight

- Before coal, nearly all energy came from the year's sunlight: food for human and animal muscle, wood for heat, wind and falling water for mills. E. A. Wrigley's term is the "organic economy." Every use of energy competed for land: land for food, for horse fodder, for firewood.
- That set a ceiling. A society could grow richer for a while and then ran short of land. This is the ceiling chapter 26 met in Song China.
- England had begun leaving that ceiling early. London heated its houses with coal shipped down the coast from Newcastle. **[CHECK: British coal output about 1700 and its share of European output; London's coal imports]**
- So by 1700 Britain already had a large coal industry with a drainage problem. The engine was invented to serve an industry that existed, not the other way round.

## 3. What people knew

- They knew the air has weight. Torricelli's barometer (1643), von Guericke's demonstrations with an evacuated sphere that teams of horses could not pull apart (1650s), and Denis Papin's piston moved by condensing steam (about 1690). **[CHECK: dates]**
- This matters for the running dispute: the first engine did not push with steam. It used steam to make a vacuum and let the weight of the atmosphere do the work. The science of the atmosphere came first and the engine followed.
- They did not know what heat was. There was no concept of energy and no law saying how much work a given amount of heat could yield. Sadi Carnot worked that out in 1824 by studying engines that already existed. Here the machine came first and the science followed.
- How much did Newcomen know of the science? Uncertain and debated. **[CHECK: what is known of Newcomen's contact with natural philosophers]**

## 4. Mechanism: how the atmospheric engine worked

Explain so a reader could explain it to someone else. One diagram.

1. A boiler makes steam at barely above air pressure.
2. Steam fills a vertical cylinder beneath a piston, and the weight of the pump rods on the far end of a rocking beam pulls the piston up.
3. A jet of cold water is sprayed into the cylinder. The steam condenses to a small volume of water, leaving a partial vacuum.
4. The atmosphere, pressing on the top of the piston, drives it down. The beam rocks and lifts the pump rods and the water.
5. Repeat, about twelve times a minute. **[CHECK: strokes per minute, typical power in horsepower]**

- The flaw: every stroke cooled the whole cylinder, and the next charge of steam had to heat it again. Most of the fuel went to reheating iron. **[CHECK: thermal efficiency of a Newcomen engine, usually given as under 1 percent]**
- Why it paid anyway: at the pithead it burned small coal and slack that could not be sold. Fuel was close to free, and the alternative was fifty horses or a drowned mine.
- Where it did not pay: the tin and copper mines of Cornwall, which had water to pump and no coal. Every ton arrived by sea. Cornwall is where fuel economy first mattered, and it is where Watt found his first customers. **[CHECK: number of Newcomen engines built by about 1730 and by 1775 or 1800]**

## 5. What had to change: Watt and after

- James Watt, instrument maker to the University of Glasgow, asked to repair a model Newcomen engine (1763–64). He saw where the heat went. His answer (1765): condense the steam in a separate vessel kept cold, so the cylinder stays hot. Patent 1769. **[CHECK: dates]**
- It took a decade to build one that worked, because no one could bore a cylinder true enough to seal a piston. John Wilkinson's machine for boring cannon solved it (1774–75). Note the link to gunmaking: military demand produced the precision the engine needed. **[CHECK]**
- Partnership with Matthew Boulton in Birmingham from 1775. Their pricing: the customer paid a third of the value of the coal saved compared with a Newcomen engine. The business model shows where the value lay. **[CHECK: royalty terms; fuel saving of roughly two-thirds to three-quarters]**
- Boulton's line to James Boswell in 1776: "I sell here, Sir, what all the world desires to have — POWER." **[CHECK: exact wording and source]**
- Rotary motion (early 1780s): until then engines only rocked up and down, which suits a pump. Turning a shaft is what let an engine drive machinery. **[CHECK: sun-and-planet gear date, double-acting engine, governor]**
- The patent ran to 1800 after Parliament extended it. Whether it delayed better engines is debated. After it lapsed, Richard Trevithick and the Cornish engineers moved to high-pressure steam, which made engines small and strong enough to put on wheels. **[CHECK: dates; the debate over the patent]**
- Coal had to reach the engine. The Duke of Bridgewater's canal (1761) brought coal into Manchester and cut its price sharply. **[CHECK: date and the "halved the price" claim]**
- Important correction to the simple story: steam did not take over quickly. Around 1800 most British factories still ran on water. Steam passed water in cotton only around the 1820s–30s. **[CHECK: installed steam horsepower about 1800, 1830, 1870; when steam overtook water in cotton; source von Tunzelmann or Kanefsky]**

## 6. Steam meets cotton

- Demand came first. Printed Indian cotton cloth was so popular in Britain that Parliament restricted it (Calico Acts, 1700 and 1721) to protect wool and silk. British makers set out to copy a product India made better and cheaper. Ties back to chapter 44. **[CHECK: dates and what the Acts banned]**
- The bottleneck was spinning. A sequence of machines broke it:
  - Hargreaves's spinning jenny (1760s), hand-powered, several spindles at once.
  - Arkwright's water frame (patent 1769) and his mill at Cromford (1771), driven by a waterwheel. This is the first factory in the modern sense: machines, a power source, and workers on a clock.
  - Crompton's mule (1779), fine strong yarn.
  - Cartwright's power loom (1785), not practical until the 1820s.
  - Roberts's self-acting mule (1825–30), built during a strike to do without skilled spinners.
  **[CHECK: all dates; fall in the price of yarn 1780s–1830s; hours of labor to spin a pound of cotton, India by hand versus mule]**
- Explain a spinning machine briefly: drawing out fibers and twisting them, and why doing it on many spindles at once from one power shaft changed the cost.
- The raw material could not be grown in Britain. Follow it back: first the Caribbean and the Ottoman lands, then, after Eli Whitney's gin (1793) made short-staple cotton cheap to clean, the American South. **[CHECK: share of British raw cotton from the US about 1800, 1830, 1860; US cotton output growth; number of enslaved people in the US 1790 and 1860]**
- The mill and the plantation grew together. A Lancashire mill hand and an enslaved picker in Mississippi were parts of one chain, joined by Liverpool merchants and credit. Ties to chapters 43, 51 and 52.
- The other end of the chain: Indian hand spinners and weavers lost their export markets and then much of their home market. How far this "deindustrialized" India, and how much British tariffs and Company rule caused it, is debated. Chapter 49 returns to the Indian cloth maker. **[CHECK: Indian textile exports to Britain, British cloth exports to India, 1800–1850; the main positions in the debate]**

## 7. Every party's perspective

For each: what they wanted, what they could decide, what they bore.

- **The mine owner.** Drain deeper or abandon the pit. The engine is a calculation.
- **The mechanic.** Newcomen was an ironmonger, Watt an instrument maker, and the engines were built and improved by millwrights, smiths and clockmakers. Britain had an unusual number of them. This is evidence for one of the explanations in section 8.
- **The mill owner.** Water power was cheap but tied the mill to a river in a valley, often far from workers, and stopped in drought or frost. Steam cost more per unit of power for decades, but it could be put in a town where labor was plentiful and it ran when the owner chose. Andreas Malm argues this control over labor and time, not cheapness, is why owners switched. **[CHECK: Malm's argument stated fairly, and the main criticism of it]**
- **The mill worker.** Many were women and children. Describe the day: hours, the pace set by the machine, fines, the heat and dust. Use one piece of sworn testimony from the parliamentary inquiries of 1832–33. **[CHECK: a real, citable quotation from the Sadler Committee or the 1833 Factory Commission; typical hours; share of children in the cotton workforce; terms of the 1833 Factory Act]** Note the limits of this evidence: the Sadler Committee was assembled to make a case.
- **The handloom weaver.** A skilled man working at home on his own time. The power loom did not improve his work. It ended his trade. **[CHECK: number of handloom weavers about 1820 and 1850; their earnings about 1800 and 1830s]** Chapter 49 covers the machine-breakers.
- **The enslaved cotton picker.** Use a first-hand account, such as Solomon Northup's description of picking quotas and the weighing of each day's cotton (1853). **[CHECK: exact passage]** Note the debate over why picking rates rose: Edward Baptist argues rising violence, Olmstead and Rhode argue better cotton varieties. **[CHECK]**
- **Did ordinary people gain?** For the first two generations, not clearly. Output per worker rose while real wages barely moved until about 1830–1840, then climbed. Robert Allen calls this "Engels' pause." Average heights fell and city death rates were high. **[CHECK: real wage figures; Manchester or Liverpool life expectancy about 1840; the optimist and pessimist positions]**

## 8. Why Britain? The contested cause

State that historians agree on the facts above and disagree on the cause. Set out each explanation, the evidence it explains, and its weak point. Do not pick a winner beyond what the evidence allows.

1. **High wages and cheap coal (Robert Allen).** British wages were high and energy was cheap compared with the Continent and Asia, so machines that swapped coal and capital for labor paid in Britain and nowhere else. Explains why the jenny and the Newcomen engine were profitable in England and ignored in France. Weak point: critics find that the women and children who actually spun were poorly paid. **[CHECK: Allen's claim; the Humphries and Schneider critique]**
2. **A culture of useful knowledge (Joel Mokyr).** An "Industrial Enlightenment": the belief that nature could be understood and put to use, spread through societies, lectures and print, and a deep pool of skilled craftsmen who could turn an idea into a working machine. Explains the stream of small improvements. Weak point: hard to measure, and France had the science too. **[CHECK]**
3. **Coal in the right place and land overseas (Kenneth Pomeranz; E. A. Wrigley).** The richest parts of China and of Europe were about level until around 1800. Britain's coal lay near its industry and near water transport, while China's lay far from its richest region. The Americas supplied cotton, sugar and timber that would have needed millions of extra British acres. Explains the timing of the divergence. Weak point: other historians date the gap in living standards earlier. **[CHECK: Pomeranz's claim; the "ghost acres" figure; the main objection]**
4. **Institutions (Douglass North and Barry Weingast; Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson).** After 1688 Parliament constrained the crown, property was secure and the state could borrow cheaply. Ties to chapter 45. Weak point: the Dutch had similar institutions earlier and did not industrialize first, and there is a century's gap between 1688 and the mills.
5. **Empire, slavery and the state (Eric Williams; later work).** Profits, markets and raw materials from the slave colonies, a navy that protected trade, and tariffs that shielded British cotton from Indian cloth. Explains why cotton, an imported fiber with export markets, led. Weak point: the profits were modest beside the whole economy, and how much they financed industry is disputed. Recent work has revived the argument. **[CHECK: Williams's thesis; the standard criticism; one recent restatement such as Berg and Hudson 2023; Parthasarathi on tariffs]**

- Point out that these are not exclusive. Most historians now combine several, and they disagree about the weights.
- Tie to the book's running dispute: explanations 1, 3 and 5 are about resources and material conditions, 2 and 4 about ideas and rules. The chapter is a case where both were needed and neither was enough.
- Return to chapter 26: Song China had coal, iron, printing and markets, and no industrial revolution. Which of these explanations survives that comparison?

## 9. Consequences

- **Immediate.** Manchester, a market town, became the first industrial city. **[CHECK: Manchester population about 1770, 1800, 1850]** British coal output multiplied. **[CHECK: output 1700, 1800, 1850]** Cotton goods became Britain's largest export. **[CHECK: share of exports]**
- **The railway.** High-pressure engines on rails, first to move coal. Stockton and Darlington (1825), Liverpool and Manchester (1830). Chapter 50 takes up what railways did to nations. **[CHECK]**
- **Later.** An economy that grows by burning stored carbon. For the first time, energy use no longer competed with food for land, and growth could continue for generations. The carbon went into the air. Chapter 82 picks this up. **[CHECK: when atmospheric CO2 began to rise measurably]**
- **What persisted.** Waterwheels, horses and hand work lasted for decades alongside steam. Most British workers in 1850 were not in factories. **[CHECK: share of workforce in factories about 1850]**

## 10. A plausible alternative

- Water. It was the incumbent, it was cheaper, and engineers proposed large reservoir schemes to make it reliable. Robert Thom built one at Greenock in the 1820s, and similar schemes were proposed for Lancashire. **[CHECK: Thom, Shaws Water Works, dates, and why the larger schemes were not built]**
- What staying with water would have required: owners sharing a river and agreeing rules, and bringing workers to the valleys. Say where this becomes speculation: water could have carried textiles further, but it could not have driven railways or scaled without limit.
