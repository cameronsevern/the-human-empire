## Factual flags

1. **Major — the revision reintroduces the corrected claim about pre-plague immobility.**

   > “With nowhere better to go, he stayed where he was and did what was asked of him.”

   The preceding “little to gain” allows for migration; this addition effectively removes it. Serfs migrated before the Black Death, both with permission and without it, to rural settlements and towns. Labour abundance weakened their bargaining position; it did not mean they necessarily stayed and complied. This is a newly introduced regression, rather than a repetition of an already resolved flag. [Matt Raven’s study of servile migration, c. 1336–1361](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0268416024000183).

2. **Major — the Egyptian comparison still contradicts its own later qualification.**

   > “Instead they did worse, and so did everyone else.”

   Later:

   > “Historians still argue over how the ordinary Egyptian worker fared in between, and some find that wages in Cairo rose for a time, as they did in England.”

   The later qualification fixes the earlier check’s specific wage assertion, but “everyone else” leaves universal impoverishment in place. Aggregate agricultural decline and higher earnings for some survivors can coexist. The correct distinction is between the country’s diminished output and the differing fortunes of its inhabitants. Pamuk and Shatzmiller reconstruct substantial post-plague wage gains. [Their study](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050714000072).

3. **Major — the description of Egyptian landholding wrongly makes one arrangement universal and permanent.**

   > “What he could not do was find a rival landlord in the next valley who would offer him better terms, because all the landlords belonged to the same army.”

   Also reinforced by the new sentence:

   > “He had no neighbour bidding against him and no fields of his own going to waste.”

   Military revenue assignments were central to Mamluk rule, but they were not the only form of landholding throughout the chapter’s period. Religious endowments and private interests mattered, and the allocation system changed substantially after the plague. By the beginning of the sixteenth century, roughly ten twenty-fourths of cultivable land was held as religious endowments. Military dominance can support the chapter’s interpretation; universal membership in one army cannot establish the absence of competing interests or alternative tenures. [Albrecht Fuess, EGYLandscape research paper](https://mhshaaban.github.io/minimal-mistakes/papers/June2020_Fuess/).

4. **Minor — the new explanation conflates transferring labour responsibility with reducing labour requirements.**

   > “Both were ways of drawing an income from land with very few hands.”

   “Both” means renting fields out and turning them over to sheep. Leasing relieved the landlord of organising cultivation and hiring labour; it did not itself make the tenant’s farming require fewer workers. Sheep farming could reduce labour requirements. The distinction matters because the paragraph explains how the economy adapted to scarcity. [Research on the shift from direct demesne management to leasing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/lordship-state-formation-and-local-authority-in-late-medieval-and-early-modern-england/introduction/6C61C3774116B1DFB693AE6C8780AEB8).

5. **Minor — the Siena caption contradicts the corrected account in the body.**

   > “Its tall wall, on the left, still stands beside the striped cathedral that was built instead.”

   The striped cathedral already existed when the enlargement began; it was intended to become the enlarged building’s cross-arm. It was not built instead of the abandoned project. The body now explains this correctly, but the caption retains the opposite chronology. [Siena cathedral authority’s construction history](https://operaduomo.siena.it/en/panorama-and-new-cathedral/).

6. **Minor — flea transmission is made automatic.**

   > “A flea that bites an infected animal takes in the bacteria with the blood, and passes them on to the next animal it bites.”

   An infected flea **can** transmit plague through a bite; infection of the next animal is not guaranteed. This is a factual distinction, not a request to soften the narrator’s position. [CDC explanation of plague transmission](https://www.cdc.gov/plague/causes/index.html).
