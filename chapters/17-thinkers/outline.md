# Chapter 17 — Why did so many great thinkers live at the same time?

**Line under the title:** China, the north of India and Greece, from about 2,600 years ago to about 2,300 years ago.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,500 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Between about 2,600 and 2,300 years ago, three lands with no contact between them each produced teachers whose arguments are still alive: Confucius and his rivals in China, the Buddha on the Ganges plain, the first natural philosophers and Socrates in the Greek cities. What the three lands shared was a political condition. Each was divided among many small states at war. Rulers in that position needed able advisers and hired them from anywhere; a thinker who failed at one court, or offended one city, could walk to the next; cities and trade fed people who did no farming; and a cheap way of keeping words (bamboo strips, the alphabet, or a trained community of reciters) let an argument outlive the person who made it. The thinkers disagreed with each other about almost everything, which is the point: these are traditions of argument. Calling the period one "Axial Age" is a modern idea, and the evidence does not support a single worldwide turning point. The narrower pattern holds.

## The story

1. **Strips in the mud.** Answer first. The robbed tomb at Guodian, 1993: eight hundred bamboo strips. How a bamboo book was made, and what was in these ones.
2. **A teacher without a post.** The Zhou order breaks into a hundred states and then seven. Rulers need able men. Confucius: rule by example, the gentleman made by learning, fourteen years on the road, no ruler takes him.
3. **Walls and heads.** Two rival answers. Mozi: care for everyone alike, and defend the small city's walls. Shang Yang in Qin: reward and punishment, a rank for an enemy head.
4. **The tortoise in the mud.** Zhuangzi turns the job down. The Laozi sayings. Kings collecting scholars at the gate of the Qi capital.
5. **The man who walked away.** The Ganges plain: iron, rice, cities, rival states, wandering teachers. Siddhartha leaves home, wakes up, teaches a middle way, and opens his community to every rank. Kept by memory.
6. **Atoms and empty space.** A thousand Greek cities and an alphabet. Thales and the earthquake; his pupil corrects him; Democritus and atoms.
7. **The man who would not leave.** Socrates asks how to live, is tried, declines to escape. Plato writes it down.
8. **Many courts, many roads.** Why divided lands: advisers, rival patrons, cities, and a way to keep words. The "Axial Age" as a modern and contested idea. The states vanish; the arguments stay.
