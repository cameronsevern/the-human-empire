1. **Unsupported historical scene — high confidence.** “If the earth rests on water, the pupil asked, what does the water rest on?” And: “A pupil had contradicted his teacher, in public, and had offered reasons that anybody could weigh.” The surviving objection about what supports the water comes from **Aristotle**, centuries later. Anaximander did propose an unsupported earth, but a prompt, public exchange with Thales is not established. Present the contrasting explanations without inventing the encounter. Sources: [Aristotle, *On the Heavens*, II.13](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/heavens.2.ii.html); [Stanford’s discussion of the uncertain teacher–pupil relationships](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2018/entries/presocratics/).

2. **Factual error and breakable absolute — high confidence.** “Among them, rank depended on how long a person had been a member, and on nothing else.” The next sentences include women, but the transmitted monastic rules subordinate nuns to monks regardless of seniority. Seniority governed precedence within an order; it did not erase the distinction between the two orders. Whether those rules originated with the Buddha is disputed, so distinguish the recorded institution from claims about his personal decisions. Source: [*Aṅguttara Nikāya* 8.51](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/anguttara-nikaya-numbered-discourses/d/doc1964794.html).

3. **Overstated isolation — high confidence that the wording needs narrowing.** “As far as anyone can tell, no thinker in any of these three lands had heard of the thinkers in the other two.” The chapter’s period extends to roughly 300 BCE. Ancient accounts place Pyrrho and Anaxarchus in India with Alexander, encountering Indian thinkers. The identity of those teachers and their influence remain uncertain, but “no thinker” excludes reported contact within the stated period. Distinguish the earlier emergence of the traditions from later encounters. Source: [Stanford, “Pyrrho,” §§1, 7](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pyrrho/).

4. **Chronological error — high confidence.** “By that time, the Greeks lived in about a thousand independent cities, scattered along the shores of the Mediterranean and the Black Sea.” “By that time” refers to the adoption of the alphabet around the eighth century BCE. The familiar thousand-city figure covers cities identified across the **Archaic and Classical periods**, not a simultaneous eighth-century population of independent states. Nor does identification as a *polis* establish continuous independence. Source: [Hansen and Nielsen’s inventory of 1,035 poleis, covering approximately 650–325 BCE](https://academic.oup.com/book/47066).

5. **Direct contradiction with chapter 11; also an absolute — high confidence.** “Reading in Greece was therefore never the property of palace clerks or of priests.” Chapter 11 says: “With the palaces went the only people in Greece who knew how to write, since writing had been used for nothing but palace accounts.” The intended distinction is between **alphabetic literacy and the earlier palace script**, but “never” contradicts that earlier account. Scope the sentence to the new alphabet. Source: [chapter 11](chapters/11-bronze/chapter.md).

6. **Misleading omission in the trial story — high confidence.** “Asked to propose his own punishment, he suggested that the city should give him free dinners for life.” This happened, but the immediate next sentence—“The jury voted for death”—makes the dinners sound like his final counterproposal. In Plato’s account, Socrates subsequently proposed a fine, ultimately thirty minas guaranteed by friends. Omitting that changes the reader’s understanding of the jury’s choice. Source: [Plato, *Apology*, 36–38](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html).

7. **Misleading account of Confucian mourning — high confidence.** “Confucians taught that a son should mourn a dead parent for three years, with costly funerals and solemn music.” This combines separate targets of Mohist criticism into one practice. The *Analects* discussion of three-year mourning explicitly treats abstention from musical enjoyment as part of mourning. Expensive funerals and court music should be explained separately. Source: [*Analects* 17.21](https://ctext.org/text.pl?if=en&node=3943).

8. **Overstated political convergence — high confidence.** “Within about a century of the burial at Guodian, every one of the warring states of China had been conquered by a single one of them, and the cities of Greece and the kingdoms of the Ganges had gone the same way, into empires.” Literally, Qin did not conquer itself. More substantively, the Greek cities did not all undergo a comparable absorption: Rhodes remained independent of the Hellenistic kingdoms. The broad growth of imperial power is defensible; the uniform disappearance of these political systems is not. Source: [*The Oxford History of the Archaic Greek World*, discussion of Rhodes](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780199383542_A63381449/preview-9780199383542_A63381449.pdf).

9. **Misleading description of the central archaeological find — high confidence.** “One was the oldest copy yet found of a book of sayings about how to live and how to rule, which is still read all over the world.” Guodian yielded **three groups of Laozi material**, corresponding to portions of the received text, with different ordering and important variants. It is not simply one copy of the familiar book. Whether these were selections from a longer work or separate developing collections remains unresolved. Source: [Stanford, “Laozi,” §3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/laozi/).

10. **Faulty explanation of the atomist argument — high confidence.** “The cutting cannot go on for ever, or there would be nothing left for things to be made of.” Repeated halving does not itself make matter disappear. The ancient argument reported by Aristotle concerns the consequences of supposing a body divided **at every point**, a different claim. Likewise, “In the end there must be pieces too small and too hard to divide” misleadingly makes smallness the explanation: Democritean atoms were indivisible regardless of size. Source: [Stanford, “Democritus,” §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/democritus/).

11. **Overconfident use of a disputed biography — medium-high confidence.** “A generation earlier, a thinker who taught in Athens that the sun was a red-hot stone had been charged with offending the gods, and had simply moved to another city, where he was honoured.” Anaxagoras’s prosecution and departure have conflicting chronologies and accounts; some sources describe imprisonment and flight after conviction. “Simply moved” turns disputed evidence into an uncomplicated demonstration that escape was easy. His eventual refuge can carry the point without that claim. Source: [Stanford, “Anaxagoras,” §1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anaxagoras/).

12. **Internal contradiction in the opening explanation — high confidence.** “Rulers needed advice, a thinker who was turned away by one ruler could walk to the next, and writing kept the arguments alive after the people who made them were dead.” This presents writing as a shared mechanism across the three regions. The Buddhist section explicitly says the teaching initially survived through collective memorisation, and the conclusion correctly distinguishes the mechanisms. The opening needs that same distinction.

13. **Breakable absolute that overstates the argument.** “No ruler could silence an idea when its owner could cross a border in a week.” The possibility of refuge does not guarantee escape, protection, or transmission. The chapter itself has just described Shang Yang’s killing. Political fragmentation gave thinkers additional opportunities; it did not make suppression impossible. This is a specific breach of the style rule on absolutes, rather than an objection to the narrator taking a side.

14. **Unsupported counterfactual and missing explanation.** “In an empire with a single court, he would have failed once.” A single sovereign does not imply a single possible patron, appointment, or opportunity: even chapter 9 describes governors and local officials beneath an imperial ruler. Explain why competing sovereign courts offered **more independent opportunities**, rather than treating one rejection as necessarily final. Earlier context: [chapter 9](chapters/09-law/chapter.md).

15. **A general reader lacks the crucial comparison.** “Set the three lands side by side, and the same conditions appear in each.” Chapters 9–11 have already supplied rival kingdoms, cities, writing, trade and specialists hired from abroad. The reader can reasonably wonder why those earlier conditions did not produce the same result. This is not a contradiction, but a missing step in the central explanation. Moreover, the Greek examples demonstrate argument and mobility, while the ruler’s-demand-for-advisers mechanism is demonstrated chiefly through China. The comparison needs to distinguish what the stories actually establish from what they suggest.

16. **Explicit signpost sentences prohibited by `style.md`.** “One of his ideas was more radical than it sounded.” “Two things about this teaching set it against the priests.” “What remains is smaller and more solid.” Each announces the significance or organisation of the explanation that follows instead of stating it. These are clearer violations than ordinary connective transitions such as “First” or “Meanwhile.”
