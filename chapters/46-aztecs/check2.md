## Changed facts

No existing number, date, name, or quotation was changed or removed. However, the revision adds factual and causal assertions. Below, **“added” means there was no corresponding old sentence**; where useful, I quote the old sentence that the addition expands. These changes are not all errors.

1. **The painting’s Spanish presence**
   
   Old: “Here and there among them rides a Spaniard on a horse.”
   
   New: “The Spaniards in the picture are few. Here and there among the warriors rides a Spaniard on a horse.”
   
   The revision makes an explicit numerical characterization of the picture.

2. **Access to the island**
   
   Old: “It was joined to the shore by three raised roads, each wide enough for eight horsemen to ride abreast, with gaps crossed by wooden bridges that could be taken up.”
   
   Added: “A city on an island needs ways in and out, and this one had three.” / “With the bridges lifted, each road became a dead end over open water.”
   
   Three roads becomes three ways of entering or leaving, overlooking water transport.

3. **Lake salinity**
   
   Old: “Fresh water came across the lake in a stone channel from springs on the mainland.”
   
   New: “The lake itself was salty, and so fresh water came across the lake in a stone channel from springs on the mainland.”
   
   Adds salinity and makes it the explanation for the aqueduct.

4. **Imperial administration**
   
   Old: “For the most part they did not garrison these lands or govern them.”
   
   New: “For the most part they did not garrison them or govern them. In other words, they posted no soldiers there and sent no governors.”
   
   The new paraphrase drops the qualification in the sentence it explains.

5. **Battlefield deaths and religion**
   
   Old: “Most of the people who died on the stone were prisoners of war. For that reason a Mexica warrior went into battle hoping above all to capture.”
   
   New: “Most of the people who died on the stone were prisoners of war. That fact changed what a battle was for. A dead enemy left on the field was of no use to the sun, while a living one could be brought home to the pyramid.”
   
   Adds a distinct religious claim about people killed in combat.

6. **Tlaxcala’s encirclement**
   
   Old: “The Mexica cut them off from trade.”
   
   New: “The Mexica, who held the land on every side of them, cut them off from trade.”
   
   Adds a geographical assertion about control of the surrounding territory.

7. **Moctezuma’s opportunity and defensive position**
   
   Old: “His messengers had been watching the strangers since they landed, and painters had brought him pictures of the ships, the horses and the guns.”
   
   Added: “He had therefore had months to think about what to do with them.”
   
   Old: “When they came on regardless, with his enemies the Tlaxcalans beside them, he decided to let them into the city, where he could watch them and where they would be surrounded by water.”
   
   Added: “With the bridges taken up, the island would hold the guests as surely as it protected their host.”
   
   These make the preparation time and potential trapping mechanism explicit.

8. **The reason for retreat**
   
   Old: “On a rainy night at the end of June 1520, the Spaniards and their Tlaxcalan allies tried to slip out along the western road.”
   
   Added immediately before it: “With their hostage dead, the Spaniards had nothing left to bargain with, and they decided to run.”
   
   Introduces a specific explanation for the decision.

9. **Universal susceptibility**
   
   Old: “Nobody on the American mainland had ever had it.”
   
   Added: “That meant nobody there was protected, at any age.”
   
   Broadens the claim to everyone present on the mainland.

10. **Smallpox’s military timing and selectivity**
    
    Old: “Much of the army’s leadership died with him.”
    
    Added: “The city thus lost many of its commanders months before the enemy returned.”
    
    Old: “The Spaniards, who had met the disease as children, were hardly touched.”
    
    Added: “A sickness that struck one army and spared the other seemed to need an explanation.”
    
    The second addition changes a distinction between populations into a distinction between armies.

11. **Naval warfare and boat construction**
    
    Old: “Cortés had learned from the night on the western road that the city could not be taken while the Mexica controlled the lake.”
    
    Added: “On that night the canoes had done much of the killing, because whoever held the water held the roads that crossed it.”
    
    Old: “The ships were then taken apart.”
    
    Added immediately before it: “Tlaxcala, however, lay in the highlands, far from the water.”
    
    Adds an assessment of the canoes’ contribution and a geographical explanation for dismantling the ships.

12. **The blockade’s completeness**
    
    Old: “The boats then swept the canoes from the lake and cut off the food.”
    
    Added: “The island was now without fresh water and without supplies.”
    
    Converts the blockade into an immediate, complete deprivation.

13. **Continuity under Spanish rule**
    
    Old: “Spanish settlers were granted whole towns whose people had to work for them.”
    
    Added: “The old arrangement of local lords and yearly bills went on, with new people at the top.”
    
    Adds a broader claim about institutional continuity.

14. **The allies’ petitions**
    
    Old: “The allies responded as subjects of a king could. They went to law, and they wrote.”
    
    Added: “Their argument was always the same: they had served the king in the war, and the king owed them. Tlaxcala made the case most stubbornly.”
    
    Adds universality and a comparison with other petitioners.

15. **Broken royal promises**
    
    Old: “Tlaxcala’s privileges were whittled away as well.”
    
    Added immediately before it: “Even for Tlaxcala, the king’s promises did not hold.”
    
    Now expressly attributes the losses to broken royal promises.

16. **Depopulation’s cause**
    
    Old: “By then there were far fewer people on either side of the old quarrel.”
    
    Added: “The cause was disease, which went on arriving long after the war was over.”
    
    Turns the ensuing account of epidemics into an explicit causal explanation.

Other additions chiefly restate existing information or develop the chapter’s chosen interpretation: the three-language chain, tribute-backed fear, Tlaxcalan partnership, and demolition as a military method. I have not treated those interpretations as errors merely because they are confidently stated.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — battlefield deaths did have religious value.**
   
   > “A dead enemy left on the field was of no use to the sun, while a living one could be brought home to the pyramid.”
   
   The first clause is wrong. Nahua accounts describe the sun and earth consuming the blood and bodies of those killed in war; warriors killed in combat also accompanied the sun. Capturing someone alive made temple sacrifice possible, but battlefield death was not religiously useless. This is a new claim, distinct from the earlier check’s corrected sentence about warriors not fighting to kill. Source: Eduardo Matos Moctezuma, quoting Sahagún, [“El destino de los guerreros y la práctica de la cremación”](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/3827).

2. **Major — Moctezuma was not their last hostage, and his death does not establish the stated reason for retreat.**
   
   > “With their hostage dead, the Spaniards had nothing left to bargain with, and they decided to run.”
   
   Cortés reports taking Moctezuma’s children and other captive rulers with him during the escape. He explains the retreat through mounting casualties, exhausted and wounded soldiers, threatened escape routes, and shortages. The correct point is that their position had become untenable; “nothing left to bargain with” is contradicted by the surviving captives and continued negotiations. Source: [Cortés’s second letter](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Letters_of_Cortes_to_Emperor_Charles_V_-_Vol_1/Second_Letter,_October_30,_1520).

3. **Major — smallpox did not spare the opposing army.**
   
   > “A sickness that struck one army and spared the other seemed to need an explanation.”
   
   This contradicts both the chapter’s opening—most of the conquering army consisted of Indigenous allies—and its explicit account of deaths in Tlaxcala. The distinction is between largely immune Spaniards and susceptible Indigenous populations on both sides, not between two armies. This new sentence reintroduces a problem the earlier revision had fixed by acknowledging Tlaxcalan deaths. Source: [Cortés’s third letter](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Letters_of_Cortes_to_Emperor_Charles_V_-_Vol_2/Third_letter,_May_15,_1522), which records smallpox deaths in Cholula and Tlaxcala.

4. **Minor — the lake’s waters were not uniformly salty.**
   
   > “The lake itself was salty, and so fresh water came across the lake in a stone channel from springs on the mainland.”
   
   The lake system included fresh and saline waters. Before the conquest, a dike separated the fresher western waters around Tenochtitlan from saline waters farther east. The aqueduct supplied drinking water from mainland springs, but the new sentence gives an oversimplified description of the surrounding lake. Source: [“Relations between man and environment in the development of precolonial settlements in the basin of Mexico”](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0188-46112003000100015&script=sci_arttext).

5. **Minor — the blockade was not immediately complete.**
   
   > “The island was now without fresh water and without supplies.”
   
   Cutting the aqueduct did not instantly eliminate every delivery of water and food. Cortés describes assigning brigantines to intercept continuing supplies of fresh water, fruit, and maize after fighting inside the city had already begun. The accurate sequence is loss of the aqueduct followed by a tightening blockade and worsening shortages. Source: [Cortés’s third letter](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Letters_of_Cortes_to_Emperor_Charles_V_-_Vol_2/Third_letter,_May_15,_1522).

6. **Minor — “nobody there” contradicts the chapter’s account of Spanish immunity.**
   
   > “That meant nobody there was protected, at any age.”
   
   The intended population is the previously unexposed Indigenous population. As written, “there” refers to the American mainland and includes the Spaniards. The chapter subsequently says: “The Spaniards, who had met the disease as children, were hardly touched.” Restrict the statement to the population being discussed. Source: the internal contradiction in [chapter.md](chapters/46-aztecs/chapter.md:99).

## Clarity flags

1. **Three roads become three total routes.**
   
   > “A city on an island needs ways in and out, and this one had three.”
   
   A reader will understand three ways altogether, although canoes are central to the story. Specify three land approaches.

2. **A new explanation obscures a repaired qualification.**
   
   > “In other words, they posted no soldiers there and sent no governors.”
   
   “For the most part” in the preceding sentence fixed the earlier absolute. This paraphrase makes the exceptionless version prominent again. Explain the terms within the qualified sentence instead.

3. **The advantages do not add up.**
   
   > “Nevertheless, Cortés had three advantages, and the guns were the least of them.”
   
   The three subsequently listed are steel, horses, and Malintzin. Guns therefore appear to be a fourth advantage, despite being described as one of three.

4. **The interpreter transition overstates the communication problem.**
   
   > “Steel and horses, however, were of little use to a commander who could not talk to anyone.”
   
   “Anyone” is confusing when the paragraph immediately introduces a Spanish-speaking interpreter who knew Maya. The missing connection was to Nahuatl speakers, not communication with everyone.

5. **Several additions are signposts rather than explanations.**
   
   > “What they did with these lands afterwards was unusual.”
   
   > “There were two ways to look at these strangers.”
   
   > “Back in the city, meanwhile, the officer he had left behind did something that could not be undone.”
   
   Each announces information that the next sentence supplies. The last sentence’s location cue is useful; its suspense clause withholds the actual event.

6. **Some transitions repeat rather than move the explanation forward.**
   
   > “Those figures show whose war it was.”
   
   This sits between the troop totals and the already effective one-to-a-hundred comparison.
   
   > “The reasoning was grim and simple: a house that no longer stood could not be retaken at night.”
   
   This repeats the preceding explanation that demolition left nothing to defend.
   
   > “The brittleness of an empire held together by fear now showed.”
   
   The chapter has already explained “rich and brittle” and repeated the fear argument in the Malintzin and coastal passages. The defections themselves demonstrate it here.

7. **The petition passage adds unnecessary absolutes.**
   
   > “Their argument was always the same: they had served the king in the war, and the king owed them.”
   
   “Always” makes every petition depend on the same argument. The story only needs this to be a recurring argument.
   
   > “An ally who goes home leaves the country to the people who live in it. One who stays becomes the new master.”
   
   This turns the particular Spanish outcome into a universal rule about allies.

8. **The broken promise is unclear.**
   
   > “Even for Tlaxcala, the king’s promises did not hold.”
   
   The immediately preceding royal promise concerns protection from grants to Spanish settlers and direct dependence on the crown. The following evidence concerns tribute and grazing. Paying tribute to the crown does not itself contradict remaining under the crown. Cortés’s earlier promise of tribute exemption is a different commitment.

9. **The heading’s time range does not cover the ending.**
   
   > “Central Mexico, from 1428 to about 1560”
   
   The narrative explicitly reaches the epidemic of 1576 and population levels around 1620. The stated range gives readers the wrong expectation.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions, or body cross-references. I have not repeated the earlier check’s resolved flags.

## Overall

The revision preserves the original figures and chronology but is not wholly fact-neutral. Several explanatory additions introduce errors or internal contradictions, especially around sacrifice, retreat, and smallpox. The chapter’s central interpretation can stand; the new causal bridges need correction, and several signposts can be cut. No files were edited.
