I found the following issues, ranked by seriousness. Line numbers refer to [chapter.md](chapters/46-aztecs/chapter.md). No files were edited.

1. **Factual error; misleading account of warfare — line 33.**
   > “For that reason a Mexica warrior did not go into battle to kill.”

   Capturing opponents brought prestige and supplied sacrifices, but Mexica armies also deliberately killed enemies and destroyed settlements. The sentence turns one important incentive into an exclusive purpose, distorting the military comparison with the Spaniards. **Confidence: high.** Source: Barry Isaac, [“Aztec Warfare: Goals and Battlefield Comportment”](https://www.latinamericanstudies.org/aztecs/aztec-warfare.pdf).

2. **Factual error; exception-breaking absolute — line 21.**
   > “They did not garrison these lands or govern them.”

   Indirect rule was common, but the empire also installed garrisons and governors. Chalco and Tlatelolco provide documented examples of imposed military government. “They usually left local rulers in place” would preserve the point accurately. **Confidence: high.** Source: [“Aztec Empire,” *The Encyclopedia of Empire*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781118455074.wbeoe025).

3. **Doubtful factual claim central to the argument and ending — line 37.**
   > “For two generations the Tlaxcalans had no cotton, so they dressed in cloth made from cactus fibre, and no salt, so they ate their food without it.”

   Colonial accounts describe an embargo, but total isolation is not established. Archaeological research finds continuing imports of obsidian from Aztec-controlled sources and explicitly calls for separate testing of the cotton claim. This does **not** prove salt and cotton remained freely available; it means their complete absence cannot safely be presented as certain. The ending’s “republic without salt” inherits the problem. **Confidence: high that qualification is needed; availability of particular goods remains uncertain.** Source: Gentil, Vicencio Castellanos and Hirth, [“Aztec and Tlaxcalan Economic Interaction”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/latin-american-antiquity/article/aztec-and-tlaxcalan-economic-interaction-blockade-or-interregional-exchange/CBE14EE68216FC6CA5BC5C8F2A1BD3C3).

   There is also a straightforward botanical error: the fibre was maguey/agave, which is not a cactus. **Confidence: high.** Source: [North Carolina State University’s Agave entry](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/agave/).

4. **Misleading omission in the explanation of victory — line 73.**
   > “While the Spaniards rested in Tlaxcala, their most effective ally was already at work in the valley, and they had not sent it.”

   Smallpox also struck the Indigenous allies. Cortés explicitly reports that his important Tlaxcalan supporter Maxixcatzin died of it. Calling disease an ally is defensible, but without this fact readers are likely to understand the epidemic as selectively weakening the Mexica. Add the allies’ losses before explaining the military consequences. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Cortés’s third letter, May 1522](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Letters_of_Cortes_to_Emperor_Charles_V_-_Vol_2/Third_letter,_May_15,_1522).

5. **Unsupported generalisation about political loyalties — line 23.**
   > “The Mexica had perhaps five million subjects, and almost all of them were waiting for a chance to stop paying.”

   Widespread resentment is supportable; the near-universal claim is not. Some subject communities fought Cortés and had to be conquered by his coalition. His own account describes campaigns against Tepeaca and other provinces. The sound claim is that tribute and existing rivalries gave Cortés opportunities to recruit allies. **Confidence: high that “almost all” exceeds the evidence.** Source: [Cortés’s third letter](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Letters_of_Cortes_to_Emperor_Charles_V_-_Vol_2/Third_letter,_May_15,_1522).

6. **Reader loses an essential causal step — line 67.**
   > “When Cortés got back, he found his men besieged in their palace.”

   The force sent to arrest him disappears from the story without resolution. Cortés defeated Narváez and recruited his soldiers before returning. Explain that here: it resolves the arrest threat and helps readers follow the changing Spanish numbers, especially the later claim that more than half died. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Cortés’s account of defeating Narváez, American Historical Association](https://www.historians.org/resource/cortes-defeats-narvaez/).

7. **Factual chronology error — line 75.**
   > “Nobody in the Americas had ever had it.”

   At this point the narrative is in 1520. Smallpox had already reached Hispaniola in late 1518 and spread through other Caribbean islands before reaching Mexico. Specify the previously unexposed population of central Mexico, or explicitly place the statement before European contact. **Confidence: high.** Source: Massimo Livi-Bacci, [“The Depopulation of Hispanic America after the Conquest”](https://labdisia.disia.unifi.it/livi/pubblicazioni/depopulation-of-hispanic-america.pdf).

8. **Factual overstatement; exception-breaking absolute — line 75.**
   > “When it arrived, therefore, everybody caught it together, in every household, the strong adults with the old and the young.”

   Lack of previous exposure made all age groups susceptible; it does not establish universal, simultaneous infection. The contemporary account describes varying severity, including people with relatively few pustules. The defensible point is that illness could disable whole households and leave patients without carers. **Confidence: high.** Source: [translation of the Florentine Codex’s smallpox account](https://rihs.org/images/RIHS_GTW-Immigration_Smallpox.pdf).

9. **Factual error and conflict with earlier context — line 45.**
   > “The second was the horse, which no one in America had seen, and which let a few riders break up a crowd of foot soldiers.”

   Horses had been brought to Hispaniola in 1493, so numerous people in the Americas had seen them before 1519. **Confidence: high.** Source: [research on an early colonial horse from Haiti](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9328532/).

   The unrestricted wording also conflicts with chapter 36’s statement: “When the first people arrived in the Americas, the continents had been home to wild horses, camels and giant ground sloths.” Restrict the claim to the Mexica’s unfamiliarity with mounted warfare.

10. **Doubtful identification of successive epidemics — line 107.**
    > “Measles followed, and then, in 1545 and again in 1576, came a fever that the Mexicans called the great pestilence, which killed more than the smallpox had.”

    This presents both outbreaks as one identified disease and, following “new diseases,” invites readers to assume another imported infection. *Cocoliztli* describes pestilence, not a securely identified pathogen. Salmonella DNA supports a candidate cause at one cemetery associated with the 1545 epidemic; it does not establish one cause for both outbreaks. **Confidence: high that their identity remains uncertain.** Sources: [Vågene and colleagues’ ancient-DNA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29335577/) and [research on the 1576 epidemic](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7110390/).

11. **Unsupported universal claim; style violation — line 37.**
    > “Every family had lost someone to the stone.”

    This requires evidence about every Tlaxcalan family that the chapter does not supply. Warfare and sacrifice caused losses; universal household bereavement is a different claim. This directly breaks the rule against absolutes that one exception defeats. **Confidence: high that the universal wording is unjustified.** The documented warfare and disputed embargo are discussed in [Gentil and colleagues’ study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/latin-american-antiquity/article/aztec-and-tlaxcalan-economic-interaction-blockade-or-interregional-exchange/CBE14EE68216FC6CA5BC5C8F2A1BD3C3).

12. **Reader orientation: city and state blur together — line 7.**
    > “Around 1552, the town council of Tlaxcala, a city in the highlands of central Mexico, paid for a painting.”

    Later, Tlaxcala becomes a state composed of four allied towns, then a modern Mexican state. A general reader needs one short explanation that the name refers to different but related political and urban entities across the story. Otherwise the town council appears to be the same institution as the wartime council of lords. **Confidence: high as a clarity issue.**

13. **Signposting that also prejudges a disputed explanation — line 35.**
    > “This custom explains the strangest feature of the empire.”

    This announces a coming curiosity rather than stating it, contrary to the signpost rule. It also initially endorses the captive-reserve explanation for Tlaxcala’s independence, even though the following paragraphs correctly present competing accounts. Start with Tlaxcala’s survival and let the rival explanations follow. **Confidence: high as a style and logical-sequencing issue.**

14. **Signpost sentence — line 27.**
    > “The Mexica also demanded something else from the world around them, and it shaped the way they made war.”

    “Something else” deliberately withholds the subject to announce the next point. The style guide asks for the idea itself first; name blood, hearts or captives directly. **Confidence: high.**

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or body cross-references. Beyond the horse wording, I found no clear contradiction with chapters 36 and 38–41.
