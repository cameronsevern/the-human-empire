## Factual flags

1. **Major — national ownership confused with operational control.**

   > “The nationalisation had been undone.”

   The 1954 settlement restored foreign operational control, but retained the National Iranian Oil Company’s ownership of the oil lands and assets. Nationalisation was frustrated in practice, not legally reversed in the categorical way this sentence says. The correct distinction is **Iranian ownership with consortium control of production and refining**. [US State Department memorandum, including its account of the 1954 agreement](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v27/d10)

2. **Major — the revision reintroduces the 1945 bargain problem.**

   > “Instead, there was an understanding.”

   In context, this means an oil-for-protection understanding reached aboard *Quincy*. The pre-revision wording had correctly described a relationship that developed afterwards. The addition restores the specific meeting-level bargain flagged in `check.md`.

   The contemporary memorandum records discussions of Palestine, Syria, Lebanon and agriculture; historical examination of the meeting distinguishes its expressions of friendship from the later security relationship. Keep the distinction between **a meeting that strengthened relations** and **an oil-for-security agreement concluded there**. [Contemporary memorandum](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v08/d2), [*Black Gold and Blackmail*, chapter 6](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781501749216-008/pdf)

3. **Major — the new price-setting transition contradicts the preceding history.**

   > “Until then the companies had set the price. Now the producers did.”

   Producers had already forced and negotiated price changes before October 1973—as the chapter’s own Libya passage explains. The February 1971 Tehran agreement established higher posted prices and scheduled further increases. October 1973 marked the Gulf producers’ unilateral setting of prices after negotiations broke down, not their first influence over prices. [Contemporary US intelligence account of the Tehran agreement](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve04/d165)

4. **Major — the worldwide disappearance of spare capacity remains incorrect.**

   > “No country any longer had idle wells that could be turned on in an emergency.”

   This is the unresolved geographical overstatement behind item 20 of the earlier check; the distinction between reserves and spare capacity has been clarified, but “No country” has not been fixed.

   A November 1970 intelligence estimate identified nearly **three million barrels a day of spare Middle Eastern production capacity**, mostly in the Persian Gulf. What had largely disappeared was America’s ability to provide readily available replacement supplies to Europe. [National Intelligence Estimate, paragraph 6](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v36/d61)

5. **Minor — empty roads do not establish absent fuel.**

   > “What was missing was the fuel.”

   The roads were empty because driving was restricted to conserve fuel during the embargo, not because Dutch motorists universally lacked petrol. The Dutch National Archives records exemptions, increased driving on Saturdays and Sunday nights, and continued access to sufficient oil through other countries. The correct immediate explanation is **a government conservation ban prompted by threatened supplies**. [Dutch National Archives](https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/beleven/onderwijs/bronnenbox/autoloze-zondag-in-nederland-1973)

6. **Minor — the airlift frequency is greatly exaggerated.**

   > “The United States flew in replacements, in giant transport planes that landed in Tel Aviv every few minutes.”

   This is unchanged text, but it was not flagged in the earlier check. The planned flow was four C-5 and twelve C-141 arrivals **per day**; the museum’s account gives a peak of eighteen arrivals in a day. The operation completed 567 missions over 32 days. “Every few minutes” implies a substantially larger operation. [Air Mobility Command Museum](https://www.amcmuseum.org/history/operation-nickel-grass/)

## Clarity flags

1. **Repeated wording and an unnecessary absolute.**

   > “For most of history, nobody would have guessed that oil could carry such power. Oil had been a curiosity for most of history…”

   “For most of history” repeats immediately. The first sentence also claims knowledge of what nobody could have anticipated. The second sentence already supplies the historical contrast.

2. **Clear violations of the prohibition on signpost sentences.**

   > “The reason was simple.”
   >
   > “The difficulty was plain.”
   >
   > “That poverty explains the bargain he struck next.”

   These announce an explanation or coming event rather than deliver it. The surrounding sentences already provide the connections.

3. **Padding that repeats the adjoining sentence.**

   > “The contrast was on show every day.”
   >
   > “That money had to go somewhere.”
   >
   > “Their rubbish had become their future.”

   The bungalows and oil-drum housing already show the contrast; the following paragraphs show where the money went; the gasoline passage has already made the waste-to-value reversal. These additions slow the narrative without helping the reader understand it.

4. **The transition supplies the wrong logical connection.**

   > “It was still the companies that kept the books, and the governments had no way to check them. As a result, the owners of the oil could not say how fast it should be pumped or what it should sell for.”

   Access to accounts concerns checking revenues and profits. Authority over production and prices concerns the concession arrangements. “As a result” makes those different mechanisms sound identical.

5. **An ambiguous pronoun introduced by the new definition.**

   > “A deal of this kind was called a concession: a ruler granted a foreigner the right to dig on his land in return for a payment and a share. He paid twenty thousand pounds in cash…”

   The subject has shifted to “a ruler,” so “He paid” can momentarily mean the shah. The payer is the speculator. “His land” also makes national territory sound like personal property.

6. **An unclear return from several countries to one.**

   > “Some of it tied the kingdom to the United States in a different way.”

   The preceding paragraph discusses several countries and their overseas spending. “The kingdom” requires the reader to infer Saudi Arabia before the next sentence identifies it.

7. **The paraphrase expands the claim.**

   > “Put plainly, Britain's treasury did better out of Iran's oil than Iran did.”

   The previous sentence compares British taxes with Iranian royalties. This version sounds like a comparison with all Iranian income and benefits, including wages and domestic economic activity. It is not simply a plainer statement of the same comparison.

8. **Absolutes that exceed the intended point.**

   > “At that price, nobody had much reason to use less.”
   >
   > “Families moved out of the cities to houses with garages, on streets with no shops, from which every errand required a drive.”
   >
   > “Every one of those errands burned petrol.”

   Cheap oil weakened incentives to conserve; it did not remove everyone’s reasons. Car-dependent suburbs made driving routine; they did not make every errand a petrol-burning journey. These are precisely the exception-breaking formulations the style sheet prohibits.

9. **Unclear contrast at the ending.**

   > “One of the men behind the oil weapon drew a different lesson from all this.”

   Different from whose lesson? The preceding passage describes the Kuwait war without stating a competing lesson. Yamani’s warning can follow directly.

There are no chapter self-mentions, explicit chapter cross-references, or hedging stacks.
