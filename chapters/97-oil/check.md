Read the requested files; no files changed. Findings below are ranked by seriousness. I found no clear factual contradiction with chapters 74 or 87; the tension with chapter 86 is identified separately.

1. **Factual error that weakens the central argument — high confidence.**

   > “The first country that tried to change this was the first in which the oil had been found.”

   Iran was not the first country to challenge foreign oil ownership. Mexico nationalised foreign oil companies in 1938 and retained the industry under PEMEX. That successful precedent also undermines the later absolute that no producing country could stand alone against the companies. Restrict the claim to Iran’s place in the Middle Eastern story. [US State Department history](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1937-1945/mexican-oil)

2. **Doubtful account of the 1945 Saudi–American bargain — medium-high confidence.**

   > “No treaty was signed, and both understood the arrangement that held for the rest of the century.”
   >
   > “American companies would have the oil, and the United States would protect the kingdom and its ruling family.”

   This presents a retrospective description of the relationship as an understanding reached at that meeting. The signed memorandum records discussion of Palestine, Syria, Lebanon and agriculture, not an oil-for-protection bargain. American oil concessions already existed. Describe the meeting as strengthening a relationship that subsequently developed along those lines; the participants’ supposed mutual understanding needs specific evidence. [Contemporary memorandum](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v08/d2)

3. **Doubtful agreement claim, plus tension with chapter 86 — medium-high confidence.**

   > “In 1974 the Saudi government agreed with Washington that it would go on pricing its oil in dollars and would invest its surplus in American government debt.”
   >
   > “It now had a link to oil.”

   The economic-cooperation and petrodollar-recycling arrangements are documented; a dollar-pricing commitment needs a separate source. The second sentence also suggests that oil replaced gold as the dollar’s backing. Dollar-denominated trade creates demand for dollars; it does not establish convertibility into oil. Chapter 86 explicitly explains continued dollar use through established international usage. These explanations can coexist, but the present wording blurs fundamentally different mechanisms. [GAO account of the cooperation arrangements](https://www.gao.gov/products/id-79-7), [assessment of the documentary record](https://statecraftencyclopedia.org/entries/petrodollar-recycling-and-us-saudi-financial-arrangements-1974/)

4. **False generalisation about nationalisation — high confidence.**

   > “By 1980 the governments of the Gulf had bought out the concessions, and Saudi Arabia owned the whole of the company that American geologists had founded.”
   >
   > “They no longer owned it.”

   The Saudi example is sound, but it cannot stand for the entire Gulf. Abu Dhabi took a 60 per cent interest in its principal onshore concession, leaving foreign partners with 40 per cent. Foreign participation continued. Distinguish state control from complete elimination of foreign ownership. [Abu Dhabi government prospectus](https://addof.gov.ae/Publications/abudhabigov/Emirate_of_Abu_Dhabi_Base_Prospectus.pdf)

5. **Misleading chronology of American dependence — high confidence.**

   > “American wells were producing flat out, their output passed its peak that year, and the United States had begun to import.”

   America had imported substantial quantities for decades: EIA records net petroleum imports already in 1949. The important change around 1970 was the production peak and disappearance of the American capacity to offset overseas disruptions, not the beginning of imports. [EIA historical petroleum balance](https://www.eia.gov/totalenergy/data/annual/showtext.php?t=ptb0501b)

6. **False universal price claim — high confidence.**

   > “Through all those years a barrel, which is a hundred and fifty-nine litres, cost less than two dollars.”

   This cannot describe oil generally throughout 1950–1973. American crude averaged $2.77 in 1950, and the chapter itself later gives a pre-embargo price approaching $3. A figure below $2 needs a specified grade, location and period—for example, particular Gulf posted prices during much of the 1960s. [EIA historical prices](https://www.eia.gov/totalenergy/data/annual/txt/ptb0518.html)

7. **Incorrect cause-and-effect chronology — high confidence.**

   > “Governments began to build nuclear power stations, to drill under the North Sea and in Alaska, to store oil against the next emergency, and to require cars that used less.”

   Nuclear construction and both drilling programmes predated the embargo. Ekofisk was discovered in 1969 and began producing in 1971; Prudhoe Bay was discovered in 1968. The shock accelerated diversification and conservation, while some measures, including strategic reserves, were new responses. “Began” incorrectly gives them all the same starting point. [Norwegian petroleum history](https://www.norskpetroleum.no/en/utskrift/), [US Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/management/march-13-1968-oil-discovered-alaskas-north-slope)

8. **Misleading identification of fertiliser’s raw material — high confidence.**

   > “Oil also became the raw material of plastics, synthetic cloth, paint, and the fertiliser that fed the world's fast-growing population.”

   Oil-derived feedstocks have been used for nitrogen fertiliser, but this formulation makes oil the general basis of the fertiliser revolution. Ammonia production increasingly used natural gas during the 1960s and 1970s; coal was another important feedstock. Phosphate and potash fertilisers are mined minerals. Distinguish oil’s role in agricultural fuel and transport from the raw materials of fertiliser. [IEA ammonia history and technology roadmap](https://www.fertilizer.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/2021_IEA_Ammonia-Technology-Roadmap.pdf)

9. **Embargo chronology compressed into the wrong day — high confidence.**

   > “The next day came the cuts and the embargo.”

   After the 16 October price decision, production cuts were agreed on 17 October. Embargoes against the United States followed on 19–20 October, with Saudi Arabia joining on the twentieth. The opening similarly folds the cuts and both named countries’ embargoes into one Kuwait decision. Separate the production decision from the subsequent country-specific embargoes. [Federal Reserve history](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/oil-shock-of-1973-74), [diplomatic oral-history account](https://adst.org/2016/01/pain-at-the-pumps-the-1973-oil-embargo-and-its-effect-on-u-s-foreign-policy/)

10. **False claim about what economists believed — high confidence.**

    > “Prices of everything rose, factories laid off workers, and the two happened together, which the economists had said could not occur.”

    Some influential policy models encouraged belief in a dependable inflation–unemployment trade-off. Economists had not collectively declared simultaneous inflation and unemployment impossible: Phelps and Friedman challenged that interpretation before 1973. “Prices of everything” is also an unnecessary absolute. [Federal Reserve account](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2007/200744/)

11. **Wrong date in the coup narrative — high confidence.**

    > “In August 1953 an officer of American intelligence, a grandson of a former president, arrived in Tehran with suitcases of banknotes.”

    Kermit Roosevelt arrived for the operation in July, not August. The coup culminated on 19 August. [Washington Post historical account](https://www.washingtonpost.com/history/2025/06/19/iran-coup-1953-us-role/)

12. **Tax-free citizenship is overstated — high confidence.**

    > “Their citizens paid no taxes and received free schooling, medicine and housing.”

    “No taxes” is false even within the period described. A contemporary IMF account lists Saudi zakat and a road tax on qualifying wages and salaries. The defensible generalisation concerns extensive subsidies and limited reliance on personal income taxation. Universal free housing also needs country-specific qualification. [IMF, *The Economy and Finances of Saudi Arabia*, 1974](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/024/1974/002/article-A001-en.xml)

13. **False absolute about Saudi revenue — high confidence.**

    > “His only income was a tax on pilgrims to Mecca, and the Depression had cut their numbers.”

    Pilgrimage was crucial, but customs duties and zakat also supplied revenue. “Only” is precisely the kind of exception-breaking absolute forbidden by the style guide. [Historical study of Saudi development](https://etheses.dur.ac.uk/1590/1/1590.pdf?EThOS+%28BL%29=)

14. **Norway’s chronology is misleadingly compressed — high confidence.**

    > “Norway, which found oil under the North Sea in 1969 and was already a settled democracy, put its earnings into a fund for future generations that is now worth more than a quarter of a million dollars for each Norwegian.”

    The fund was established in 1990 and received its first transfer in 1996. The sentence makes saving through the fund sound like Norway’s response from the beginning of production. Explain the intervening decades; the historical distinction matters more than the undated present-day valuation. [Norges Bank fund history](https://www.nbim.no/en/about-us/about-the-fund/the-history/)

15. **Incorrect speed-limit scope and timing — high confidence.**

    > “The speed limit on every road in the country was cut to fifty-five miles an hour.”

    The measure imposed a maximum of 55 mph through federal highway-funding conditions; roads with lower limits retained them. Nixon signed the legislation on 2 January 1974, so its placement inside the November scene also misleads. [Federal Highway Administration](https://highways.fhwa.dot.gov/highway-history/interstate-system/50th-anniversary/interstate-frequently-asked-questions)

16. **Posted prices confused with purchase prices — high confidence.**

    > “In 1959 and again in 1960, with oil plentiful, the companies cut the price that they paid, without consulting anyone.”

    The cuts were to **posted prices**, the accounting prices used to calculate payments to producing governments. These were not simply prices paid to buy crude from governments. The earlier explanation nearly establishes this distinction; repeat it plainly here so the reader understands what triggered OPEC’s formation. [OPEC historical account](https://www.opec.org/speech-detail/341-24-march-2005.html)

17. **Overbroad “first” — high confidence.**

    > “It was the first oil found in the Middle East.”

    Oil and bitumen had been known and exploited in the region since antiquity. The 1908 discovery established its first major commercial oilfield, not its first knowledge of oil. This is a factual precision issue and a direct breach of the rule on absolutes. [American Association of Petroleum Geologists](https://www.aapg.org/news-and-media/explorer/the-first-oil-field-in-the-middle-east/)

18. **Unspecified poverty comparison — high confidence that qualification is needed.**

    > “Nigeria and Venezuela earned hundreds of billions from oil and ended with most of their people poorer than before.”

    Before which year, and by what measure? Nigeria’s worsening poverty over particular late-twentieth-century intervals is documented, but that does not establish this combined claim about both countries or an indefinite “before oil” baseline. Use one country and a defined comparison. [NBER discussion of Nigeria’s 1970–1998 poverty figures](https://www.nber.org/digest/oct02/economic-growth-reducing-global-poverty)

19. **Reader orientation: the central organisation is never named.**

    > “That September the oil ministers of Venezuela, Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq and Kuwait met in Baghdad and founded an organisation to defend their interests.”

    Give “Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries—OPEC” here. The next sentence jokes about initials the reader has never been given. Later distinguish this organisation from the Arab producers imposing the embargo; otherwise Iran’s participation in price rises but absence from the embargo seems arbitrary.

20. **Reader orientation: “reserve” conflates two different things.**

    > “Nobody had a reserve any longer.”

    A general reader can take this to mean that underground oil reserves were exhausted. The intended point concerns **spare production capacity**: wells and equipment that could quickly deliver additional oil. Explain that distinction and restrict the claim to the loss of America’s former balancing role. “Nobody” is also broader than the evidence supplied. [Federal Reserve explanation of excess production capacity](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/oil-shock-of-1973-74)

21. **Reader orientation: the physical reason for oil dependence is missing.**

    > “The industry was saved by the engine that burns its fuel inside the cylinder.”
    >
    > “That engine ran best on the liquid that the refiners had been throwing in the river.”

    The reader gets neither a simple explanation of the engine nor why liquid fuel suited vehicles so well. A brief account of burning fuel pushing a piston, and of portable liquid fuel carrying substantial energy, would connect the machinery to the chapter’s geopolitical argument. Without it, the switch from lamp waste to indispensable transport fuel feels asserted rather than explained.

22. **Style: an absolute replaces the resource-curse mechanism.**

    > “When the price falls, there is nothing else.”

    “Nothing” is broken by any surviving farm, factory or service industry. The substantive claim is that oil dependence leaves too few alternative sources of government revenue and export earnings. State that directly; it preserves the argument without an indefensible absolute.

23. **Doubtful superlative — medium confidence; unsupported as written.**

    > “It was the largest peaceful transfer of wealth that had ever taken place.”

    The following revenue figures do not establish this historical ranking. “Transfer of wealth” also needs a definition: gross export receipts, additional receipts caused by higher prices, or accumulated assets? A large increase in annual income cannot by itself support “largest ever.” Remove the ranking unless there is a source and a comparable measure.

I found no clear hedging stacks, prohibited signpost sentences, chapter self-mentions or explicit cross-references in chapter 97. The main style breaches are the absolutes identified above.
