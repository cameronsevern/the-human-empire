# Chapter 55 — Why did the English cut off their king's head?

**Line under the title:** England, Scotland and Ireland, from 1625 to 1694.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Charles I was executed because he fought a war against his own Parliament over who had the last word, lost it, and then started it again. The army that had beaten him decided that there could be no settlement while he lived. The quarrel was about money and religion: the king needed taxes, only Parliament could grant them, and he tried to rule without it. The republic that followed turned into rule by the army and collapsed. The lasting change came forty years later, when Parliament removed a second king without a war and gave the crown to his daughter and her Dutch husband on written conditions. After that, the king of England could not tax, keep an army or suspend a law without Parliament's consent. One result was that Britain's government became the most trusted borrower in Europe, which is how a small island paid for the wars that made it a world power.

## The story

1. **Opening: two shirts.** Answer first. A cold morning in January 1649, a scaffold outside a palace window.
2. **Called gods.** What kings claimed and what Parliament was for. The king's one weakness: he had to ask for money.
3. **Twenty shillings.** Eleven years without Parliament. A tax on ships charged far from the sea. A prayer book that started a war with Scotland. The king has to ask after all.
4. **The birds have flown.** January 1642: the king walks into the House of Commons with soldiers. The war begins.
5. **Plain captains.** The war and what it cost. An army promoted by merit, full of men who read and argued.
6. **The poorest he.** A church near London, 1647: soldiers debate who should have a vote.
7. **A man of blood.** The king starts a second war. The purge of Parliament, the trial, the sentence.
8. **No Christmas.** The republic: Ireland, rule by generals, the end of the experiment, a head on a pole.
9. **An invitation.** 1688: a Catholic king, a baby, a Dutch fleet. The crown offered on conditions.
10. **Three per cent.** What the conditions did: yearly Parliaments, a bank, cheap borrowing. Two philosophers who drew opposite lessons. Ending on a door slammed once a year.
