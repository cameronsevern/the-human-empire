1. **Doubtful central argument — high confidence that qualification is needed.** “France had three times Britain's population, but its kings, who answered to nobody, borrowed at twice the price.” ([Line 123](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:123))  
   This makes a variable comparison sound like a permanent consequence of absolutism. British borrowing costs remained high and volatile for roughly four decades after 1688; parliamentary government did not immediately or consistently confer cheaper credit than less constrained governments enjoyed. Specify the period and comparable loans, and distinguish Parliament’s contribution from taxation, financial institutions and wartime conditions. [Sussman and Yafeh’s study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/abs/institutional-reforms-financial-development-and-sovereign-debt-britain-16901790/07B3157C3D86DECEF4B20EC24B9B11E5) directly challenges the simple causal account.

2. **Wrong sequence and missing event — high confidence.** “In 1640, after eleven years, he had to call Parliament.” ([Line 39](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:39))  
   The surrounding paragraphs put the Scottish invasion first and identify the recalled Parliament as November’s. Charles actually summoned the **Short Parliament in April 1640**, dissolved it in May, suffered the Scottish invasion in August, and summoned the **Long Parliament in November**. Omitting April reverses part of the causal sequence and conceals another failed negotiation. [UK Parliament’s chronology](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/civilwar/key-dates/1603-1640/); [English Heritage’s account](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/histories/the-english-civil-wars-history-and-stories/the-english-civil-wars/).

3. **Wrong chronology — high confidence.** “The officers held a prayer meeting and resolved to call ‘that man of blood’ to account.” ([Line 79](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:79))  
   Placed after Cromwell’s defeat of the Scottish invasion, this dates the decision too late. The Windsor prayer meetings occurred in **late April 1648**, before that summer’s invasion and August’s victory at Preston. The army’s commitment developed before its final victory in the second war. [Cromwell Association historical analysis](https://www.olivercromwell.org/wordpress/articles/king-killing-no-murder-cromwell-in-1648/).

4. **Misleading scope — high confidence.** “Hardly a shot was fired.” ([Line 107](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:107))  
   Defensible as shorthand for the relatively slight military resistance during William’s English invasion, but misleading in a chapter expressly covering England, Scotland and Ireland through 1694. The settlement brought fighting in Scotland and a major Irish war in 1689–91. Add the geographical limit and acknowledge those consequences. [Parliament’s account explicitly distinguishes the three kingdoms](https://hansard.parliament.uk/%E2%80%8CCommons/1988-07-07/debates/df55418a-5981-4207-ac3a-130a3086a37b/RevolutionsOf1688%E2%80%9389%28Tercentenary%29).

5. **Misleading account of army politicisation — high confidence.** “The New Model Army was paid regularly, drilled hard and promoted on merit.” ([Line 61](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:61))  
   Regular pay was an intention, not a dependable experience. By 1647 infantry pay was eighteen weeks in arrears and cavalry pay forty-two weeks behind. Those grievances help explain why the army became a political force; the present transition substitutes Bible-reading for an important material cause. [Royal Army Pay Corps Association’s history](https://rapc-association.org.uk/pay-services-history/notes-on-army-pay-2.html).

6. **Misleading implication — high confidence.** “However, nobody had forgotten what had happened to a king who tried to rule alone, least of all the king.” ([Line 99](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:99))  
   Alongside “Charles II was careful,” this implies that his father’s fate taught Charles II to retain Parliament. He actually ruled **without Parliament from 1681 until his death in 1685**. His success in doing so matters to the chapter’s argument: the Restoration had not yet made parliamentary dependence unavoidable. [Grant Tapsell, *The Personal Rule of Charles II, 1681–85*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/personal-rule-of-charles-ii-168185/political-partisanship-and-government-without-parliament/A179522F2283A96A5C9E1B175794A4DD).

7. **Wrong composition date — high confidence.** “John Locke, writing just after the Dutch invasion, argued that governments exist to protect the lives, liberty and property of the people who set them up, and that a ruler who breaks that trust may be removed.” ([Line 119](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:119))  
   Locke **published** the *Two Treatises* in 1689, but substantially composed them earlier, during the Exclusion Crisis. The post-revolutionary preface helped create the misleading impression that the argument had been written in response to 1688. Change the distinction between writing and publication. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke/?level=1).

8. **Wrong numerical claim — high confidence.** “Of the hundred and thirty-five men named as his judges, fewer than half would attend, and fifty-nine signed the warrant for his death.” ([Line 83](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:83))  
   **Sixty-eight** commissioners attended the sentencing session: slightly **more** than half of 135. The fifty-nine signatures are a different count. [Michael Kirby’s account of the trial, hosted by the High Court of Australia](https://www.hcourt.gov.au/sites/default/files/assets/publications/speeches/former-justices/kirbyj/kirbyj_charle88.pdf).

9. **Overgeneralisation — high confidence.** “The new king had the surviving judges hunted down and executed.” ([Line 95](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:95))  
   This reads as the fate of all surviving judges. Some were executed, others imprisoned, and others escaped abroad. Ten people were executed in the initial 1660 proceedings, including participants who had not been judges; three further regicides were executed in 1662. [BCW Project’s account and individual outcomes](https://bcw-project.org.uk/biography/regicides-index); [Cambridge history of the trials](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/state-trials-and-the-politics-of-justice-in-later-stuart-england/blood-will-have-blood-the-regicide-trials-and-the-popular-press/5D1B9C61BACC1517A85546B53AF24C0E).

10. **Misassigned death estimate — high confidence about the scope problem.** “About one person in twenty-five in England died of it, from wounds or from the diseases that followed the armies, which is a greater share than the country lost in the First World War.” ([Line 55](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:55))  
    The paragraph begins with the four-year first war, making “it” appear to mean 1642–46. Estimates around four per cent generally concern the **civil wars collectively**, through 1651, and are estimates rather than firm counts. State the longer period. [English Heritage’s overview and casualty estimate](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/histories/the-english-civil-wars-history-and-stories/the-english-civil-wars/).

11. **Unsupported two-century jump; also an exception-breaking absolute — high confidence.** “The generals closed the meeting and nothing came of the proposal for two hundred years.” ([Line 73](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:73))  
    Revised *Agreements of the People* followed immediately: one reached Parliament in January 1649 and another appeared in May. The proposal was not adopted, but “nothing came” erases its continued political life. Nor is there an identified event around 1847 that fulfils the promise. Say that the generals prevented its implementation. [BCW Project’s chronology of the Agreements](https://bcw-project.org.uk/church-and-state/second-civil-war/agreement-of-the-people).

12. **Overstatement and internal explanatory gap — high confidence.** “By a custom four hundred years old, he could not impose a tax without Parliament's agreement.” ([Line 23](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:23))  
    Parliamentary consent to taxation was an established principle, but the boundary between taxes requiring consent and revenues claimed under royal prerogative was contested. Otherwise readers cannot understand why seven judges subsequently uphold ship money. Explain that Charles claimed these demands fell within existing royal rights, while opponents regarded them as unauthorised taxation. [Parliament on the Petition of Right](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/civilwar/overview/petition-of-right/); [Oxford study of the ship-money case](https://academic.oup.com/manchester-scholarship-online/book/42414/chapter-abstract/356648896).

13. **Reader loses the reason for Scotland’s reversal.** “At the end of 1647 he made a secret agreement with the Scots, who invaded England on his behalf the following summer.” ([Line 79](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:79))  
    Scotland last appeared fighting Charles over religion. Readers need the bargain that changed the alignment: Charles promised a trial of Presbyterian church government in England in return for Scottish military support. Also distinguish his Scottish supporters from “the Scots” collectively; Scotland was divided. The agreement’s religious terms supply the missing causal link. [English Heritage’s narrative](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/histories/the-english-civil-wars-history-and-stories/the-english-civil-wars/). **Confidence: high.**

14. **Reader lacks a decisive fact about William.** “It was addressed to William of Orange, the Dutch head of state, who was married to James's elder daughter Mary.” ([Line 105](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:105))  
    State explicitly that **William was Protestant**. After making Catholic succession the trigger, the chapter leaves readers to infer why this foreign ruler solves the problem. Chapter 45’s religious history does not establish William’s personal religion. [Parliament’s account of James II’s reign](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/revolution/overview/reignofjames/). **Confidence: high.**

15. **Continuity problem with chapter 36, rather than a direct contradiction.** “The king was under the law, and the last word belonged to an assembly that he could not do without.” ([Line 117](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:117))  
    Chapter 36 already says: “The king was bound by the law like anyone else, and there were things he could not do to his subjects without their consent.” Explain what changed from an old principle to a stronger practical constraint: recurring parliamentary control of revenue and the army. Otherwise the reader encounters the same constitutional breakthrough twice. I found no clear direct contradiction with chapters 45 or 54.

16. **Minor factual error — high confidence.** “Within three weeks, seven leading noblemen and bishops sent a letter to Holland.” ([Line 105](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:105))  
    The seven included **one bishop, four peers and two commoners**, Edward Russell and Henry Sidney. “Seven leading opponents of James” would cover the actual group without adding names. [The National Archives’ identification of the signatories](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/glorious-revolution/glorious-revolution-source-1/).

17. **Style: explicit signposting.** “To see how England arrived at that scaffold, it helps to know what kings in the 1600s believed about themselves.” ([Line 17](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:17))  
    This announces the explanatory programme instead of giving the explanation, contrary to “No signpost sentences.” The following paragraph already supplies the substantive opening. I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or body cross-references.

18. **Style and factual precision: an unnecessary absolute — high confidence.** “Each year, when the monarch comes to open Parliament, a royal messenger walks to the House of Commons to summon the members.” ([Line 125](chapters/55-parliament/chapter.md:125))  
    State Opening marks a **new parliamentary session**, which does not invariably coincide with each calendar year. “At the State Opening of Parliament” preserves the ending without the exception-breaking claim. [House of Commons Library’s account of the ceremony](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-9501/).
