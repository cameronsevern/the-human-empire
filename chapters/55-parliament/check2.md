## Factual flags

1. **Major — the Scottish chronology remains wrong, and the new transition reinforces it.**

   > “The Scots raised an army, and it marched into England.”  
   > “Sending Parliament home did not send the Scots home, however.”

   The chapter still places the invasion before the April–May 1640 Short Parliament. Adding that Parliament fixed its omission, but not the sequence. Charles fought the first Bishops’ War in 1639, summoned the Short Parliament in April 1640 to finance renewed war, dissolved it in May, and faced the Scottish invasion in August. The new “did not send the Scots home” sentence specifically implies that they were already occupying England in May. [UK Parliament’s account of the Personal Rule](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/civilwar/overview/personal-rule/) gives the correct sequence.

2. **Major — Parliament was necessary for sustainable funding, not for the existence of an army.**

   > “Only a new tax could pay for one, and only Parliament could grant a new tax.”

   Charles had already raised and maintained an army for the 1639 campaign without summoning Parliament. He also resumed military preparations after dissolving the Short Parliament without obtaining its supply. His problem was inadequate resources for continued warfare, not the impossibility of financing any army without a new parliamentary tax. [UK Parliament’s history](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/civilwar/overview/personal-rule/) describes both campaigns and the badly paid English army.

3. **Minor — the ship-money changes did not happen “at once.”**

   > “Charles stretched it in three directions at once.”

   The peacetime levy began with coastal communities in **1634**; its extension to inland counties followed in **1635**, with repeated levies establishing its annual character. The preceding version listed the changes without asserting simultaneity. [Pauline Gregg’s *King Charles I*](https://pub-ucpec2-prd.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?anchor.id=0&brand=eschol&chunk.id=d0e2510&doc.view=content&docId=ft9v19p2p6&toc.depth=1) dates the first writ to October 1634; [UK Parliament’s chronology](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/civilwar/key-dates/1603-1640/) dates the inland extension to 1635.

4. **Major — possessing soldiers and tax collectors did not eliminate the Spanish crown’s need for consent.**

   > “A king with his own soldiers and his own tax collectors had no need to ask anyone's leave, and an assembly that is never asked soon stops meeting.”

   Applied to the preceding France-and-Spain comparison, this makes a false institutional claim. Castilian taxation still involved negotiated consent. Even after the Cortes stopped meeting in 1665, bargaining over tax grants continued through the represented cities. The disappearance of meetings did not mean the disappearance of consent. [José Manuel de Bernardo Ares’s study of Castile, 1665–1700](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02606755.2005.9522205) examines precisely this arrangement.

5. **Minor — Charles and Cromwell were not trying to do the same thing in the Commons.**

   > “In 1653 Cromwell lost patience with what remained of Parliament, marched in with a squad of musketeers, and cleared the chamber, just as the king had tried to do. The difference was that Cromwell succeeded.”

   Charles’s January 1642 objective was to arrest five named MPs, not clear the chamber or dissolve Parliament by force. Cromwell expelled the sitting Rump in April 1653. The old comparison was already inaccurate; the new sentence makes the mistaken equivalence explicit. It also conflicts with the chapter’s own earlier description of Charles’s objective. See [the Commons Journal recording Charles’s intervention](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/civilwar/collections/commons-journal/) and [Parliament’s account of Cromwell’s dissolution](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/civilwar/overview/rump-dissolved/).

6. **Major — a new sentence partly undoes the earlier correction to the borrowing argument.**

   > “A loan to the government therefore became a safe loan, and a safe loan is a cheap one.”

   Immediately following “After 1689,” this presents safety and cheap credit as an achieved consequence of the settlement. The following paragraph still says the change took time and gives high 1690s rates, creating an internal mismatch. British borrowing costs remained high and volatile for decades; parliamentary backing did not make all government loans safe or cheap. The chapter can retain its interpretation about Parliament’s long-term contribution while preserving the delayed outcome it already acknowledges. [Sussman and Yafeh’s research](https://bschool-en.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/businesshe/files/institutional_reform_financial_development_and_sovereign_debt_jeh_2006.pdf) documents the timing. This flag concerns the **new sentence**, not the already-corrected comparison with France.

## Clarity flags

- **An absolute larger than the argument needs:**  
  > “That idea ran against everything that kings then believed about themselves.”  
  “Everything” extends a specific conflict over accountability to every royal belief. The following paragraph already identifies the relevant doctrine precisely.

- **Unnecessary universal wording:**  
  > “It asked for its grievances to be heard first, as the Commons did whenever a king needed money, and he dismissed it within a month.”  
  “Whenever” converts a recurring bargaining practice into an exceptionless rule. The chapter has already explained the practice.

- **Repeated explanation:**  
  > “They could picture a king who was forced to bargain. They could not picture a king who had been beaten outright, and they fought accordingly.”  
  These repeat the preceding sentence about generals shrinking from the consequences of victory. They add emphasis but little understanding.

- **A transition contradicts the nearby narrative:**  
  > “That was the theory.”  
  Its immediate antecedents are the republic and the abolition of monarchy and Lords, which actually happened. Presumably it refers to popular sovereignty; that reference is too remote and ambiguous.

- **“Next” creates a misleading sequence:**  
  > “Parliament itself was the next to feel it.”  
  Parliament has already experienced the army’s purge in December 1648. This makes the 1653 expulsion sound like its first encounter with military coercion.

- **Signposting without content:**  
  > “The order of events made the point.”  
  > “The list then goes further.”  
  Both announce the explanatory work that the next sentence performs. They fall under the style sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences.

- **Chronological backtracking becomes harder to follow:**  
  > “The new order reached into ordinary pleasures as well.”  
  This follows the Protectorate and major-generals, but introduces theatre and Christmas prohibitions dating from before the Protectorate. “Already” helps, but the new transition encourages the wrong association.

- **An unresolved grammatical reference:**  
  > “His brother James, who followed him in 1685, was not.”  
  The preceding sentence describes Charles II as easy-going, avoiding a showdown, and managing without Parliament. “Was not” should clearly attach to the first characteristic.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions, or explicit body cross-references.

## Overall

The revision generally improves the explanatory links, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. Its main risks are new absolutes, the reinforced Scottish chronology error, and the renewed implication that parliamentary government immediately produced cheap credit.
