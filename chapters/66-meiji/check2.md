## Changed facts

No existing date, name, quotation or numerical value was removed or changed. However, the revision introduces factual assertions as well as transitions. Below, **“none” means an added sentence with no old counterpart**. Purely rhetorical additions and restatements are excluded.

1. **Definition of shogun added.**  
   **Old:** “For more than two hundred years the shoguns had kept the Western world at arm's length.”  
   **New:** “For more than two hundred years the shoguns, the generals who ruled Japan, had kept the Western world at arm's length.”  
   This is an accurate explanatory addition.

2. **A specific military capability added.**  
   **Old:** None.  
   **New:** “A fleet in the bay could therefore starve Edo and shell it as well.”  
   The old text described dependence on coastal shipping and exposure to naval guns; the new sentence expressly asserts the feasibility of blockade and bombardment.

3. **Legal immunity broadened.**  
   **Old:** “Ports were opened to foreign merchants, who could be tried only by their own consuls.”  
   **New:** “First, ports were opened to foreign merchants, who could be tried only by their own consuls.” Followed by: “A foreigner in Japan therefore stood outside Japanese law.”  
   The addition turns a statement about jurisdiction into a broader claim about applicable law. See factual flag 1.

4. **The size and age of the travelling government restated more broadly.**  
   **Old:** “In December 1871, with the country barely pacified, about fifty officials, among them half the leading men of the government, boarded an American steamer and left Japan for nearly two years.”  
   **New:** That sentence remains, followed by: “A government less than four years old had sent half of itself to the far side of the world to learn how the job was done.”  
   “Less than four years” is a correct calculation. “Half of itself” loses the preceding qualification that this means leading men.

5. **A purpose assigned to foreign appointments.**  
   **Old:** “Each was given Japanese assistants to train, and when the assistants were ready, the foreigner's contract was not renewed.”  
   **New:** That sentence remains, preceded by: “The foreigners were hired to make themselves unnecessary.”  
   This makes the existing account of training and replacement an explicit statement of government intention.

6. **Hereditary status made explicit.**  
   **Old:** “The old ranking of samurai, farmers, craftsmen and merchants was dismantled in the same years.”  
   **New:** “The old ranking of samurai, farmers, craftsmen and merchants, which had fixed each family's place by birth, was dismantled in the same years.”  
   The inserted clause adds a categorical description of how status worked.

7. **The tax’s consequences elaborated.**  
   **Old:** “It gave the state an income that it could count on in advance, and it weighed heavily on the peasants.”  
   **New:** That sentence remains, followed by: “Both effects came from the same rule. Because the sum was fixed in money, the government knew what it would receive whether the rice grew or not. For the same reason, a farmer owed the full amount even in a year when his crop failed.”  
   This explains an existing claim rather than changing the tax rate or basis.

8. **Universal family liability added.**  
   **Old:** “Every man of twenty was liable for three years' service in a new national army, armed with rifles and drilled in the European manner.”  
   **New:** That sentence remains, followed by: “Defending the country had become a duty that fell on every family.”  
   The addition reinforces an inaccurate universality. See factual flag 2.

9. **A new elapsed-time figure added.**  
   **Old:** None.  
   **New:** “Eighteen years after the toy on the beach, Japan had the real thing.”  
   The calculation, 1854–1872, is correct.

10. **The samurai’s losses expanded into a comprehensive summary.**  
    **Old:** “Now the lords were gone, any farmer's son could be a soldier, and in 1876 the government swapped the salaries for government bonds, whose yearly interest came to far less than the old salary, and forbade the wearing of swords in public.”  
    **New:** That sentence remains, followed by: “Within a few years the samurai had lost their lords, their monopoly of arms, most of their income and the badge of their rank.”  
    “Monopoly of arms” is a broader claim than the preceding account of soldiering and sword wearing; see factual flag 3. “Most of their income” also broadens a statement specifically about stipend income.

11. **Prior intention to privatise asserted.**  
    **Old:** “When the factories were running, most of them were sold off cheaply to a few merchant families with good connections, whose firms grew into the giant business groups that still dominate the Japanese economy.”  
    **New:** That sentence remains, preceded by: “The state did not mean to run these works for ever.”  
    Eventual sale has become an assertion about prior intention.

12. **A distributional consequence of avoiding foreign loans added.**  
    **Old:** “Japan borrowed very little abroad, because its leaders had seen what foreign creditors did to countries that could not repay.”  
    **New:** “It borrowed very little abroad, because its leaders had seen what foreign creditors did to countries that could not repay.” Followed by: “The cost of that caution fell at home, on the mill girls and the farmers.”  
    This explicitly connects the borrowing policy to the burdens previously described.

13. **Constitutional continuity asserted.**  
    **Old:** “It was modelled on the German one.”  
    **New:** “It was modelled on the German one, and it had the look of a Western constitution while leaving power much where it was.”  
    This adds an interpretation of the constitution’s practical effect, not a changed date or institutional detail.

14. **A general rule about officials’ mobility added.**  
    **Old:** “It was run by a class of educated officials who lived on salaries and owned no land, and so could be reassigned.”  
    **New:** “It was also run by a class of educated officials who lived on salaries and owned no land, and so could be reassigned.” Followed by: “A man who lives on a salary goes where he is sent, whereas a man with an estate stays beside it.”  
    The addition turns an institutional explanation into an exceptionless rule.

15. **A specific explanation of treaty revision added.**  
    **Old:** “The Western powers had just agreed to give up their special courts in Japan, and did so in 1899.”  
    **New:** That sentence remains, preceded by: “The condition laid down in Washington a generation earlier, laws and courts that Westerners could respect, was judged to have been met.”  
    This newly presents treaty revision as a judgment that the earlier condition had been satisfied.

16. **Russia’s classification made explicit.**  
    **Old:** “The next rival was Russia, which was pushing its railway and its soldiers into the north of China and towards Korea.”  
    **New:** “The next rival was of a different order. It was Russia, a European great power, which was pushing its railway and its soldiers into the north of China and towards Korea.”  
    The added classification is accurate.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — consular jurisdiction is not exemption from all Japanese law.**

   > “A foreigner in Japan therefore stood outside Japanese law.”

   The inference is too broad. Treaty-protected foreigners were generally outside Japanese **court jurisdiction**, but Japanese regulations could still apply to them. Nor did every foreign nationality automatically enjoy the same treaty protection. An American diplomatic memorandum of 1875 expressly recognised Japan’s power to enact generally applicable laws affecting resident Americans, subject to treaty restrictions, while distinguishing enforcement and punishment through American consular courts. The correct fact is exemption from Japanese jurisdiction under the relevant treaties, not blanket immunity from Japanese law. [US diplomatic correspondence, 1875](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1875v02/d61)

2. **Major — the original conscription system had substantial exemptions.**

   > “Every man of twenty was liable for three years' service in a new national army, armed with rifles and drilled in the European manner.”  
   > “Defending the country had become a duty that fell on every family.”

   Universal service was the stated principle, but the 1873 ordinance exempted substantial categories from active service, including household heads, government employees, students at government schools and men who paid a substitution fee. The new sentence makes the existing overstatement more emphatic. The correct account is that conscription crossed hereditary class boundaries but initially operated with extensive exemptions. [National Archives of Japan: the 1873 conscription ordinance](https://www.archives.go.jp/ayumi/kobetsu/m06_1873_01.html)

3. **Minor — samurai did not exclusively carry all swords.**

   > “They alone had carried swords, and they had lived on salaries of rice paid by their lords.”

   Commoners could carry certain short swords: the Pitt Rivers Museum identifies the wakizashi as a sword carried by merchants and townsmen during the Edo period. The distinctive samurai privilege concerned wearing the paired long and short swords, not exclusive possession or carrying of every sword. The new phrase “their monopoly of arms” widens the same error. [Pitt Rivers Museum: wakizashi](https://web.prm.ox.ac.uk/weapons/index.php/tour-by-region/asia/asia/arms-and-armour-asia-115/)

4. **Minor — the emperor’s ceremonial train was not the first train.**

   > “The emperor rode on the first train.”

   Regular passenger services between Shinagawa and Yokohama began on **12 June 1872**. The emperor travelled at the formal opening of the completed Shimbashi–Yokohama line on **14 October**. “The inaugural ceremonial train” would identify the correct event without claiming the first operation. [Eiichi Aoki, “Dawn of Japanese Railways,” Japan Railway & Transport Review](https://www.ejrcf.or.jp/jrtr/jrtr01/history.html)

5. **Minor — Washington was not the embassy’s first stop.**

   > “At its first stop, in Washington, the embassy learned that nothing would be changed until Japan had laws and courts that Westerners could respect.”

   The embassy arrived in San Francisco on **15 January 1872**, visited institutions there, and reached Washington on **29 February**. Washington was the location of its treaty negotiations, not its first stop. This is a separate geographical error from the earlier check’s corrected claim about negotiations “everywhere.” [Japan Center for Asian Historical Records: mission itinerary](https://www.jacar.go.jp/exhibition/iwakura/history/)

## Clarity flags

1. **An explicit signpost.**

   > “The high pay bought something specific, however.”

   This announces an explanation without giving it—the exact kind of sentence the style sheet prohibits. The following sentence supplies the point.

2. **Another withheld subject.**

   > “There was a third possible source, and Japan mostly refused it.”

   Readers must wait for the next sentence to discover that the source is foreign borrowing. Naming it here would preserve the transition without announcing an answer.

3. **Repeated explanation of the shogun’s failure.**

   > “The one thing his title promised was the thing he had failed to do.”

   The immediately preceding sentences have already translated the title and said that he confronted foreigners and gave in. This adds emphasis but no explanatory step.

4. **Repeated summary of the samurai’s losses.**

   > “Each of those privileges was taken away in turn.”  
   > “Within a few years the samurai had lost their lords, their monopoly of arms, most of their income and the badge of their rank.”

   These bracket a sentence that already lists the losses. The paragraph introduces, describes and then summarises the same point.

5. **An absolute that obscures the useful explanation.**

   > “A man who lives on a salary goes where he is sent, whereas a man with an estate stays beside it.”

   Salaried employment does not guarantee obedience or mobility, and landowners need not remain physically beside their estates. The relevant distinction is ease of reassignment, already expressed in the preceding sentence.

6. **A confusing either/or.**

   > “The second depended on the first, because rifles, warships and railways must be either made at home or paid for.”

   Domestic manufacture also costs money. The intended distinction appears to be domestic production versus purchase from abroad.

7. **Awkward wording.**

   > “Their leader was no enemy of the new order by origin.”

   “By origin” could mean birth, class or earlier political allegiance. The following sentence already explains that he helped overthrow the shogun.

8. **Ambiguous map caption.**

   > “Japan and its neighbours about 1905: the home islands and Taiwan, Japanese from 1895; …”

   Grammatically, “Japanese from 1895” can modify both the home islands and Taiwan. It needs to attach unmistakably to Taiwan.

9. **An argument announced as a comparison.**

   > “That made the revolution far easier to carry through, as the comparison with China shows.”

   The causal interpretation is legitimate within the book’s chosen voice. The trailing phrase announces the coming comparison rather than narrating it; the next sentence already begins that comparison.

## Overall

The revision preserves the existing chronology and figures, but it is not entirely fact-neutral: several bridges add claims about legal scope, universality and intention. The clearest new problems are blanket exemption from Japanese law and the assertion that conscription reached every family. Other additions mainly need trimming or more precise wording.
