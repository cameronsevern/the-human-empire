1. **Wrong cause — high confidence.** “Their leader was the most admired man in Japan, a huge, plain-living general who had commanded the army that overthrew the shogun, and who had left the government in disgust at what his colleagues were doing to his class.”

   Saigō Takamori resigned in 1873 over the government’s rejection of his proposed mission to Korea, in a dispute over confrontation with Korea. Samurai grievances matter to the subsequent rebellion, but this sentence substitutes them for the immediate cause of his resignation. [Smithsonian](https://www.si.edu/object/fsg_S1999.62)

2. **False first; also breaks the rule against vulnerable absolutes — high confidence.** “In 1889 the emperor presented his people with a constitution, the first in Asia.”

   The Ottoman Empire promulgated its constitution in 1876. “Japan’s first modern constitution” would preserve the relevant point without the false continental claim. [Constitute’s constitutional history of Asia](https://www.constituteproject.org/countries/Asia)

3. **Overbroad historical claim — high confidence that qualification is needed.** “No Asian country had beaten a European great power in a modern war before.”

   Afghanistan had defeated Britain’s attempt to impose a dependent ruler in the First Anglo-Afghan War, ending in British withdrawal in 1842. “Modern war” does not explain why that conflict is excluded. Japan’s distinction was defeating a European great power with a modern industrial army and navy; the present wording makes a much broader claim. [National Army Museum](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/first-afghan-war)

4. **Wrong duration and misleading chronology — high confidence.** “Finally, the foreign powers were busy elsewhere, in China above all, and for a crucial decade the United States was fighting its own civil war.”

   The American Civil War lasted four years, 1861–1865, and ended before the Restoration. It can explain reduced American attention during part of the preceding crisis, not a decade of distraction during Meiji reconstruction. [US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/civilwar/facts.htm)

5. **Wrong educational chronology — high confidence.** “Four years of schooling were made compulsory for boys and girls.”

   Attached to the preceding 1872 law, this is wrong. The 1872 scheme envisaged eight years of elementary education, with weak enforcement; the four-year compulsory course was established in 1886. [Japanese education ministry](https://www.mext.go.jp/b_menu/hakusho/html/others/detail/1317317.htm)

6. **Doubtful statistic and inconsistency with chapter 49 — high confidence about the inconsistency; moderate confidence about any replacement percentage.** “Japan already had thousands of small private schools and a population half of which could read, so the buildings and the habit were there to build on.”

   Chapter 49 says “nearly half of Japanese **men** were literate,” not half the population. Chapter 66 silently extends that estimate to women and children. Historical literacy estimates vary substantially, and schooling rates are often mistaken for literacy rates. The secure point is the extensive existing school network; the population-wide percentage needs a specific supporting study. [Chapter 49](chapters/49-japan/chapter.md:130); [J. Marshall Unger’s discussion of the estimates](https://u.osu.edu/unger.26/online-publications/chapter-3-of-the-fifth-generation-fallacy/)

7. **Misleading sequence of foreign models — high confidence.** “They took what they judged to be the best model for each purpose: the navy from Britain, the army from Germany, which had just beaten France, the police from France, and the schools from America.”

   The early Meiji army continued to rely heavily on French advisers, including a mission beginning in 1872, after France’s defeat. German influence became decisive later, particularly with Meckel’s arrival in 1885. Schooling also combined American educational influences with French administrative organization. The sentence makes a gradual, mixed process sound like one immediate allocation. [Study of the French military missions](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rharm_0035-3299_2001_num_224_3_5034?pageId=T1_57); [JICA’s educational history](https://www.jica.go.jp/Resource/jica-ri/IFIC_and_JBICI-Studies/english/publications/reports/study/topical/educational/pdf/educational_01.pdf)

8. **Misleading treaty chronology — high confidence.** “Soon afterwards the Western powers agreed to give up their special courts in Japan, and in 1902 Britain, the greatest naval power in the world, signed an alliance with it as an equal.”

   Following the victory over China, this suggests that victory secured the agreement. Britain signed the breakthrough treaty on **16 July 1894**, before the war began; its provisions abolishing consular jurisdiction took effect in **1899**. Distinguish the agreement from its implementation. [National Archives of Japan](https://www.archives.go.jp/ayumi/kobetsu/m27_1894_01.html)

9. **Overcompressed social reform — high confidence.** “The four hereditary classes of samurai, farmers, craftsmen and merchants were abolished in the same year.”

   The old hierarchy was dismantled through several measures, not replaced by a classless legal order in 1871. Farmers, craftsmen and merchants were grouped as commoners, while hereditary noble and former-samurai categories remained. This also leaves readers wondering why samurai privileges still exist in the subsequent 1876 passage. [Japanese parliamentary historical chronology](https://dl.ndl.go.jp/view/download/digidepo_11343084_po_shukenshi027.pdf?alternativeNo=&contentNo=1)

10. **Wrong generalization and contradiction with chapter 49 — high confidence.** “They allowed one Dutch ship a year into one harbour and required its captain to report the news.”

    In the context of the preceding “two hundred and fifty years,” this incorrectly makes a temporary restriction the standing arrangement. The permitted number changed; the one-ship restriction dates from 1790. Chapter 49 correctly describes “a few ships” arriving annually in its earlier period. [Dejima’s official history](https://nagasakidejima.jp/english/history/); [chapter 49](chapters/49-japan/chapter.md:117)

11. **Wrong electoral denominator — high confidence.** “There was an elected parliament, chosen by the one man in a hundred who paid enough tax to vote, and ministers who answered to the emperor alone.”

    The electorate was about **1.1% of the total population**, not 1% of men. Only the lower house was elected; the House of Peers was not. The accurate concise formulation is that the elected lower house represented a tax-qualified male electorate amounting to roughly one person in a hundred. [National Diet Library](https://www.ndl.go.jp/modern/e/cha2/description11.html)

12. **Exaggerated scope and internal contradiction — high confidence.** “Fifty-one years later, a Japanese fleet built on those lessons destroyed the navy of the Russian empire in an afternoon.”

    Tsushima destroyed the Russian fleet sent to East Asia, not Russia’s entire navy. The battle continued through **27–28 May**, as the chapter’s ending itself acknowledges. “In an afternoon” conflicts with its later account of fighting through the following evening. [Mikasa memorial museum](https://www.kinenkan-mikasa.or.jp/en/mikasa/battle_of_soj.html)

13. **Wrong disposition of surviving ships — high confidence.** “By the following evening, of the thirty-eight Russian ships, twenty-one had been sunk and most of the rest had surrendered.”

    Standard accounts give about six or seven captured ships, not a majority of the seventeen remaining after twenty-one sinkings. Others escaped or were interned in neutral ports. Counts vary with the vessels included, but “most of the rest” does not follow from the usual figures. [US National Defense University Press](https://ndupress.ndu.edu/Media/News/News-Article-View/Article/3106443/ghosts-of-tsushima-or-kobayashi-maru-japans-problematic-preoccupation-with-deci/)

14. **False geographic absolute — high confidence.** “Japan was a chain of islands whose capital was fed by coastal shipping and whose every large city lay within range of naval guns.”

    Kyoto alone breaks this claim: it was a major inland city, well beyond bombardment from the coast. The vulnerability of coastal cities and shipping supports the argument without “every.” [Kyoto city’s geographical description](https://www.city.kyoto.lg.jp/tokei/cmsfiles/contents/0000281/281300/1shou.pdf)

15. **Inflated description of the embassy — high confidence.** “In December 1871, with the country barely pacified, about fifty of the most senior men in the government boarded an American steamer and left Japan for nearly two years.”

    About fifty describes the official delegation broadly, not fifty top government leaders. It included subordinate officials and staff alongside a small group of leading statesmen. “Half the government” refers to the concentration of senior leadership aboard, not the rank of every delegate. [Japan Center for Asian Historical Records’ delegation roster](https://www.jacar.go.jp/english/exhibition/iwakura_en/person/index.html)

16. **Unsupported universal account of negotiations — high confidence.** “It had hoped to renegotiate the treaties, and was told everywhere that Japan must first have laws and courts that Westerners could respect.”

    Serious revision negotiations failed in the United States; the mission then refrained from substantive treaty negotiations in the subsequent countries. The need for legal reform is sound context, but “was told everywhere” invents a repeated negotiating sequence. [National Archives of Japan](https://www.archives.go.jp/exhibition/digital/modean_state/contents/Iwakura-mission/index.html)

17. **Anecdote placed in the wrong location — high confidence.** “A schoolboy in India named Jawaharlal Nehru, who would one day lead his country to independence, remembered waiting each morning for the newspaper.”

    Nehru followed the war eagerly while in India, but his autobiography places him on the train from Dover to London when he read about **Tsushima**. As positioned immediately after that victory, the sentence conflates his earlier enthusiasm with his location when its news arrived. [Nehru, *An Autobiography*, “Harrow and Cambridge”](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Nehru_Autobiography.pdf)

18. **Financial ambiguity and reader gap — high confidence.** “Now the lords were gone, any farmer's son could be a soldier, and in 1876 the government converted the salaries into bonds worth a fraction of their value and forbade the wearing of swords in public.”

    “Their value” has no clear basis of comparison. The bonds’ face value generally represented several years of stipends; their annual interest payments were lower than the former annual income. Explain that a bond was a government promise to repay money and pay interest, and distinguish its capital value from the income it provided. [*The Samurai Bond*, commutation scales](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/167197/1/702213.pdf)

19. **Misleading “first”; continuity problem — high confidence.** “It gave the state a steady income for the first time, and it weighed heavily on the peasants.”

    Earlier governments already collected recurring land revenue, something chapter 49’s surveys and tax officials have prepared readers to understand. The innovation was a nationally standardized **cash tax based on assessed land value**, payable regardless of the harvest—not the invention of dependable government income. If “the state” means specifically the new centralized Meiji government, say so. [National Archives’ account of the reform](https://www.archives.go.jp/ayumi/kobetsu/m06_1873_03.html); [chapter 49](chapters/49-japan/chapter.md:128)

20. **Missing causal link for a general reader.** “That summer a British squadron bombarded the capital of Satsuma and burned much of it.”

    The preceding action is Chōshū firing on foreign ships. Readers will naturally infer that Britain retaliated against the wrong domain. Satsuma’s separate confrontation arose from demands for redress after its retainers killed the British merchant Charles Richardson. One sentence establishing that dispute would repair the transition. **Historical correction: high confidence.** [Ernest Satow’s contemporary account, chapter VII](https://www.cyberlibrary.org/en/books/a-diplomat-in-japan-the-inner-history-of-the-critical-years-in-c638bd66/chapters/13/)

21. **Unexplained importance.** “The third was the Western calendar.”

    The paragraph introduces three measures “on which everything else depended,” explains the tax and army, then merely names the calendar. A reader needs to know what changed and why it mattered to the rebuilding of the state—or the claim of foundational importance needs reducing.

    Both announce the coming point instead of stating it. The next sentence, “The wealth of the West was new,” already supplies the substantive opening.
