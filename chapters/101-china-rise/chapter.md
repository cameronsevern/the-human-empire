# 101. How did China get rich?

*China, from 1978 to 2020*

China got rich by letting its people work for themselves, while the party that ruled them kept every lever of political power. It began when peasants were allowed to farm their own plots. It continued when foreign companies were invited to make goods for export on the coast, using the labour of more than two hundred million people who left the villages. In forty years, eight hundred million Chinese climbed out of extreme poverty, which is the largest escape from poverty ever recorded.

On a cold night near the end of 1978, the heads of eighteen families met in a mud-walled house in Xiaogang, a village in the eastern province of Anhui. Xiaogang was one of the poorest places in China. In the great famine twenty years earlier, sixty-seven of its hundred and twenty people had starved. In most winters since, the villagers had gone out on the roads to beg.

The land belonged to the collective farm. Everyone worked it together, at the sound of a whistle, and was paid the same whether he worked hard or leaned on his hoe. That night the eighteen men agreed to do something for which they could be shot. They would secretly divide the land among the families. Each household would deliver its share of the state's quota, and keep whatever else it grew.

One of them wrote the agreement out on a sheet of paper torn from a child's exercise book. It ended with a promise. If any of the men were jailed or executed, the others would raise his children until they were eighteen. Each pressed his thumb in red ink beside his name. The owner of the house hid the paper inside a bamboo pole in the roof.

The next autumn Xiaogang harvested more grain than in the previous five years put together. The neighbouring villages noticed at once. So did the party secretary of the county, who came to investigate, and so, in time, did the men who had just taken charge in Beijing.

## The survivor

The most important of those men was seventy-four, stood barely a metre and a half tall, and had spent a large part of his life being punished for common sense.

Deng Xiaoping was born in 1904, the son of a landowner in the inland province of Sichuan. At sixteen he sailed to France on a scheme for poor students, and worked for five years in a steel mill and a car factory, where he learned to love croissants and joined the Communist Party. He was on the Long March. He was a commander in the civil war and the party's chief administrator in the 1950s. After the famine he helped to put the country back on its feet, with the remark that the colour of a cat does not matter as long as it catches mice.

For that he was thrown out as a follower of the capitalist road, and spent years repairing tractors while his son lay paralysed after a fall from a window in the custody of Red Guards. He was brought back, and thrown out again. By 1978, two years after Mao's death, he had outmanoeuvred Mao's chosen heir and become the man who decided.

He had no grand theory. He described his method as crossing the river by feeling for the stones. What he had was a clear view of how far China had fallen behind. That year a Chinese person was poorer than the average African south of the Sahara. Deng went to see the alternatives for himself. In Japan he rode the bullet train and said that it felt as if someone were pushing you from behind. In America, early in 1979, he toured a car plant and the space centre, and at a rodeo in Texas he put on a ten-gallon hat, which did more than any speech to tell the Chinese that the Americans were now to be learned from.

In December 1978 he told a meeting of the party's leaders that it was time to stop arguing about doctrine and to seek truth from facts. Some people and some regions, he said later, should be allowed to get rich first.

## Farming for yourself

When the reports from villages like Xiaogang reached Beijing, some leaders wanted the peasants arrested. Deng said to let them carry on and see. It was permitted first in the poorest districts, and within five years the arrangement had spread to almost every village in China. The land still belonged to the collective on paper. Each family was assigned its own fields on a long lease, owed the state a fixed amount, and could sell the rest in the market.

The state also paid more for grain, and better seed and fertiliser were arriving. The great change was the incentive. In six years the country's grain harvest rose by a third. The communes, which had organised the lives of five hundred million people since 1958, were quietly dissolved. Farmers with money to spend wanted bricks, bicycles and cloth, and village governments set up small factories to supply them, which soon employed a hundred million people.

## A fishing town

The second experiment was conducted on the coast. Across a narrow river from the British colony of Hong Kong lay a county of rice fields and fishing villages called Shenzhen. Every year thousands of its young people swam the bay at night to reach the factories on the other side, where wages were many times higher, and some drowned.

Deng's answer was to bring the factories to them. In 1980 Shenzhen was declared a special zone, and soon afterwards fenced off from the rest of the country. Inside the fence, foreign companies could set up plants, import machinery free of tax, hire and fire as they chose, and send their profits home. The businessmen of Hong Kong, whose own costs were rising, moved their production over the border almost at once. The zone would supply the land and the workers, and the foreigners the money, the machines and the customers.

The model was close at hand. Japan first, and then South Korea, Taiwan and Singapore, had each gone from poverty to wealth in a generation by making things for the rich countries to buy: clothes and toys, then radios and ships, then cars and computers. China was offering the same bargain on a scale that nobody had seen, with a workforce larger than those of America and Europe combined.

Shenzhen had about thirty thousand people in its market town in 1980. Its builders boasted of adding a floor to a tower every three days. Today it has more than seventeen million.

## One child

The same pragmatists who freed the farmers took another decision in those years, and it was the harshest of all. They had concluded that China could not become prosperous if its population kept growing, and their engineers calculated that it must be held to about 1.2 billion by the end of the century.

From 1980 most couples were permitted one child. Women had to register their pregnancies and apply for permission to give birth. Those who conceived without permission were pressed, and often forced, to have an abortion, sometimes late in pregnancy, and in 1983 alone twenty million people were sterilised. Families who evaded the rules had their houses pulled down.

In the countryside a son was a family's only pension. When they could have only one child, many made sure that it was a boy, by aborting girls once scans made it possible to tell, or by abandoning them. At the worst there were nearly a hundred and twenty boys born for every hundred girls. China today has some thirty million more men than women, and a population that is ageing very fast. The policy was ended in 2015.

## The square

Deng had loosened the economy, and he had no intention of loosening anything else. In the spring of 1989 that was put to the test.

A decade of reform had brought inflation, which neared twenty per cent, and it had brought corruption. Steel, coal and many other goods now had two prices, a low one fixed by the state for the planned part of production and a higher one in the market for the rest. Officials and their children used their positions to buy at the first and sell at the second. Students, whose prospects depended on connections that they did not have, were angry, and so were workers on fixed wages.

In April a popular reforming leader died, and students from Beijing's universities marched to the great square in the middle of the capital to mourn him. They stayed. They asked for a free press, for dialogue with the leaders, and for officials to disclose what they owned. In May several thousand began a hunger strike, timed for a visit by the Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, which the government had to move out of the square in embarrassment. Workers, journalists and even policemen marched in support, and at the peak a million people filled the square. Art students built a statue of white foam and plaster ten metres high, a woman holding a torch in both hands, facing the portrait of Mao.

The party's general secretary, Zhao Ziyang, who was its formal head and Deng's own choice for the post, wanted to talk to them. Deng and the old soldiers around him remembered the Red Guards, and saw chaos. On 20 May martial law was declared. The first troops sent into the city were blocked by crowds of ordinary citizens, who lectured the young soldiers and fed them.

On the night of 3 June the army came back with tanks and live ammunition. Most of the killing took place on the broad avenue leading into the square from the west, where crowds had built barricades of buses. Soldiers fired into them. By dawn the square had been cleared. The government has never given a full account of the dead, and estimates run from several hundred to more than two thousand.

On the morning of 5 June a column of tanks rolling down the avenue was stopped by a single man in a white shirt, with a shopping bag in each hand, who stepped in front of the leading tank and moved from side to side to block it. Nobody knows his name or what became of him. Inside China his photograph cannot be shown, and many young Chinese have never seen it.

Zhao spent the remaining fifteen years of his life under house arrest. The bargain that the party offered the people from then on was plain. They could get rich, travel, and choose their own work, clothes and amusements. They could not question who ruled.

## Southern tour

For three years after the killings the conservatives had the upper hand, and reform stalled. In January 1992 Deng, who was eighty-seven and held no office, boarded a train and travelled south to Shenzhen with his family. At every stop he said the same thing. Development is the only hard truth. Whoever is against reform should step down. The newspapers in Beijing ignored the trip for weeks, and then gave in.

What followed was the real take-off. The state's loss-making factories were closed or sold, and thirty million workers who had been promised a job for life were dismissed. Private business, which had been barely tolerated, was now encouraged.

The labour came from the villages. Over the next thirty years more than two hundred and fifty million people left the countryside to work in the cities and the coastal factories. Nothing like it has happened anywhere, at any time. Most were young, and many were women in their teens, who lived twelve to a room in factory dormitories, worked eleven hours a day, six or seven days a week, and sent their wages home.

They did this as second-class citizens in their own country. Every Chinese is registered at birth as belonging to a particular place. A migrant registered in a village had no right to a free place for his children in a city school or to the city's medical insurance. So the children stayed behind with their grandparents, tens of millions of them, and saw their parents once a year, when the whole country travelled home for the new year festival.

## A box

One more thing was needed to make a factory in the Pearl River delta the supplier of a shop in Ohio, and it had been invented by an American trucking boss with no interest in China at all.

Until the 1950s, cargo went to sea much as it had in the days of sail. Gangs of dockers carried sacks, crates, barrels and bales up the gangway one at a time and stowed them in the hold by hand. A ship spent as long in port as at sea. Loading and unloading accounted for half the cost of sending goods abroad, and a good part of every cargo was broken or stolen on the quay.

Malcolm McLean owned a fleet of lorries and was tired of watching them wait at the docks. It occurred to him that the whole trailer might be lifted off its wheels and onto the ship, without anyone touching what was inside. On 26 April 1956 a converted oil tanker sailed from a port near New York with fifty-eight steel boxes lashed to its deck. Loading loose cargo by hand cost nearly six dollars a ton. Loading that ship cost sixteen cents.

It took twenty years, and a fight with the dockers' unions, for the idea to conquer. The boxes were made to standard sizes, so that the same one could ride on a lorry, a railway wagon and a ship, and be lifted by the same crane in any port in the world. The largest ships now carry the equivalent of twenty-four thousand of the smaller size. Sending a television set from China to Europe costs a few dollars.

The container made distance almost irrelevant. A manufacturer no longer needed to be near his customers, or to make every part in one place. A telephone could be designed in California, with a screen made in Korea and chips made in Taiwan, and put together by hand in a Chinese city where three hundred thousand people worked in a single compound.

## Made in China

In December 2001, after fifteen years of negotiation, China was admitted to the organisation that sets the rules of world trade. Its goods now entered the markets of the rich countries on nearly the same terms as anyone's, and foreign firms could invest there with confidence that the door would stay open.

In the seven years that followed, Chinese exports multiplied fivefold. The country became the largest maker of steel, ships, clothes, toys, furniture, telephones and computers, and before long of almost everything. In 2000 it made about a fifteenth of the world's manufactured goods, by value. Twenty years later it made nearly a third.

Shoppers in the West got a generation of falling prices. The towns that had made those goods got something else. In the United States, economists have traced the loss of at least a million factory jobs in the decade after 2001 to competition from China. It fell on particular places, a furniture town in North Carolina, a tyre town in Ohio, where the plant closed and nothing replaced it, and the anger of those places has shaped American politics ever since.

## The reckoning

For thirty years the Chinese economy grew at about ten per cent a year, which doubles it every seven. In 1981 nearly nine Chinese in ten lived on less than what two dollars a day would buy in America. By 2015 fewer than one in a hundred did. A child born in China today can expect to live to seventy-eight.

The physical change is hard to grasp. China opened its first 350-kilometre-an-hour railway in 2008, and now has more than the rest of the world put together. In the three years from 2011 it poured more cement than the United States used in the whole of the twentieth century.

The costs were of the same order. For years the air of the northern cities in winter was dangerous to breathe, and China now puts more carbon into the atmosphere than all the other rich countries combined. Farmers were pushed off their land for a fraction of its value. A country that had been one of the most equal on Earth became one of the more unequal.

What did not change was who governed. Many in the West had been sure that trade and wealth would bring democracy. In 2000 an American president said that China's attempt to control the internet was like trying to nail jelly to a wall. China nailed it. It built a filtered network of its own, behind a barrier that blocks foreign news and social media, and a system of cameras and data that follows its citizens more closely than any state has before.

Since 2012 the country has been led by Xi Jinping, the purged official's son who spent his teens in a cave village, and he has removed the limit on the number of terms that a president may serve. In the far western region of Xinjiang, from 2017, an estimated million or more Uyghurs and members of other Muslim peoples were held in camps for what the government called re-education. The party has nearly a hundred million members and no rivals.

The men of Xiaogang were not shot. Their county secretary protected them, and within four years what they had done in secret was national policy. A copy of the paper with the eighteen thumbprints now lies in a glass case in the national museum in Beijing, beside the square. The state whose punishment they had risked for it displays it as the founding document of the new China.
