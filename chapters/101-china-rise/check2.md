## Factual flags

1. **Major — state-industry reform did not begin after 1992.**

   > “This time reform reached the state’s own industry.”

   In this position, “this time” implies that earlier reforms had not reached state industry. Experiments granting state enterprises more autonomy and profit retention began in **1978–79**, and a substantial industrial reform programme followed in **1984**. The later change was a deeper restructuring, including closures, ownership changes and mass layoffs. The chapter’s earlier account of dual-track industrial prices also presupposes pre-1992 industrial reform. [World Bank history of state-enterprise reform](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/763341468025224944/pdf/577490NWP0Box353767B01PUBLIC10gcwp050web.pdf).

2. **Major — the communes retained substantial functions beyond farming.**

   > “With the land in family hands, the old structure had little left to do.”

   Household farming removed collective cultivation, but communes also performed governmental functions and supported services and enterprises. Their abolition involved **separating or transferring functions**, not simply retiring an organisation whose work had disappeared. Their role in financing rural healthcare was sufficiently important that dismantling collective funding contributed to the collapse of cooperative medical coverage. [Research on administrative restructuring](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/7fm329p1), [World Bank rural-health report](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/390331468769254476/pdf/multi0page.pdf).

3. **Minor — the new sentence turns a feared punishment into a definite outcome.**

   > “If it were found, it would condemn all eighteen.”

   If “condemn” means punishment or conviction, this conflicts with the chapter’s own account of officials discovering and protecting the experiment. The documented fact is that the signers **feared imprisonment or execution**. If the intended meaning is merely that the paper identified everyone involved, the wording does not distinguish that from sentencing them. This is a new regression, separate from the already-corrected ending. [NPR’s reporting from Xiaogang](https://www.northcountrypublicradio.org/news/npr/312488659/).

4. **Minor — the description of pre-container loading omits established machinery.**

   > “Gangs of dockers carried sacks, crates, barrels and bales up the gangway one at a time and stowed them in the hold by hand.”

   This is unchanged by the revision, but was not flagged in the earlier check. At major ports well before the 1950s, cargo was commonly assembled into **slingloads and hoisted over the ship’s side by winches or cranes**, then manually stowed. Containerisation reduced repeated handling; it did not replace a general system of carrying everything up gangways. A contemporary account of operations around 1934 describes the hoisting process directly. [Port of San Francisco historical documentation](https://www.sfport.com/files/2022-12/EmbarcaderoRegisterNominationSec8.pdf).

5. **Minor — the new component-transport sentence overextends the container example.**

   > “Each part could be made wherever it was cheapest, and the box would carry it to the next stage.”

   Following the telephone example, this suggests its international component chain travelled throughout by shipping container. Electronics supply chains use **both air and sea freight**, with air transport particularly useful for components and short production lead times. Containers helped make distributed production economical, but do not explain every transport stage of this example. [Transport Intelligence’s mobile-phone supply-chain analysis](https://www.ti-insight.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/High-Tech-Trends-and-Consumer-Electronics-Global-Market-Sizing.pdf).

## Clarity flags

- **The environmental distinction contradicts the example immediately following it.**

  > “The first cost fell on the environment.”  
  > “The others fell on people.”

  Dangerous air is already a cost to people. “First” also leaves the reader unsure whether this means earliest historically or first in the narrator’s list.

- **A new transition joins two different measurements.**

  > “Measured another way, in 2000 it made about a fifteenth of the world’s manufactured goods, by value.”

  Manufacturing’s global value share is not simply another measurement of the preceding seven-year export increase. The transition blurs exports and production, and moves backwards from the post-2001 period to 2000.

- **Several additions announce an explanation rather than give it.**

  > “The division of labour was simple.”  
  > “The reason lay in a system of registration.”  
  > “Much of the trick lay in agreement.”

  These are close matches for the style sheet’s prohibited point-announcing signposts. The sentences immediately after them supply the actual explanation.

- **Some new sentences repeat an adjacent sentence without adding useful orientation.**

  > “Each time he survived.”  
  > “Meanwhile the new money in the villages went looking for things to buy.”  
  > “All that handling was expensive.”  
  > “The saving was enormous.”

  Survival is already evident from Deng’s repeated returns. The village-money sentence immediately repeats “Farmers with money to spend wanted…”. The two shipping sentences announce conclusions that the adjoining cost figures make plainly.

- **The standardisation explanation leaves its key connection unstated.**

  > “Once the boxes matched, the ships could grow.”

  “Matched” is vague, and the reader is not told how standard dimensions made larger container operations practical. This compresses the mechanism precisely where the style sheet asks for a step-by-step explanation.

- **The container’s dimensions are never introduced.**

  > “The largest ships now carry the equivalent of twenty-four thousand of the smaller size.”

  “The smaller size” has no established referent. The earlier factual correction to capacity is retained, but a general reader still needs to know that this means twenty-foot containers.

- **The railway comparison has an unclear unit.**

  > “China opened its first 350-kilometre-an-hour railway in 2008, and now has more than the rest of the world put together.”

  “More” could mean railway lines, route length, trains or specifically 350-km/h railways. This is a clarity issue with the current comparison, not a repetition of the corrected opening-date flag.

- **Several absolutes break the style sheet’s exception rule.**

  > “The rule reached into every stage of a family’s life.”

  “Every stage” adds a universal reach the examples do not establish.

  > “In the countryside a son was a family’s only pension.”

  The intended point is dependence on sons for old-age support; “only” excludes daughters, savings and other support.

  > “The boxes were made to standard sizes, so that the same one could ride on a lorry, a railway wagon and a ship, and be lifted by the same crane in any port in the world.”

  “Any port” includes ports without suitable container-handling equipment.

  > “As a result, the children stayed behind with their grandparents, tens of millions of them, and saw their parents once a year, when the whole country travelled home for the new year festival.”

  “The whole country” is plainly too absolute.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit body cross-references. The added explanations of the Long March and Red Guards help meet the requirement to explain historical terms.

## Overall

The revision preserves the numerical facts and improves several explanations, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. The most important new problems are the implied post-1992 beginning of industrial reform and the explanation of why communes disappeared. Several additional transitions introduce avoidable repetition or ambiguity. No files were edited.
