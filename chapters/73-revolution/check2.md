## Changed facts

No numbers, names, dates or quotations were changed or removed. Most changes split sentences or make existing implications explicit. The following additions go beyond flow alone; inclusion here does not necessarily mean they are wrong.

1. **The revolutionaries’ reaction**
   
   Old: “The socialist parties had not planned this.”
   
   New: “The revolutionaries were caught by surprise.”
   
   Not planning an event and being surprised by it are different claims. The new sentence also extends the statement beyond the socialist parties.

2. **A new explanation of mass mobilisation**
   
   Old: “They were packed together, and they were angry.”
   
   New adds: “That pairing was dangerous, because angry people packed together can be brought out onto the streets in a single day.”
   
   This introduces a general causal claim and a timescale.

3. **The army’s loyalty**
   
   Old: “Its first order told every military unit to elect a committee of soldiers, which would control the weapons, and to follow the government's commands only where the Soviet agreed.”
   
   New adds: “That order put the loyalty of the army in the Soviet's hands rather than the government's.”
   
   This turns restrictions on authority into a claim about the whole army’s allegiance. See factual flag 1.

4. **The Bolshevik split and name**
   
   Old: “In 1903 the Russian Marxists split over who should be allowed to join, and Lenin's faction, which had won one vote at the congress, took the name Bolsheviks, ‘those of the majority’.”
   
   New adds, before and after that sentence: “That idea divided the movement.” / “The name was more boast than description.”
   
   These additions introduce a causal explanation of the split and a judgment about what the name purported to describe. See factual flag 2.

5. **Identification of the Red Guards**
   
   Old: “In the early hours a crowd of Red Guards wandered in through an unlocked door, lost their way in the corridors, and eventually found the ministers and arrested them.”
   
   New: “In the early hours a crowd of Red Guards, the Bolsheviks' armed workers, wandered in through an unlocked door, lost their way in the corridors, and eventually found the ministers and arrested them.”
   
   This adds a historical identification, rather than merely connecting sentences.

6. **Who promised the assembly**
   
   Old: “The elections for the long-promised assembly were held a few weeks later, and they were the freest that Russia would have for seventy years.”
   
   New: “There remained the assembly that every government since February had promised.” / “The elections for it were held a few weeks later, and they were the freest that Russia would have for seventy years.”
   
   “Long-promised” becomes an explicit claim about every intervening government.

7. **Lenin’s reason for accepting peace**
   
   Old: “He made peace with Germany in March 1918 at a terrible price, giving up Poland, the Baltic lands and Ukraine, and waited for Europe to catch fire.”
   
   New: “With that hope in mind, he made peace with Germany in March 1918 at a terrible price, giving up Poland, the Baltic lands and Ukraine.” / “Then he waited for Europe to catch fire.”
   
   The revision makes the anticipated European revolution an explicit motivation. This is compatible with Lenin’s argument for preserving Soviet power while awaiting international revolution, although the immediate military necessity remains unstated. [Lenin’s report on ratifying the treaty](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1918/mar/13.htm).

8. **What the revolution promised**
   
   Old: “That same year the sailors of the naval base outside Petrograd, who had been the pride of the revolution, rose and demanded soviets that were freely elected.”
   
   New: “The sailors of the naval base outside Petrograd, who had been the pride of the revolution, rose and demanded soviets that were freely elected.” / “In other words, they asked for what the revolution had promised.”
   
   The added sentence attributes a specific promise to “the revolution,” without identifying whose promise.

Other additions—such as the march growing, the mutiny spreading, Nicholas failing to reach the capital, cruelty occurring on both sides, and hunger and typhus killing more people than battle—make facts already conveyed by the old passage explicit. They do not materially change its factual content.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — Order No. 1 becomes a transfer of the whole army’s allegiance.**
   
   > “That order put the loyalty of the army in the Soviet's hands rather than the government's.”
   
   The preceding summary also says “every military unit.” The order was addressed to the **Petrograd district garrison**. It subordinated political activity to the Soviet, restricted obedience to the Duma’s Military Commission where orders conflicted, and explicitly retained military discipline on duty. Its influence spread, but it did not place the entire army’s loyalty under the Petrograd Soviet.
   
   **Correct fact:** The order established soldiers’ committees and sharply limited officers’ and parliamentary authorities’ control over the Petrograd garrison. [Full text, Yale’s Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/soviet_001.asp).

2. **Minor — New transitions revive the previously corrected explanation of the Bolshevik split.**
   
   > “That idea divided the movement.”
   >
   > “The name was more boast than description.”
   
   “That idea” points back to the need for professional revolutionary leadership, making that the dividing issue again. The second addition, followed immediately by the comparison between party membership and Russia’s population, revives the misleading denominator comparison identified in the earlier check. This is a regression introduced by the revision, rather than a repetition of an already resolved flag.
   
   **Correct fact:** The immediate disputes concerned membership rules and control of party institutions. “Majority” referred to the congress alignment in elections to central bodies, not a claim that most Russians belonged to the party. [Lenin’s account of the congress and split](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1905/feb/00.htm).

3. **Minor — October takes one night in the summary but two nights in the narrative.**
   
   > “There were no crowds. It was planned by a committee and carried out in a night.”
   
   The chapter itself begins the operation on the evening of 24 October and places the ministers’ arrest after the late-night signal on the 25th: early on the 26th. It also subsequently describes “a crowd of Red Guards,” contradicting the unqualified “no crowds.”
   
   **Correct fact:** The Petrograd seizure unfolded on **24–26 October**, with the ministers arrested early on the 26th. It lacked February’s mass street mobilisation; that is different from having no crowds. [Saint Petersburg Encyclopaedia](https://www.encspb.ru/object/2855711270?dv=1000000000&lc=en); [Hermitage account of the arrest](https://support.hermitagemuseum.org/en/news/view/72).

4. **Major — Germany’s revolution is confused with the defeated communist uprisings.**
   
   > “Revolutions did break out in Germany and Hungary at the end of the war, and each was put down within months.”
   
   Germany’s November Revolution was not simply put down: it ended the monarchy and produced the republic. Subsequent attempts to establish revolutionary council governments were suppressed. The earlier correction established that a German revolution occurred, but this replacement sentence introduces a different error about its outcome.
   
   **Correct fact:** Germany became a republic; communist and radical-left attempts to carry the revolution further were defeated. Hungary’s Soviet Republic was also overthrown. [German Historical Museum’s account](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/kapitel/weimarer-republik/revolution).

5. **Minor — Lenin’s foreign residence is presented as uninterrupted.**
   
   > “He became a lawyer and a follower of Marx, was exiled to Siberia, and from 1900 lived abroad, in Munich, London, Geneva and Zurich, writing under the name Lenin.”
   
   In a biography leading directly to his return in 1917, this omits an important interruption.
   
   **Correct fact:** Lenin returned in November 1905 and spent the following period in Russia and Finland, then part of the Russian Empire, before leaving again in late 1907. “Lived mostly abroad” would preserve the compression accurately. [V. I. Lenin Museum biography](https://lenin.shm.ru/en/main-page/biography/).

## Clarity flags

- **New signposting:** “What those peasants wanted would shape much of what followed.” This announces the importance of material still to come. The next paragraph already supplies the concrete land grievance.

- **Repeated wording:** “They were packed together, and they were angry.” / “That pairing was dangerous, because angry people packed together…” The explanation adds something, but immediately repeats both phrases almost verbatim.

- **Vague setup sentences:** “Something had broken, however.” and “His hold over her had a sad cause.” Both announce what the following sentence explains. They are close to the style sheet’s prohibited announcement of a coming point.

- **Repeated patience explanation:** “The trouble was that both decisions asked for patience from people who had none left.” This is followed by what the soldiers and peasants wanted, then “The government was asking both to wait, and they did not wait.” The opening and closing sentences perform substantially the same job.

- **Distant, ambiguous referent:** “Within hours he had issued two decrees, one for each of his first two words.” “His first two words” could mean the beginning of his speech to the congress. It actually refers to “peace, land, bread” several paragraphs earlier.

- **Unclear palace:** “Power had fallen into the street, and two bodies picked it up at the same moment, in opposite wings of the same palace.” The last named palace is the Winter Palace, but this means the **Tauride Palace**. A general reader will probably picture the wrong building.

- **Overloaded sentence:** “The crowds filled the main avenue, and the mounted police who were sent to clear it found that the Cossacks, the army's mounted troops, who were supposed to help them were letting the marchers duck under their horses' bellies.” The explanatory insertion and successive relative clauses obscure who is supposed to help whom.

- **Ambiguous comparison:** “Prices in the cities had risen fourfold since the start of the war, and wages had not.” Does this mean wages had not risen at all, or had not risen fourfold? The intended comparison needs stating.

- **Unsupported absolutes:** “The workers and soldiers obeyed it, and they did not obey anybody else”; “every peasant soldier would walk home”; and “not what anyone had marched for in February.” These make universal claims where the argument needs only predominant behaviour or expectations. The problem is their literal scope, not the narrator’s confidence.

- **Unclear replacement:** “The women of the bread queues had asked for bread, and the soldiers had wanted to go home. What they got instead was the rule of a single party.” “Instead” makes political dictatorship sound like an alternative to demobilisation, although the chapter has already described peace. The political outcome and the fulfilment of material demands need distinguishing.

There are no evident hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-references or chapter-number cross-references.

## Overall

The revision largely preserves the historical material, but it is not entirely fact-neutral: several transitions add causal claims, and the Bolshevik-name passage revives an earlier confusion. The clearest remaining corrections concern Order No. 1, Germany’s revolutionary outcome and October’s chronology. Most added breathing room fits the style sheet; the repeated explanations and announcement sentences do not. No files were edited.
