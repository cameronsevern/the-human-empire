   - Old: “It began with an accident.”
   - New: “It began with an accident rather than a plan.”
   - The addition now excludes planning, rather than simply identifying the immediate trigger. See factual flag 1.

2. **Timing of the revolt’s spread**

   - New: “It made no difference, because the revolt had already spread beyond that city.”

3. **Puyi’s life after abdication**

## Factual flags

1. **Minor — an accidental trigger is made to exclude prior planning.**

   > “It began with an accident rather than a plan.”

   Revolutionaries had already organised and planned an uprising. The accidental explosion exposed their organisation and precipitated action. The correct distinction is between a planned revolt and its premature, accidental trigger—not between accident and planning. [Academy of Chinese Studies, account of the 1911 Revolution](https://chiculture.org.hk/en/photo-story/1822).

2. **Minor — Song Jiaoren did not die on the railway platform.**

   > “Before he could take office, he was shot dead on a railway platform in Shanghai.”

   He was shot at Shanghai railway station on **20 March 1913** and died **two days later**. “Shot dead on a railway platform” places his death at the attack. This error was present before the flow revision. [Chinese Ministry of Culture’s historical account](https://en.chinaculture.org/focus/focus/2011xh100nian/2011-09/24/content_423610.htm).

3. **Major — Yuan’s nationwide obedience contradicts the chapter’s own account.**

   > “With him went the last authority that the whole country obeyed.”

   The preceding paragraph says his generals refused to follow him and provinces revolted. Those revolts began before his death; he therefore did not command the obedience of the whole country at the moment described. His death removed the strongest remaining central figure and accelerated fragmentation. The contemporary *China Mission Year Book* records provincial independence and the failure of his authority before his death. [1917 yearbook, opening chapter](https://www.jcyxysg.sdu.edu.cn/ziliao/1-1/China_Mission_Year_Book/1917.pdf).

4. **Minor — Manchukuo’s establishment remains attached to 1931.**

   > “When their army seized Manchuria, the homeland of his ancestors, in 1931, they set up a puppet state there, a country with a ruler of its own and Japanese officers in control.”

   The invasion began in **1931**, but Manchukuo was established in **March 1932**, when Puyi became its chief executive. His elevation to emperor in **1934** is now correctly distinguished; that fixed issue need not be reopened. The remaining problem is the sentence’s dating of the state’s creation. [Historian Suzuki Sadami, “Manchukuo: Imperial Japan’s Puppet State”](https://www.nippon.com/en/articles/-/14880).

## Clarity flags

- **Explicit signpost:** “It had two advantages.” This announces the explanation instead of giving it, contrary to the style sheet. The count also becomes unclear: training, motivation, and Communist organising appear to supply three advantages.

- **Answer-announcing setup:** “Its members asked why China had become so weak, and their answer was radical.” This tells readers an answer is coming; the next sentence gives it. It falls under the prohibition on signpost sentences.

- **Repeated fame:** “Oddly, one of his worst moments made him famous.” The short paragraph ends, “He was released a famous man.” The opening both previews the anecdote’s payoff and repeats its wording.

- **Padding immediately before evidence:** “They were a mixed company,” “The party began very small,” and “The advance was fast” each anticipate what the next sentence immediately establishes. Unlike the useful definitions of protectorate and puppet state, these add little orientation.

- **Breakable absolutes:** “answerable to nobody” and the newly added “with nowhere else to go” are unnecessarily categorical. The intended points are independence from effective central control and Sun’s turn to Soviet assistance; neither requires an unrestricted claim.

- **Unclear geography:** “in a city on the middle Yangzi” followed by “they retook part of the city” conceals the relationship between Hankou, where the bomb exploded, and Wuchang, where the uprising began. A reader cannot tell whether these are the same place or neighbouring cities.

- **An unexplained term carrying real content:** “on the Bolshevik pattern, with cells, discipline and a party army.” “Cells” needs a brief explanation as small local party groups.

- **An unhelpful measurement:** “about one coin’s weight for every person.” Coins have no standard weight, so the comparison gives readers no dependable sense of the silver involved. This is a clarity problem distinct from the earlier, corrected confusion between taels and ounces.

I found no chapter self-mentions, explicit cross-references, or hedging stacks.

## Overall

The revision largely preserves the historical content and improves breathing room. It introduces one misleading contrast about revolutionary planning and several unnecessary signposts or absolutes. The railway-station death, Yuan’s supposed universal authority, and Manchukuo’s founding chronology still need correction. No files were edited.
