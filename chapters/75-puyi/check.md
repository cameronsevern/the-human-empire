1. **Factual overstatement; style — high confidence.** “Overnight, the learning of millions of men became worthless, and the educated class lost its reason for loyalty.”

   Abolishing the examinations weakened an important connection between educated families and the dynasty; it did not instantly erase their education’s value or their loyalty. Leading constitutionalists continued supporting a reformed monarchy until 1911. Zhang Jian is a concrete counterexample. Preserve the causal argument, but remove the instantaneous, universal conversion. [Edward Rhoads, *Manchus and Han*](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/untitled-88783134-3ba9-49ab-95e5-0b51ee6d035b/section/89a9a5f8-dc3a-4ef2-9fba-bf22b8a40c3b).

2. **Misleading account of the revolution — high confidence.** “A determined government could have put the mutiny down.” “Instead, province after province simply declared that it no longer recognised the dynasty.”

   This sequence implies that the court failed to mount a serious military response. It did: imperial forces counterattacked and recovered Hankou, while revolutionary resistance continued. The important distinction is between military success and political disintegration, not action versus inaction. The counterfactual claim that determination would have sufficed is less secure than the narration suggests. [Rhoads, “The Court’s Military Response”](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/untitled-88783134-3ba9-49ab-95e5-0b51ee6d035b/section/89a9a5f8-dc3a-4ef2-9fba-bf22b8a40c3b).

3. **Contradiction with chapter 31; breakable absolute — high confidence.** “In every district, the families of landowners and merchants had their sons spend twenty years memorising the old books, because that was the one road to office, honour and wealth.”

   Chapter 31 explicitly says: “A high official could still ease a son or a nephew into a junior post.” It also explains that examinations mattered relatively little under the Tang and became the main route under the Song. “One road” contradicts those qualifications; extending that exclusivity to wealth is still harder to defend. The preceding “For thirteen hundred years” compounds the misleading impression of an unchanged system. [Chapter 31](chapters/31-exams/chapter.md).

4. **Factual overstatement; style — high confidence.** “All three ideas were borrowed from the West, and that was true of every remedy that young Chinese were now discussing.”

   Sun combined Western political ideas with Chinese traditions; Chinese reform proposals were not exclusively Western imports. His use of Confucian political ethics and traditional examination institutions supplies concrete exceptions. “Every remedy” violates the rule against absolutes that one exception breaks. [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Modern Chinese Philosophy”](https://iep.utm.edu/modern-chinese-philosophy/).

5. **Doubtful intellectual contrast; tension with chapter 73 — high confidence about the overstatement.** “Mao, who was among them, drew a conclusion that Marx and the Russians would have found absurd.”

   The following sentences need to distinguish **peasant participation** from Mao’s strategy of building rural bases and surrounding cities. Lenin explicitly supported a revolutionary alliance with the peasantry. Chapter 73 has already described peasants taking estates, Lenin endorsing their action, and Trotsky recruiting them. Mao’s strategic departure is real, but “the Russians” collectively finding it absurd is too broad. [Lenin, “Socialism and the Peasantry”](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1905/oct/10.htm); [chapter 73](chapters/73-revolution/chapter.md).

6. **Factual errors — high confidence.** “The peace terms required China to pay the eight nations four hundred and fifty million ounces of silver, with interest, over thirty-nine years.”

   The amount was **450 million Haikwan taels**, not ounces; a tael was a different weight/accounting unit. The settlement involved **eleven foreign powers**, not just the eight military allies. The following “one ounce for every person” consequently needs adjustment too. [Boxer Protocol, preamble and Article VI](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/United_States_Treaty_Series/Volume_1/Boxer_Protocol).

7. **Factual error — high confidence.** “Sun came home, and on 1 January 1912 the delegates of the rebel provinces elected him president of a Chinese republic.”

   He was elected on **29 December 1911** and inaugurated on **1 January 1912**. [Contemporary US diplomatic report](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d89).

8. **Factual error — high confidence.** “In July 1921 thirteen young men met in a girls' school in the French quarter of Shanghai to found a Chinese Communist party.”

   The congress met at **Li Hanjun’s residence**. The girls’ school was accommodation for visiting delegates, a detail sometimes conflated with the meeting venue. [Shanghai government’s account of the congress memorial](https://english.shanghai.gov.cn/en-MuseumsGalleries/20231222/511685f345fb4c798ebd06bf18d2ba01.html).

9. **Doubtful anecdotal detail — medium-high confidence.** “In 1896 he was seized on a London street by officials of the Chinese embassy, who locked him in an upstairs room and planned to ship him home in a crate to be executed.”

   His detention in the Chinese legation is secure; being physically seized in the street is not. Accounts differ over whether he entered voluntarily or was induced to enter. State the detention without choosing an uncertain capture scene. [Study of Sun’s London kidnapping accounts](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17535654.2021.2108602).

10. **Factual error — high confidence.** “An elder brother who had emigrated to Hawaii sent for him at thirteen and put him in a missionary school, where he learned English and became a Christian.”

    Hawaii introduced Sun to Christianity, but his baptism took place in **Hong Kong in 1883**, after his return. The sentence relocates his conversion. [Dr Sun Yat-sen Museum educational material](https://hk.drsunyatsen.museum/documents/4.-Activities/Education-Kit/Part-I/Edukits_P1.3b_comic-strip.pdf).

11. **Factual overstatement; style — high confidence.** “They began to write in the language that people actually spoke, where all serious writing had been done in a classical style as remote from speech as Latin.”

    Vernacular writing had a substantial history, including major Ming and Qing novels. The movement promoted vernacular writing into domains dominated by classical Chinese; it did not introduce serious vernacular literature. “All serious writing” is both a breakable absolute and an unexplained dismissal of those earlier works. [Columbia University, introduction to Ming vernacular fiction](https://video.afe.easia.columbia.edu/teaching-guides/introduction-to-ming-dynasty-vernacular-fiction-viewing-guide/).

12. **Misleading geography — high confidence.** “In the spring of 1919 the news reached Beijing that the victors had awarded it to Japan.”

    “It” appears to mean the whole province of Shandong. Versailles transferred **Germany’s rights and concessions in Shandong**, including the leased territory and railway and mining rights—not sovereignty over the entire province. Naming the concessions would prevent a general reader picturing the wrong territorial settlement. [Treaty of Versailles, Article 156](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919Parisv13/ch13subch8).

13. **Misleading chronology — high confidence.** “In nine months it had reached the Yangzi.”

    The Northern Expedition began in July 1926 and reached the Wuhan cities on the Yangzi by September–October, roughly three months later. March 1927 marks its arrival at **Shanghai and Nanjing**. The sentence merges those stages and obscures the geography immediately before the Shanghai massacre. [*Biographical Dictionary of Republican China*, Chiang Kai-shek entry](https://xboorman.enpchina.eu/biographie/jiang-jieshi/).

14. **Internal inconsistency; breakable absolute — high confidence.** “The only real army in China was the northern one, and it obeyed a general named Yuan Shikai, whom the court had just called out of retirement to save it.”

    The chapter has just described provincial army units overthrowing governments. The Hubei New Army was a substantial military force, not merely a nominal army. Yuan commanded the strongest force; “only real army” erases the forces whose actions the reader must understand. [Rhoads on the Hubei New Army and imperial counteroffensive](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/untitled-88783134-3ba9-49ab-95e5-0b51ee6d035b/section/89a9a5f8-dc3a-4ef2-9fba-bf22b8a40c3b).

15. **Reader orientation.** “When their army seized Manchuria, the homeland of his ancestors, they made him emperor of a puppet state there, and for eleven years he signed whatever was put in front of him.”

    This compresses three stages: invasion in **1931**, installation as chief executive of Manchukuo in **1932**, and elevation to emperor in **1934**. Eleven years is correct for his imperial title, but the sentence makes it sound as though invasion and enthronement coincided. One date and the state’s name would make the sequence intelligible. [Rhoads, “Court and Manchus after 1911”](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/6dc24326-22ee-4cdc-9f66-52376c2d4f10/section/7e1e0f94-50c9-4c3f-b2cf-83ccff13fb79).

16. **Reader orientation; excessive compression.** “In 1934, hard pressed by Chiang's armies, eighty-six thousand Communists broke out of their base in the south and set off on foot.”

    Since the last account of them, the Communists have changed from persecuted party members into an army governing a territorial base. Explain that transformation briefly, and identify these marchers as the main Communist army and accompanying personnel. Otherwise the reader cannot tell who is marching, what the base is, or why Chiang must conduct a military campaign against it.
