1. **Wrong financial claim — high confidence.**  
   > “The Mughal emperor's income was many times that of any king in Europe, which is why his grandson could spend twelve years of it on a tomb.”

   “Twelve years of it” means twelve years of imperial income. That confuses construction time with expenditure. Lahori reports twelve years’ construction and a cost of five million rupees; estimated annual imperial revenue was vastly greater. Say that Shah Jahan could finance twelve years of building. Sources: [Lahori’s figures reported by historians](https://www.dawn.com/news/371828), [study giving Shah Jahan’s revenue](https://pu.edu.pk/images/journal/csas/PDF/9%20Rukhsana%20Iftikhar_v28_2_13.pdf).

2. **Wrong geography and direct contradiction with chapter 29 — high confidence.**  
   > “It runs today, with small changes, between Turkey and Iraq on one side and Iran on the other, and it still divides lands that are mostly Sunni from lands that are mostly Shia.”

   Iraq has a Shia majority, including substantial populations along its Iranian frontier. The political border does not divide Sunni and Shia populations this way. Chapter 29 explicitly says Shias “form the majority in Iran and Iraq.” Keep the political frontier; remove the claimed religious boundary. Source: [Pew’s Sunni–Shia population estimates](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2011/01/27/future-of-the-global-muslim-population-sunni-and-shia/).

3. **Misleading Sikh chronology — high confidence.**  
   > “The dead man's son then turned the Sikhs into a brotherhood of soldiers.”

   This makes Sikh militarisation begin with Guru Gobind Singh after the execution of his father in 1675. Guru Hargobind had established an armed Sikh force after his own father’s execution in 1606. Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa in **1699**, a distinct development more than twenty years after the event just narrated. State that distinction and date; otherwise “then” supplies a false sequence. Source: [Wallace Collection’s Sikh historical context](https://www.wallacecollection.org/explore/explore-in-depth/the-sikh-empire/punjab-land-of-the-five-rivers/historical-context/).

4. **Unsupported generalisation about conversion — high confidence that it needs narrowing.**  
   > “Most of the Indians who became Muslims did so around such shrines, and the same kind of teacher carried Islam to Bengal and across the sea to Java.”

   Sufi shrines mattered enormously, but “most” asserts a measurable, subcontinent-wide explanation that the evidence cannot establish. Conversion followed different regional processes; in Bengal, religious change was closely connected with agricultural settlement and local community formation. Chapter 33 has also established merchants and harbour rulers as agents in Southeast Asia: teachers should be connected to that account, rather than silently replacing it. This is a continuity gap, not a direct contradiction. Source: [Richard Eaton, *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier*](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/the-rise-of-islam-and-the-bengal-frontier-1204-1760.html).

5. **Doubtful battle detail presented as established fact — medium-high confidence.**  
   > “The elephants, which had never heard gunfire, turned and trampled their own side.”

   The familiar elephant-panic story is not securely established by the contemporary account, and the claim about what these particular animals had previously heard is especially unsupported. Babur’s defensive position, flanking cavalry and concentrated missile fire provide the safer explanation. The Smithsonian’s account explicitly cautions against making either elephants or artillery decisive. Source: [Smithsonian, “From Peshawar to Panipat”](https://asia-archive.si.edu/ko/essays/memoir-06/).

6. **An absolute contradicted by the chapter’s own example — high confidence.**  
   > “A man taken from a Christian village at twelve had no relatives at court and nobody to plot with.”

   Recruitment could initially detach a boy from established court interests; it did not prevent him from acquiring relatives, clients or fellow recruits to conspire with. Sokollu Mehmed—the very example that follows—brought relatives into imperial service and built a substantial family network. Explain the sultans’ intention without claiming that it permanently worked. This also breaks style.md’s rule against exception-breaking absolutes. Source: [*Encyclopaedia of Islam*, Sokollu entry](https://isamveri.org/pdfdkm/18/DKM181197.pdf).

7. **A regional tax system presented as universal — high confidence.**  
   > “The tax was due in money.”

   The preceding account describes the measured, cash-assessed *zabt* system, not every Mughal district’s arrangements. Other assessment and collection methods continued, including payment in kind. Introduce the passage as how taxation worked in the provinces using this system; otherwise the reader learns a single uniform mechanism for the entire empire. Source: [NCERT’s account, hosted by IIT Kanpur](https://sathee.iitk.ac.in/sathee-railway-exams/student-corner/ncert-books/class-07/social-science/history-our-past-2/chapter-04-the-mughals-16th-to-17th-century/).

8. **Wrong or insufficiently supported opening location — medium-high confidence.**  
   > “In June 1631, in a tent in the middle of an army camp in central India, a woman named Mumtaz Mahal died giving birth to her fourteenth child.”

   She died at Burhanpur while accompanying Shah Jahan’s campaign. Official heritage accounts locate the death in the royal palace there, not a tent. “At Burhanpur, where her husband was conducting a campaign” preserves the secure fact without inventing the room. Source: [National Monuments Authority’s account of Burhanpur Palace](https://www.nma.gov.in/showfile.php?lang=1&level=1&lid=102&ls_id=131&nma_type=0).

9. **Doubtful retrospective diagnosis — high confidence that “probably” exceeds the evidence.**  
   > “He could not read, probably because of a condition we would now call dyslexia, and he had books read to him every evening and remembered them.”

   Dyslexia is a modern hypothesis, not an established explanation of Akbar’s reported illiteracy. Keep the reading aloud and memory; remove the diagnosis. This is precisely style.md’s “settle doubt before writing” case: the uncertain detail does not carry the argument. Source: [introduction to *The Writings of Antoni de Montserrat at the Mughal Court*](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004471993/front-1.pdf).

10. **Overstatement of Armenian control — high confidence.**  
    > “There they were allowed to build churches, ring bells and make wine, and the shah gave them control of the silk export.”

    Abbas made silk exports a **crown monopoly** in 1619. Armenian merchants received privileges and became important exporters, particularly to Europe, but the shah did not hand them unrestricted control of the export trade. Source: [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Julfa: Safavid Period”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/julfa-i-safavid-period/).

11. **False picture of unbroken religious opposition — high confidence.**  
    > “They had fought every Muslim ruler of Delhi for three hundred years.”

    Rajput houses followed different policies, including accommodation, service and alliances with Muslim rulers. Akbar expanded and institutionalised partnerships; he did not replace three centuries of unanimous hostility. “Every” also violates the explicit rule on absolutes. Source: [IGNOU, “Rajput States”](https://egyankosh.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/20224/1/Unit-11.pdf).

12. **Legal restrictions stated without their significant exceptions — high confidence.**  
    > “They could not ride horses or carry weapons, their churches could not be taller than the mosques, and their word counted for less in a Muslim court.”

    Restrictions existed, but Christian soldiers and other privileged groups could bear arms and ride horses; enforcement also varied. Present these as discriminatory rules to which non-Muslims were subject, rather than universal descriptions of their lives. This is another substantive breach of the absolutes rule. Source: [Vjeran Kursar, study of Ottoman legal and social structures](https://www.academia.edu/1780404/Some_Remarks_on_the_Organization_of_Ottoman_Society_in_the_Early_Modern_Period_The_Question_of_Legal_Dualism_and_Societal_Structures).

13. **Misleading terminology for a general reader — high confidence.**  
    > “The Taj Mahal is a Muslim tomb in a mostly Hindu country, built with Persian handwriting and Indian stonework, on land sold by a Hindu general to a Muslim king.”

    “Persian handwriting” easily reads as Persian-language inscriptions. The prominent Quranic inscriptions are Arabic, designed by a Persian calligrapher. “Arabic verses written by a Persian calligrapher” preserves the intended point about cultural collaboration. Source: [official Taj Mahal calligraphy account](https://www.tajmahal.gov.in/calligraphy.aspx).

14. **A compressed explanation obscures the distinction between public wealth and state revenue.**  
    > “Tolerance was affordable because the empire was rich, and it was rich because of a tax.”

    A tax transfers part of existing production to the state; it does not by itself explain why the economy produces so much. The reader needs the distinction between prosperous farms and workshops, an effective revenue system, and a wealthy imperial treasury. This is particularly noticeable after chapter 43’s careful distinction between money and wealth. It is an explanatory gap, not an objection to the narrator taking a position.

15. **The comparison assumes knowledge the intended reader may not have.**  
    > “A century after Ismail, his descendant Shah Abbas built a new capital at Isfahan, with a square seven times the size of St Mark's in Venice.”

    Unless readers know St Mark’s Square, this gives them two unknown sizes. Style.md asks for comparisons to something familiar today. Give a readily pictured scale, or describe what happened in the square so its size has a purpose.

16. **A genuine numerical stack under style.md.**  
    > “By his own account he had about twelve thousand men, and the sultan had a hundred thousand, with a thousand war elephants.”

    Three figures arrive together, followed shortly by several thousand musketeers and seven hundred carts. The point is that Babur claimed to be heavily outnumbered. Keep the approximate disparity and whichever single concrete figure best serves the battle scene.
