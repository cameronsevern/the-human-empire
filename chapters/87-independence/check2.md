## Factual flags

1. **Major — newly introduced: a former imperial power attended Bandung.**
   
   > “Much of the point lay in the meeting itself: former subjects talking to one another, with none of their old rulers in the room.”

   Japan attended. It had recently occupied several participating countries, including Indonesia, Burma and the Philippines—occupations the chapter itself has already described. The correct distinction is that the European colonial powers were absent, not that none of the participants’ former rulers attended. Japan appears in the conference’s own [final communiqué](https://worldjpn.net/documents/texts/docs/19550424.D1E.html).

2. **Minor — newly introduced: not all Bandung participants were already independent.**
   
   > “Meanwhile, the countries that had already won their freedom were looking for a voice of their own.”

   As the setup for the participant list, this incorrectly includes the Gold Coast and Sudan among already independent countries. Both attended in April 1955; Sudan became independent in January 1956 and Ghana in March 1957. The chapter’s opening already supplies Ghana’s later date. Sources: [conference communiqué](https://worldjpn.net/documents/texts/docs/19550424.D1E.html), [British parliamentary list of independence dates](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1977/feb/16/independent-states-former-british-empire).

3. **Major — newly introduced: commodity prices do not determine prosperity regardless of government policy.**
   
   > “A country in that position is rich when the price is high and poor when it falls, whatever its government does.”

   Commodity dependence creates exposure to price changes; it does not make government decisions irrelevant or guarantee national wealth during a price boom. Ghana’s crisis involved both cocoa prices and borrowing, investment choices, spending and depletion of reserves. The correct claim is that falling cocoa prices sharply constrained Ghana’s finances. The IMF’s historical account explicitly identifies both policy and prices as causes. [IMF, *Further Expansion of Activities*](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781451971477/ch029.xml).

4. **Minor — pre-existing: the Atlantic Charter was issued, not signed, in 1941.**
   
   > “In 1941 the British prime minister and the American president had signed a declaration promising to respect the right of all peoples to choose their own form of government.”

   Roosevelt and Churchill agreed and issued the declaration in August 1941. They did not sign it then. George Elsey later obtained their signatures on a commemorative copy in 1943. “Issued a declaration” supplies the correct fact without changing the argument. [Elsey’s oral history, Truman Library](https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/oral-histories/elsey8).

5. **Minor — pre-existing: Zimbabwe is assigned the wrong decade in the map’s alternative text.**
   
   > “Angola, Mozambique and Zimbabwe in the 1970s.”

   Zimbabwe became independent on **18 April 1980**, so it belongs in the 1980s. This finding concerns the Markdown image description; the image itself needs checking against that correction. [Zimbabwe Independence Order 1980](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1980/394/made/data.xht).

## Clarity flags

- **Dangling comparison:**  
  > “Among them were the vice-president of the United States and, in the crowd on the polo ground, a young Black minister from Alabama named Martin Luther King, who wept as well.”  
  Nobody else in the current opening has been described as weeping. “As well” refers to something no longer present.

- **A slogan appears to change constitutional authority:**  
  > “The difference was a matter of a few words, and it changed who set the timetable.”  
  The following account still shows the British holding elections, releasing Nkrumah and arranging the transfer. Explain whether this means political pressure shifted, rather than implying that the slogan itself transferred control.

- **Breakable absolute:**  
  > “They had no stake in the colony as it was, and there were far more of them than there were lawyers.”  
  “No stake” erases the livelihoods and property interests of clerks and market women. The intended distinction seems to be exclusion from political power.

- **Unclear scope:**  
  > “The grievance was simple to state: a people had lost its land, and had no lawful way to get it back.”  
  Does “get it back” mean recover particular confiscated plots, purchase land, or overturn the highlands’ racial land regime? The absolute makes those different claims indistinguishable.

- **Suez becomes an unlimited rule:**  
  > “Everyone watching could draw the conclusion: Britain and France could no longer go to war against American wishes.”  
  The evidence just narrated establishes their inability to sustain this operation under American financial pressure. “Could no longer go to war” has no geographical, financial or chronological limit.

- **Universal knowledge of private expectations:**  
  > “Nobody in Brussels expected that to matter soon.”  
  “Nobody” breaks the style sheet’s rule against absolutes that one exception defeats. It also leaves the time of that expectation unclear.

- **Confusing comparison of timetables:**  
  > “Thirty years had shrunk to five months.”  
  The thirty years belonged to a professor’s 1955 proposal; the five months ran from negotiations in 1960. This sounds like one official timetable suddenly being shortened.

- **Signposting rather than explanation:**  
  > “The clearest lesson in how far the old powers had fallen came not from a colony but from a canal.”  
  This announces a lesson before presenting it, contrary to the explicit signpost rule. Similarly, “Their method was simple and brutal” contributes little immediately before the concrete account of torture.

- **Repeated wording and conclusions:**  
  > “Like the third estate, they were many, they were poor, and they meant to become something.”  
  Repeats the analogy and “become something” already given in the same paragraph.  
  > “However, there were also gains, and they were real.”  
  “And they were real” adds emphasis without explanation; the next sentence supplies the evidence.

- **Unclear anniversary:**  
  > “Fifty years after that, the British government expressed its regret in parliament and paid compensation to five thousand elderly survivors of the camps.”  
  The nearest dated event is Kenyatta becoming president in 1964, but the compensation was in 2013. Anchor the interval to independence in 1963.
