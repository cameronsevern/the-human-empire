1. **Misleading central claim — high confidence.**  
   > “They gave them up because the war had left them too poor and too weak to hold on, because the people they ruled had organised to throw them out, and because the two powers that now dominated the world, the United States and the Soviet Union, both wanted the old empires gone.”

   American opposition to European empire repeatedly gave way to anti-communism and alliance politics. Washington supported France’s colonial war in Indochina. The opening needs to distinguish opposition to empire in principle from support for particular independence movements; otherwise the later American intervention against Lumumba seems inexplicable. [US State Department history](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1953-1960/dien-bien-phu).

2. **Wrong legal chronology — high confidence.**  
   > “Around them lived nine million Arab and Berber Muslims, who were French subjects without being French citizens.”  
   > “They could become citizens only by giving up Islamic law, and their votes were counted in a separate list that elected the same number of deputies as the million Europeans.”

   This combines different periods. Citizenship was extended to Algerians in 1946 without requiring abandonment of their personal legal status. The discriminatory electoral colleges persisted: formal citizenship did not produce equal political power. That distinction matters to the explanation of why reform failed. [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://gallica.bnf.fr/accueil/fr/html/la-nationalite-en-algerie-pendant-la-periode-coloniale-22-1870-1962).

3. **Contradiction with chapter 69; breakable absolute — high confidence.**  
   > “Their subjects believed that the Europeans could not be beaten.”

   Chapter 69 has already shown Africans repeatedly resisting conquest, winning at Isandlwana, and celebrating Ethiopia’s victory at Adwa internationally. It explicitly says, “Africans fought the conquest in nearly every region, and sometimes they won battles.” European military prestige mattered, but universal belief in European invincibility contradicts the reader’s evidence. [Chapter 69](chapters/69-africa/chapter.md).

4. **Overstated explanation of peaceful versus violent withdrawal — high confidence.**  
   > “It went that way where the rulers had little to lose.”

   Following the orderly flag-lowering ceremonies, this makes settler numbers and limited interests explain too much. Britain fought a prolonged war in Malaya, where strategic and commercial interests mattered greatly, then transferred power to a friendly government during the conflict. A short qualification would preserve the useful settler-colony distinction without making it a general rule. [National Army Museum](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/malayan-emergency).

5. **Wrong date and missing identification — high confidence.**  
   > “The man whom the British had jailed as the leader of the rising became the first president of independent Kenya in 1963.”

   Jomo Kenyatta became prime minister in 1963 and president on 12 December 1964. He also needs naming: “the man” has no previously introduced individual as its antecedent. [Kenya’s Presidential Library](https://www.presidentiallibrary.go.ke/audios/jomo-kenyatta-becomes-prime-minister-1963-and-president).

6. **Misleading Congo chronology — high confidence.**  
   > “Mobutu ruled the country for thirty-two years, as a firm friend of the West, and stole several billion dollars from it.”

   The placement immediately after his September 1960 coup implies that his uninterrupted dictatorship began then. His 32-year presidency ran from **1965 to 1997**, following a second coup. The missing five-year interval needs a bridge. [House of Commons research briefing](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/RP06-51/RP06-51.pdf).

7. **Misleading account of Lumumba’s turn to Moscow — high confidence.**  
   > “Lumumba asked the United Nations to expel the Belgians, and when it moved too slowly he turned to the Soviet Union, which sent aircraft and trucks.”

   The decisive disagreement was not simply speed. UN forces deployed rapidly, but the secretary-general refused to use them to crush Katanga’s secession. Lumumba sought Soviet help to overcome that refusal. Without this, readers cannot understand what either party wanted the UN to do. [UN chronology](https://www.un.org/depts/dhl/dag/time1960.htm).

8. **Doubtful precision in a prominent example — high confidence that qualification is needed; moderate on the exact replacement count.**  
   > “Sixteen of them had university degrees.”

   “Sixteen” is a frequently repeated estimate, not a secure census total. Pedro Monaville’s research suggests approximately thirty degree-holders. Herbert Weiss also warns that the familiar statistic obscures substantial seminary education. “Only a few dozen had university degrees” is safer; the heading “Sixteen graduates” shares the problem. [Monaville’s dissertation](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/102373/1/pmonavil_1.pdf); [Weiss, *African Studies Review*](https://doi.org/10.1353/arw.2012.0019).

9. **Misleading account of Congolese agency — high confidence.**  
   > “Then riots broke out in the capital, the Belgians lost their nerve, and they announced that they would leave in six months.”

   This collapses the January 1959 riots into the independence timetable negotiated in early 1960. Organised boycotts and Congolese leaders’ united demand at the Brussels conference were important intervening causes. The present version makes independence sound like a unilateral Belgian evacuation decision. [Weiss’s account of the negotiations](https://doi.org/10.1353/arw.2012.0019).

10. **False absolute — high confidence.**  
    > “The last empire to go was the first that had come.”

    Portugal’s African withdrawal was among the last major waves, not the end of European colonial rule. Spain remained in Western Sahara until 1976, and Zimbabwe attained independence in 1980. “Last” breaks both the factual claim and the style rule. [UN: Western Sahara](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/nsgt/western-sahara); [UN: Zimbabwe’s independence](https://legal.un.org/repertory/art73/english/rep_supp6_vol5_art73.pdf).

11. **Misleading financing — high confidence.**  
    > “The Suez Canal, which runs through Egypt and joins the Mediterranean to the Red Sea, had been built with French money and was owned by a company whose largest shareholder was the British government.”

    Egyptian financing was substantial: Egypt subscribed roughly 44 per cent of the original company capital. “French and Egyptian money” would avoid erasing Egypt’s contribution immediately before describing nationalisation. The British-shareholder clause is a separate, later fact. [Suez Canal Authority history](https://www.suezcanal.gov.eg/English/About/SuezCanal/Pages/CanalHistory.aspx).

12. **Misidentified troops — high confidence.**  
    > “In February 1942 a Japanese army captured Singapore, Britain's great fortress in Asia, and eighty thousand British soldiers marched into captivity with their hands up.”

    The captured force included British, Indian, Australian and local troops. “British-led troops” would preserve the point without turning colonial and Commonwealth soldiers into Britons. [Australian War Memorial](https://www.awmlondon.gov.au/battles/singapore).

13. **Misleading election denominator — high confidence.**  
    > “Nkrumah's party took thirty-four of the thirty-eight seats, and he was elected from his cell.”

    These were the 38 popularly elected seats, not the whole legislature. The assembly had 84 members, including territorial representatives and appointed members. Adding “popularly elected” supplies the necessary distinction without burdening the reader with all the numbers. [Ghanaian Museum’s account](https://ghanaianmuseum.com/the-first-legislative-assembly-elections-was-held-in-the-gold-coast-now-ghana-on-february-8-1951/).

14. **Doubtful anecdote; explicit style breach — moderate confidence about historicity, high about the editorial problem.**  
    > “At one of the receptions, the vice-president is said to have clapped a Black guest on the shoulder and asked him how it felt to be free.”

    The Alabama exchange circulates as an apocryphal story; “is said to have” does not settle its reliability. This violates the instruction to replace a weak example rather than qualify it. A documented alternative is King’s request that Nixon visit Alabama, where people were seeking the freedom Ghana was celebrating. [Contemporary oral-history discussion](https://static.jfklibrary.org/p4xucf82f37qv3m36cyd5x8rh28t48jm.pdf?odc=20231115181330-0500); [Stanford’s King Papers](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/interview-etta-moten-barnett).

15. **Chronological conflict with chapter 69 — high confidence.**  
    > “Their borders were the lines that Europeans had ruled across the map of Africa in the 1880s, which bundled rival peoples into one state and split others between several.”

    Chapter 69 carefully explains that Berlin established rules rather than drawing the continent’s borders, and that the partition unfolded over roughly thirty years. Restricting the inherited boundaries to “the 1880s” undoes that distinction. “During the partition of Africa” would retain the point. [Chapter 69](chapters/69-africa/chapter.md).

16. **Missing conceptual distinction for general readers.**  
    > “The poor countries, outside both the capitalist and the communist camps, were a third world.”

    The preceding paragraph includes communist China and American allies at Bandung. Readers need to know that the developing world, the Bandung participants and politically non-aligned states were overlapping categories, not identical memberships. As written, the definition appears to exclude countries just included in the story.

17. **Signpost sentence and avoidable chronological puzzle.**  
    > “Any doubt about who now gave the orders had been settled in a single week, six years before.”

    This announces the lesson before giving the event, contrary to “No signpost sentences.” It also asks readers to calculate backwards from Algeria’s independence. Opening directly with Suez in 1956 would orient them immediately.

18. **Unsupported universal claim; explicit style breach.**  
    > “There was truth in it, and there were also gains that no one who lived through those years has wished to give back.”

    “No one” claims knowledge of every participant’s wishes. The following evidence about education and life expectancy can establish real gains without this universal assertion. The problem is the exception-proof wording, not the favourable judgment of independence.
