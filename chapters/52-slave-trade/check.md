1. **Factual error; internal contradiction; broken absolute.** “For three hundred years they stayed on the beach.” “No army could be kept alive there, and none was sent.”

   Portuguese forces campaigned inland in Angola and conquered Ndongo’s stronghold at Pungo Andongo in 1671. Disease severely constrained European expansion; it did not prevent armies from being sent. The later Njinga passage contradicts these sentences directly. **Confidence: high.** [Research on the conquest of Ndongo](https://revistas.usp.br/revhistoria/en/article/view/121827).

2. **Factual overstatement in the central answer.** “Because the plantations of the Americas needed workers by the million, and Africa was the one place where European ships could buy them.”

   Africa was the principal source for the transatlantic plantation trade, not the only place Europeans bought enslaved people. Europeans also purchased and transported Indigenous Americans, whose enslavement continued alongside African slavery. The sentence turns an explanation of scale into a false claim of exclusivity. **Confidence: high.** [Brown University’s Indigenous slavery research](https://pstc.brown.edu/research/uncovering-untold-story-enslaved-native-americans).

3. **Disputed explanation presented as an inescapable rule.** “Her case shows the trap in which every ruler stood.” “His neighbour would sell, and would be paid in muskets, and would come for him.”

   The guns-for-captives cycle is a substantial historical argument, but its operation varied by region and period; historians dispute how systematically it explains the trade. “Every ruler” and the guaranteed sequence exceed the evidence. Your own example of Benin restricting exports for two centuries needs reconciling with this universal trap. **Confidence: high that the universal formulation is untenable; moderate on the mechanism’s relative importance.** [Research reviewing the competing evidence](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20780389.2019.1694408).

4. **Historical inference presented as an identified chain of people.** “Those prisoners were soldiers.” “They had been trained in the Kongo fashion, to fight in loose order with firearms, and many were Catholics who spoke some Portuguese.”

   The transition into Stono makes the rebels sound like identifiable prisoners from the preceding forty-year civil war. John Thornton argues that some rebels were probably Kongolese veterans, including possible captives from later wars in the 1730s; he does not establish this biography for the entire group. The earlier civil war and the later captives need separating. **Confidence: high about the evidential distinction; moderate about individual rebels’ origins.** [Thornton, “African Dimensions of the Stono Rebellion”](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.lib.jmu.edu/dist/b/420/files/2016/08/thornton-stono.pdf).

5. **Unsupported economic absolute.** “None of it was the kind of wealth that grows.”

   This excludes productive uses of imported goods and the circulation of trading income without establishing either. The chapter itself lists iron and currency among imports. Research supports a damaging diversion of resources towards capturing people; that is a different claim from saying none of the resulting wealth could support production or accumulation. **Confidence: high that “none” is unjustified.** [Whatley and Gillezeau’s economic analysis](https://websites.umich.edu/~baileymj/Whatley_Gillezeau.pdf); [research on imported iron](https://academic.oup.com/past/article/239/1/41/4791264).

6. **Doubtful identification at the centre of the eyewitness story.** “He was taken to Cape Coast Castle, the chief English fort.”

   Cugoano says he had forgotten the fort’s name and was carried from it by ship to another vessel ready to sail from Cape Coast. His account does not securely establish confinement in Cape Coast Castle. Consequently, the dungeon and chapel description cannot safely be presented as his personal surroundings without additional evidence. **Confidence: high that his account does not establish the identification.** [Cugoano’s narrative](https://brycchancarey.com/cugoano/extract1.htm).

7. **Factual overgeneralisation about disease.** “Both diseases had come from Africa in the ships.”

   Malaria is not a single parasite with one route of introduction. Ancient-genome research links American *Plasmodium vivax* to European strains, while supporting an African origin through the slave trade for American *P. falciparum*. Specify the latter if that is the intended claim. **Confidence: high.** [Michel and colleagues, *Nature*, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07546-2).

8. **Incorrect total.** “About ten and a half million were alive when the ships arrived.”

   The standard SlaveVoyages estimate paired with 12.5 million departures is **10.7 million arrivals**, not 10.5 million. **Confidence: high.** [SlaveVoyages methodology](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/blog/methodology-trans-atlantic).

9. **Misleading account of Dahomey’s organisation.** “After that the slave trade was the king’s own business.”

   If this means a royal monopoly, it is wrong. Kings controlled military captives and enjoyed commercial privileges, but private merchants remained important sellers. Royal participation should be distinguished from exclusive royal ownership. **Confidence: high.** [Robin Law, “Royal Monopoly and Private Enterprise … Dahomey”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/royal-monopoly-and-private-enterprise-in-the-atlantic-trade-the-case-of-dahomey1/885FB97D2FA6B22C2C762862DD6DB609).

10. **Incorrect ending to Njinga’s military career.** “Njinga fought them, made peace with them, allied with the Dutch against them, and fought them again until she died in her eighties.”

    Njinga concluded peace with Portugal in 1656, seven years before her death in 1663. Her final years included diplomatic and religious consolidation, rather than continued fighting against the Portuguese until death. **Confidence: high.** [Study of Njinga’s diplomacy and the 1656 settlement](https://dacb.org/resources/journal/5-1/5-1-JAN2020-JACB-ejournal.pdf).

11. **Uncertain demographic reconstruction presented as settled measurement.** “The population of West Africa barely changed in a hundred and fifty years.”

    The period is unspecified, and historical population totals are reconstructed from sparse evidence. Manning’s estimates describe substantial decline in coastal West and Central Africa from approximately 1730 into the nineteenth century; neither that geographical unit nor that trajectory is simply “West Africa barely changed.” State the period and region and identify this as an estimate. **Confidence: high that qualification is needed; moderate about the exact demographic trajectory.** [Manning’s regional reconstruction](https://patrickmanningworldhistorian.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/H3.2014.AfricanPop-Akyeampong.pdf).

12. **Misleading chronology for a general reader.** “The planters then tried poor Europeans.”

    The surrounding sequence implies a common progression across the Americas: Indigenous labour, then European servants, then Africans. These systems overlapped and differed between colonies. Brazil’s sugar industry shifted substantially from Indigenous to African labour during approximately 1560–1620; European indenture was especially important in other colonial settings. Locate the servants geographically and chronologically. **Confidence: high.** [SlaveVoyages historical overview](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/blog/brief-overview-trans-atlantic-slave-trade).

13. **Overgeneralisation; tension with chapter 47.** “They were sent wherever sugar grew, because that was where the labour was used up fastest.”

    Sugar was the largest destination for enslaved labour, but mining and other crops also drove the trade. Chapter 47 has already placed enslaved Africans in mints as well as plantations. Read as an explanation of everyone’s destination, this sentence erases work the reader already knows about. Make sugar’s predominance explicit without making it universal. **Confidence: high.** [SlaveVoyages overview](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/blog/brief-overview-trans-atlantic-slave-trade); [chapter 47](chapters/47-silver/chapter.md).

14. **Contradiction with chapter 44 unless geographically restricted.** “The Portuguese had planted a colony on her coast, the one place where they did try to conquer, and they wanted her kingdom for the captives it could yield.”

    Chapter 44 describes a Portuguese army capturing Ceuta and Portuguese forces seizing harbours from East Africa to Malaysia. “The one place” therefore cannot stand for Africa generally, much less Portuguese expansion generally. If you mean the principal exception among the tropical Atlantic trading regions discussed here, say that. **Confidence: high about the textual contradiction.** [Chapter 44](chapters/44-columbus/chapter.md).

15. **Missing geographical and motivational explanation.** “In 1739, in the English colony of South Carolina, a band of such men broke into a store of guns, raised a banner and marched south towards Spanish territory with drums beating, in the largest slave rising that the mainland colonies ever saw.”

    A general reader needs to know that the destination was **Spanish Florida**, where fugitives from British slavery could obtain freedom under Spanish conditions. Otherwise, marching towards another slaveholding empire seems unexplained. Also specify **British** mainland colonies for the superlative. **Confidence: high.** [Contemporary accounts of Stono, with historical introduction](https://www.nationalhumanitiescenter.org/pds/becomingamer/peoples/text4/stonorebellion.pdf).
