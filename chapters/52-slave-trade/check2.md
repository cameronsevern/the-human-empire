## Changed facts

No names, dates, numbers or quotations were changed or removed. However, several additions introduce claims or strengthen the meaning of existing ones. For additions, “old” quotes the relevant preceding text.

1. **Afonso’s response to the trade**
   
   **Old:** “His objection was that the trade had escaped his control and was eating his own people.”
   
   **New:** “His objection was a narrower one: the trade had escaped his control and was eating his own people. For that reason he did not try to end it.”
   
   The added sentence makes a new claim about what Afonso did not attempt. See factual flag 1.

2. **Practical protection of servants’ rights**
   
   **Old:** “However, there were never enough of them, they could not be held beyond their term, and they had the legal rights of Christians and subjects.”
   
   **New:** “Third, they had the legal rights of Christians and subjects, which a master could not simply ignore.”
   
   Having rights becomes a stronger assertion about their practical enforceability.

3. **Disease as the deciding cause**
   
   **Old:** “Yellow fever and the deadliest kind of malaria had come from Africa in the ships.”
   
   **New addition:** “Those two diseases are the reason the planters’ search ended where it did.”
   
   This promotes disease from an explanatory factor to the deciding explanation. That is a substantive interpretive addition, although I am not flagging the interpretation merely for being confidently stated.

4. **Europeans’ subsequent role**
   
   **Old:** “The Portuguese learned quickly that it was safer and cheaper to buy.”
   
   **New:** “The Portuguese learned quickly that it was safer and cheaper to buy. From then on, the Europeans on the coast were customers.”
   
   A claim about Portuguese practice becomes a continuing generalisation about all Europeans on the coast.

5. **The direction of the forts’ guns**
   
   **Old:** “Their cannon pointed out to sea, at rival Europeans.”
   
   **New:** “Their cannon pointed out to sea, at rival Europeans, and not inland at the Africans around them.”
   
   The revision explicitly excludes a use of the guns that the old sentence did not exclude. See factual flag 2.

6. **The objectives of African warfare**
   
   **Old:** “A great man was one with many people.”
   
   **New addition:** “A ruler who wanted to grow stronger therefore went looking for people, not for acres, and war was one way to get them.”
   
   This adds a claim about rulers’ objectives, including an exclusion of territorial acquisition.

7. **Identical trading circumstances**
   
   **Old:** “African rulers did not respond alike.”
   
   **New:** “Every ruler on the coast now faced the same customers and the same offer. African rulers did not respond alike.”
   
   The new sentence introduces universal sameness of customers and terms, which the old comparison did not require.

8. **Dahomey’s control of the trade**
   
   **Old:** “After that the king was the largest seller in his own port, and private merchants paid him for leave to trade beside him.”
   
   **New addition:** “The conquest put the trade in royal hands.”
   
   This introduces broader language about royal control. The retained sentence about private merchants prevents it from being an unambiguous monopoly claim, but the wording is less precise.

9. **The location of Cugoano’s sale**
   
   **Old:** “He was sold for a gun, a piece of cloth and some lead.”
   
   **New:** “There he was sold for a gun, a piece of cloth and some lead.”
   
   “There” explicitly locates the sale in the town near the sea mentioned immediately before it.

10. **Survival of documentary evidence**
    
    **Old:** “Captains kept logs, port officials collected duties, and insurers wrote policies.”
    
    **New addition:** “Because so much paper was produced, a great deal of it has survived.”
    
    This adds a causal explanation for the surviving evidence, rather than merely identifying the records.

11. **The meaning of the difference between totals**
    
    **Old:** “About ten million seven hundred thousand were alive when the ships arrived.”
    
    **New addition:** “The difference between the two figures is the number who died at sea.”
    
    This explicitly interprets the difference between the unchanged departure and arrival estimates as mortality.

12. **Replacement purchases and Atlantic crossings**
    
    **Old:** “The great majority were sent where sugar grew, because that was where labour was used up fastest.”
    
    **New addition:** “A sugar planter wore out his workers and bought more, and each purchase meant another passage across the ocean.”
    
    This introduces a one-purchase–one-crossing claim. See factual flag 3.

13. **The relative agricultural importance of those taken**
    
    **Old:** “The planters wanted young men, and the ships carried nearly two males for every female.”
    
    **New addition:** “The people removed were therefore the ones a farming community could least spare.”
    
    The sex ratio becomes a claim about which workers were most indispensable. That conclusion does not follow from the ratio alone.

14. **Whose soldiers Kongo sold**
    
    **Old:** “Each side sold its prisoners to the ships to pay for muskets.”
    
    **New addition:** “The kingdom whose king had once tried to inspect every sale was now selling its own soldiers.”
    
    This shifts the description from rival factions selling prisoners to the kingdom selling “its own soldiers,” creating an ambiguity about who captured and sold whom.

Other additions chiefly restate existing material: the pre-American plantation arrangement, the guns-and-captives cycle, Njinga’s simultaneous resistance and trading, the *Brookes*’ lawful capacity, losses before embarkation, and the transportation of military experience.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — newly introduced: Afonso’s stated request is contradicted.**
   
   > “For that reason he did not try to end it.”

   “It” refers to the trade. In his letter of 6 July 1526, Afonso explicitly requested that there should be no slave trade or outlet for slaves in his kingdoms. His participation in slavery and his measures to regulate exports do not erase that request. The correct distinction is between opposing slavery as an institution and asking Portugal to stop this trade from his kingdoms. [Afonso’s 1526 letters, translated text](https://sites.google.com/view/hist101-02/primary-sources/king-afonso-l-of-the-kongo).

2. **Major — newly strengthened: the forts’ guns were not exclusively seaward.**
   
   > “Their cannon pointed out to sea, at rival Europeans, and not inland at the Africans around them.”

   Cape Coast’s 1749 ordnance inventory lists guns on its northern as well as southern bastions. Records also describe fort guns being fired upon the adjoining African town, killing inhabitants and destroying houses. Seaward defence against European rivals was important; the categorical exclusion of landward defence and attacks on Africans is wrong. [*Records Relating to the Gold Coast Settlements*, foreword and p. 4](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781136960703_A23876854/preview-9781136960703_A23876854.pdf).

3. **Minor — newly introduced: purchases did not each require an Atlantic crossing.**
   
   > “A sugar planter wore out his workers and bought more, and each purchase meant another passage across the ocean.”

   Planters also bought people already in the Americas, including people born there and people resold after earlier importation. Replacement demand sustained Atlantic trafficking, but individual purchases cannot be equated with new transatlantic passages. [SlaveVoyages’ intra-American database methodology](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/blog/methodology-intra-american).

4. **Minor — pre-existing, not covered by the earlier check: first contact and baptism are conflated.**
   
   > “The king of Kongo had been baptised thirty-five years earlier, when the first Portuguese ships arrived, and had taken the name Afonso.”

   The baptism belongs to 1491, but Portuguese contact began in 1482–83, several years earlier. The 1491 arrival was a subsequent embassy and missionary expedition. [Malyn Newitt, *The Portuguese in West Africa*, “Discovery of the Kingdom of Kongo”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/portuguese-in-west-africa-14151670/discovery-of-the-kingdom-of-kongo/A2502C8275AA58193C3859661562B921).

## Clarity flags

1. **Explicit signposts have been added or restored.**
   
   > “Two things kept them there.”
   
   > “The details show who was in charge.”
   
   > “His story is known because of what happened next.”
   
   > “Three kinds of loss, nevertheless, are plain.”
   
   These announce the explanation instead of supplying it. The last also restores the function of “Some things are plain,” flagged in the earlier check.

2. **The prohibited surprise announcement has returned.**
   
   > “Where they were taken may surprise a reader who thinks first of the United States.”
   
   This is the same stylistic problem identified previously, now reintroduced in slightly different wording. The destination figures already deliver the surprise.

3. **The servants’ enumeration miscounts its own explanation.**
   
   > “From the planter’s point of view, however, such servants had three drawbacks.”
   
   After three numbered drawbacks comes “They also died”—a fourth drawback. The signpost both interrupts the narrative and creates an avoidable structural mismatch.

4. **Several additions repeat the immediately surrounding explanation.**
   
   > “He was, in short, a ruler who had taken up the newcomers’ religion and language, and who wrote to their king as an equal.”
   
   This summarises facts just supplied in the opening paragraph.
   
   > “The trade paid its African suppliers, and it left them with little to build on.”
   
   This repeats the preceding account of proceeds and their uses.
   
   > “The ships carried that training across the ocean along with the men.”
   
   The Stono episode immediately demonstrates this point more vividly. These are candidates for padding under the style sheet, rather than necessary orientation.

5. **New universal wording is broader than the comparison needs.**
   
   > “Every ruler on the coast now faced the same customers and the same offer.”
   
   “Every,” “same” and “now” flatten different regions and centuries into one situation. The examples move from early-sixteenth-century Benin to eighteenth-century Dahomey.
   
   > “A ruler who wanted to grow stronger therefore went looking for people, not for acres, and war was one way to get them.”
   
   The exclusion of land makes an argument about the relative importance of people sound like a universal rule about rulers’ objectives.

6. **The new agricultural conclusion needs an actual connection.**
   
   > “The people removed were therefore the ones a farming community could least spare.”
   
   “Therefore” does not follow from the preceding male-to-female ratio: males include boys, and the ratio establishes neither agricultural roles nor the comparative importance of women’s work. The damage caused by removing working-age people is understandable without that ranking.

7. **“Its own soldiers” obscures the civil-war mechanism.**
   
   > “The kingdom whose king had once tried to inspect every sale was now selling its own soldiers.”
   
   A reader may understand this as rulers selling their serving troops. The preceding sentence describes rival factions selling captured opponents. The new sentence blurs that distinction.

8. **A few terms remain unexplained for the intended reader.**
   
   > “Wealth and power were therefore counted in dependants: wives, children, clients and slaves.”
   
   “Clients” means political or social dependants here, not customers.
   
   > “He paid customs to the king before he was allowed to open trade…”
   
   “Customs” means duties or trading charges, not local practices.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or chapter-number cross-references.

## Overall

The revision preserves the numerical facts but is not entirely fact-neutral. Its main problems are new categorical claims about Afonso, fort artillery and replacement purchases, plus several signposts and redundant summaries. The chapter’s chosen interpretations can remain confident; these specific claims need correction. No files were edited.
