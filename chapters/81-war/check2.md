No names, dates, numbers or quotations were removed or changed. However, the revision adds assertions and makes some previously implied relationships explicit.

**Changes to existing sentences:**

1. **The Blitz’s chronology**
   - Old: “The Germans turned to bombing London and the other cities by night, and killed forty-three thousand civilians in eight months.”
   - New: “After that, the Germans turned to bombing London and the other cities by night, and killed forty-three thousand civilians in eight months.”
   
   “After that” places the Blitz after the failed campaign against the RAF. The campaigns overlapped; see factual flag 2.

2. **Steam engines’ limitations**
   - Old: “It can move a ship or a train.”
   - New: “It can move a ship or a train, but it is far too heavy for anything small.”
   
   This introduces a false technological limitation; see factual flag 1.

3. **The engine’s sequence**
   - Old: “A mist of petrol and air is drawn in, squeezed by the rising piston, and set off by a spark, and the small explosion drives the piston down.”
   - New: “First, a mist of petrol and air is drawn in, squeezed by the rising piston, and set off by a spark, and the small explosion drives the piston down.”
   - Old: “A crank at the bottom turns that push into the spinning of a shaft, as a cyclist's leg turns a pedal.”
   - New: “Next, a crank at the bottom turns that push into the spinning of a shaft, as a cyclist's leg turns a pedal.”
   - Old: “The piston rises again to sweep out the burnt gas, and the cycle repeats thousands of times a minute.”
   - New: “Finally, the piston rises again to sweep out the burnt gas, and the cycle repeats thousands of times a minute.”
   
   The added sequence makes crank rotation sound like a separate subsequent operation; see factual flag 3.

4. **Penicillin production**

   This explicitly connects industrial production to the melon-derived strain, rather than leaving that connection implicit.

   - New: “Caught at that moment, with fuel and bombs in their hangars, three were ablaze within six minutes, and the fourth was hit that afternoon.”

## Factual flags

1. **Major — newly introduced false limitation on steam power.**
   
   > “It can move a ship or a train, but it is far too heavy for anything small.”

   Steam engines powered cars, including commercially produced light vehicles. The useful distinction is the petrol engine’s practical advantages in weight, operation and range—not steam’s inability to propel small vehicles. The Science Museum holds an **1899 two-seat Stanley steam car**. [Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co24790)

2. **Minor — newly sharpened chronological error.**
   
   > “After that, the Germans turned to bombing London and the other cities by night, and killed forty-three thousand civilians in eight months.”

   Following the preceding account of Germany’s failure to destroy the RAF, “After that” makes the campaigns sequential. The Blitz began on **7 September 1940**, while the Battle of Britain continued through **31 October**. The shift to bombing London occurred during the struggle for air superiority. [Imperial War Museums factsheet](https://www.iwm.org.uk/sites/default/files/press-release/Battle_of_Britain_and_Blitz_Factsheet.pdf)

3. **Minor — the new sequencing misdescribes the engine.**
   
   > “Next, a crank at the bottom turns that push into the spinning of a shaft, as a cyclist's leg turns a pedal.”

   The crank turns **as** the piston descends; this is not the next step after the downward stroke. The four strokes are intake, compression, power and exhaust, with the crankshaft rotating throughout. [NASA’s engine explanation](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/engine-thermodynamic-analysis/)

4. **Minor — unchanged sentence assigns the declarations of war to the wrong day.**
   
   > “Nine days later, on 1 September, the German army crossed the Polish border, and Britain and France, which had promised to defend Poland, declared war.”

   The shared date makes both events happen on 1 September. Germany invaded that day; Britain and France declared war on **3 September 1939**. [United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/timeline-event/holocaust/1939-1941/britain-and-france-declare-war)

5. **Minor — unchanged mistranslation of Tanya’s diary.**
   
   > “Zhenya died on 28 December at twelve in the morning, 1941.”

   The diary records **noon**, not midnight. This matters because the passage presents the diary’s wording directly. [Photograph and translation of the diary entry](https://www.rferl.org/a/operation-barbarossa-photos-nazi-invasion-soviet-union/31320468.html)

6. **Minor — unchanged claim that penicillin went unused.**
   
   > “He could not extract the substance, and the discovery lay unused for ten years.”

   Penicillin had limited experimental and clinical uses before the Oxford programme. Cecil George Paine successfully treated eye infections with crude penicillin in **1930**. The accurate distinction is that it had not become a widely available, reliably manufactured medicine. [University of Sheffield’s account](https://sheffield.ac.uk/biosciences/news/first-use-penicillin-university-sheffield-recognised-uks-best-breakthroughs-list)

These are separate from the errors already corrected following `check.md`.

## Clarity flags

1. **Repeated wording at Midway**
   
   > “The carriers were waiting, and on the morning of 4 June 1942 their dive bombers caught the Japanese carriers while fuelled and armed aircraft crowded their hangars.”
   >
   > “Caught at that moment, with fuel and bombs in their hangars, three were ablaze within six minutes, and the fourth was hit that afternoon.”

   “Caught,” the fuel and armaments, and the hangars all recur immediately. The added clause slows the decisive moment without clarifying it.

2. **Empty emphasis**
   
   > “That mattered enormously.”

   This is padding. The next sentence already explains the significance through earlier wartime deaths.

3. **Mechanical signposting**
   
   > “The engine works in a cycle of steps.”

   This announces the explanation instead of beginning it. The following “First / Next / Finally” also divides the mechanism inaccurately, as noted above. The style sheet welcomes guidance, but this framing adds little.

4. **An absolute contradicted inside the chapter**
   
   > “The price was that everything now ran on oil.”

   The chapter itself repeatedly describes armies dependent on horses. “Everything” breaks the style sheet’s rule and obscures the intended point about motorised warfare.

5. **An unexplained comparison**
   
   > “An armoured division burned as much fuel in a day as a town, and an army that ran dry stopped where it stood.”

   A town of what size, using fuel for what? The comparison sounds concrete but gives a general reader no usable sense of scale.

6. **An unidentified battle**
   
   > “The German army attacked once more, the following summer, and was beaten in the largest tank battle ever fought.”

   The reader is not told that this means Kursk. The superlative draws attention to an event the narrative leaves unnamed and unlocated.

7. **An unidentified antecedent**
   
   > “Stalin had been demanding it for two years.”

   After the Normandy landing sentence, “it” sounds like that particular landing. The intended demand was for a second front in western Europe; naming that would clarify the political point.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or body cross-references. Most of the added transitions provide the breathing room the style sheet requests.

## Overall

The revision improves paragraphing and generally preserves the argument, but it is not entirely fact-neutral: the steam claim is new and false, and two sequencing changes introduce misleading implications. Several smaller errors remain in unchanged text. No files were edited.
