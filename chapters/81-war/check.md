Read the requested files; no files edited. Findings below are ranked by seriousness. Confidence labels apply to factual corrections.

1. **Factual error — the ending’s geography.**  
   > “The river on which they met was, within a few years, a border between two halves of a continent, and their two armies faced each other across it for the next forty-five years.”

   This implies that the Torgau meeting line became the Cold War frontier. Torgau and both banks there became part of East Germany. Only a roughly 95-kilometre stretch of the lower Elbe, much farther downstream, formed the inter-German border. The armies did not remain opposite each other at their meeting place. **Confidence: high.** [Study of the Elbe and East Germany’s borders](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/central-european-history/article/river-runs-through-it-the-elbe-socialist-security-and-east-germanys-borders/EACFD112DC3CFDA03BB4181A4430A181).

2. **Factual overstatement and contradiction with chapter 80 — the opening.**  
   > “Germany and Japan began the war with the best armies in the world and conquered most of Europe and half of Asia in two years.”

   “Half of Asia” substantially exaggerates Japan’s territorial conquests. The shared two-year chronology also fails: chapter 80 has Japan invading Manchuria in 1931, beginning full-scale war in China in 1937, and conquering its southern empire in 1941–42. That chapter also describes Soviet forces defeating a Japanese division in 1939, making an unqualified ranking of Japan’s army as the world’s best particularly doubtful. State their demonstrated early successes instead. **Confidence: high on geography and chronology; medium-high on the unsupported ranking.** [U.S. Army wartime maps of Japanese expansion](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark%3A/67531/metadc858/).

3. **Factual error — Midway’s decisive attacks.**  
   > “The carriers were waiting, and on the morning of 4 June 1942 their dive bombers caught four Japanese carriers with their decks full of fuelled and armed aircraft.”

   Three carriers were fatally hit in the morning; *Hiryū* survived to launch counterattacks and was hit late that afternoon. The familiar picture of four flight decks crowded with ready strike aircraft is also wrong: much of the dangerous concentration was in the hangars. **Confidence: high.** [U.S. Navy interrogations of Japanese participants](https://www.history.navy.mil/content/history/nhhc/research/library/online-reading-room/title-list-alphabetically/b/battle-of-midway-interrogation-of-japanese-officials.html), [National WWII Museum account of the afternoon attack](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/words-war-28).

4. **Factual error — French tank organisation.**  
   > “They used them in the old way, scattered along the line to support the infantry.”

   France also fielded concentrated armoured formations: light mechanised divisions and armoured divisions. Infantry-support deployment was part of the picture, not the whole explanation for defeat. The contrast needs to turn on command, communications and coordination rather than France supposedly having no concentrated armour. **Confidence: high.** [The Tank Museum, French Second World War tanks](https://tankmuseum.org/article/french-second-world-war-tanks).

5. **Factual error — the German tank comparison.**  
   > “German engineers built the most powerful tank of the war, a monster of fifty-seven tons that could destroy any opponent at two kilometres.”

   The weight and subsequent production figure identify the Tiger I. Germany later fielded the heavier Tiger II, with stronger armour and a more powerful gun. “Any opponent at two kilometres” also ignores armour, angle and ammunition; it is precisely an absolute that exceptions break. Describe the Tiger I’s actual advantage against contemporary opponents. **Confidence: high.** [National Army Museum, Tiger II](https://collection.nam.ac.uk/detail.php?acc=2007-06-3-29), [Tank Museum comparison of the two guns](https://tankmuseum.org/article/same-size-different-round).

6. **Factual distortion — the engine’s importance in the previous war.**  
   > “All of this turned on a machine that had hardly mattered in the previous war.”

   Internal-combustion engines already mattered greatly in 1914–18, powering aircraft, trucks and tanks. At Verdun, motor transport sustained the French defence along the supply road where a truck passed approximately every fourteen seconds. The defensible distinction is greatly increased dependence on engines, not their previous insignificance. **Confidence: high.** [Verdun Memorial’s battle history](https://memorial-verdun.fr/en/ressources/la-bataille-de-verdun).

7. **Factual error — where the evacuated factories went.**  
   > “In the summer and autumn of 1941, as the front came nearer, more than fifteen hundred factories were unbolted from their floors, loaded onto railway wagons with their workers, and carried a thousand miles east, beyond the Ural mountains.”

   The familiar figure of 1,523 large enterprises includes factories moved **to the Urals**, the Volga region, Siberia, Kazakhstan and Central Asia. They were not all transported beyond the Urals, nor a uniform thousand miles. **Confidence: high.** [Rosstat’s statistical account, with destination totals](https://95.rosstat.gov.ru/storage/mediabank/Rosstat_Tsifry_Pobedy%281%29.pdf).

8. **Factual error — Tanya’s evacuation.**  
   > “She was taken out of the city, across the ice of the lake behind it, and died two years later of the effects of starvation.”

   Tanya was evacuated in **August 1942**, so the ice crossing is wrong. She died in July 1944. This central human example needs the season corrected; naming Lake Ladoga would also make the escape route intelligible. **Confidence: high.** [Saint Petersburg Encyclopaedia](https://encspb.ru/object/2855719790?lc=en), [Soka University’s account of Tanya’s diary](https://www.soka.ac.jp/en/special/berggolts/tanya/).

9. **Doubtful factual detail — siege bread.**  
   > “By November the daily ration for anyone who was not a worker or a soldier was a hundred and twenty-five grams of bread, a slice the size of a pack of cards, and the bread was half sawdust.”

   The minimum ration is broadly right, but “half sawdust” is not a reliable description. Recipes changed and used flour substitutes, oilseed cake and, at points, processed cellulose. Cellulose is not interchangeable with raw sawdust, and the stated proportion needs evidence. **Confidence: high that the wording requires correction; medium on any single replacement recipe because composition varied.** [Museum account of siege-bread recipes](https://www.muzei163.ru/o-muzee/novosti-muzeya/krayushka-xleba-blokadnogo-leningralda.-vyistavka).

10. **Factual overstatement — uninterrupted retreat after Stalingrad.**  
    > “From Stalingrad the German army went backwards, for two years, all the way to Berlin.”

    This turns the eventual trajectory into continuous retreat. Germany still launched the substantial Kursk offensive in July 1943. “The Germans ultimately retreated to Berlin” would preserve the argument without erasing the intervening struggle for the initiative. **Confidence: high.** [Cambridge history of the Battle of Kursk](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-companion-to-the-nazisoviet-war/battle-of-kursk-1943/01BAAC4C9FFD107E1E976ACBFC41C459).

11. **Factual overstatement and break of the absolutes rule — Britain alone.**  
    > “Britain was left alone, and most of the world expected it to make terms.”

    Britain retained the support of the dominions, its empire and forces from occupied Allied countries. Its isolation needs a European strategic qualification. The present wording also sits awkwardly with this chapter’s later acknowledgement of Indian and African troops. **Confidence: high on the correction to “alone.”** [Scholarly study of the “Britain alone” narrative](https://academic.oup.com/ehr/article/136/581/950/6369662).

12. **Factual error and break of the absolutes rule — Pacific islands.**  
    > “American forces advanced across the ocean from island to island, each one defended to the last man.”

    Some garrisons were evacuated: the Japanese withdrew from Kiska before the Allied landing. “Each one” and “the last man” are both untenable; the sentence also conceals the strategy of bypassing selected strongholds. **Confidence: high.** [National Park Service, invasion of Kiska](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/invasion-of-kiska.htm).

13. **Factual imprecision — D-Day’s troop total.**  
    > “Seven thousand ships and boats crossed the Channel, and a hundred and fifty-six thousand men went ashore on the first day.”

    The roughly 156,000 total includes approximately 23,000 airborne troops. They did not all go ashore from the ships just mentioned. Distinguish sea landings from the overall number arriving in Normandy. **Confidence: high.** [National WWII Museum, D-Day research guide](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/students-teachers/student-resources/research-starters/research-starters-d-day).

14. **Chronological ambiguity — the first meeting.**  
    > “It was the first time that the three had met.”

    In a paragraph specifically set at dinner on 30 November, this says their first meeting happened that evening. Their first joint conference session was on 28 November. Tehran was their first meeting **as a trio**, but the birthday dinner was not. **Confidence: high.** [Office of the Historian, Tehran Conference](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1937-1945/tehran-conf).

15. **Reader comprehension — the engine explanation stops before explaining useful motion.**  
    > “A mist of petrol and air is drawn in, squeezed by the piston, and set off by a spark, and the small explosion drives the piston down.”

    Neither “cylinder” nor “piston” is explained, and the reader never learns how the downward movement turns wheels or a propeller, or how the engine clears the burnt gases and repeats. Under the style guide’s “explain it to a friend” test, this needs a short physical description and the missing connection from reciprocating movement to rotation.

16. **Reader comprehension — an apparently sealed city has an unexplained exit.**  
    > “The city of Leningrad, the old capital, was surrounded in September 1941.”

    The passage later takes Tanya out across “the lake behind it” without explaining how that route remained available. A sentence locating Lake Ladoga and explaining the precarious supply and evacuation connection would resolve the apparent contradiction between complete encirclement and escape.

17. **Reader comprehension — the armies on the flanks appear without explanation.**  
    > “On 19 November they attacked the weak armies guarding the German flanks, and in four days the two arms met behind the city.”

    The reader has met only German and Soviet armies in this battle. Whose armies are these, and what made them vulnerable? Identify the Romanian forces and briefly explain the exposed positions or inadequate anti-tank defences. “Flanks” and “the two arms” also need enough spatial explanation to let a novice picture the encirclement.

18. **Style — an unnecessary universal claim.**  
    > “Everything that America made had to cross an ocean, and for three years the most dangerous enemy that the Allies faced was the German submarine.”

    “Everything” includes production consumed in the United States, so the first clause fails the explicit rule against exception-breaking absolutes. The intended subject is American supplies destined for overseas fronts. The submarine’s importance can remain forcefully stated.

19. **Style — explicit signposting.**  
    > “There was also a secret.”

    This announces a revelation instead of giving it. Start with the German radio messages or the work of the Polish mathematicians.

20. **Style — explicit answer signposting.**  
    > “The last part of the answer was American.”

    This labels the argument’s structure rather than moving the story forward. Begin with the American shipyards or the standard cargo ship.
