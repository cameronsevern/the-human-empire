# Chapter 108 — Where does the money go when you tap a card?

**Line under the title:** Kenya, the United States, China, India and Sweden, from 1950 to 2025.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years. Explain each mechanism so that a reader could explain it to a friend.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

When a card is tapped, nothing travels. A message goes out, and numbers are changed in the books of two banks. Most money had been a record in a bank's books for a long time; what is new is that nearly every purchase is now a record as well, kept by a company or a state. It began with a cardboard card for restaurant bills in 1950. Different countries then took different roads: America built private card networks that take a cut of every sale, China leapt straight to the telephone, India built a public system that charges nothing, Kenya's telephone company turned airtime into a bank for people who had never had one, and an unknown programmer tried to make money with no issuer at all. The convenience is real, and it has brought hundreds of millions of people into the money economy. It has been paid for in privacy, and in the power of whoever runs the network to see every purchase and to switch a person off.
