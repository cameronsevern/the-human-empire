1. **Misleading central claim — high confidence.**  
   > “They attacked two great empires that had just fought each other to exhaustion, and they asked very little of the people they conquered.”

   Religious and administrative continuity did not necessarily mean light demands. Taxes, requisitions and compulsory labour could be substantial. The chapter’s own opening illustrates this, while John of Nikiu reports increased taxation and violence alongside protection of churches. The defensible distinction is between preserving local institutions and imposing little on their inhabitants. [Study of the conquest-era fiscal evidence](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rebyz_0766-5598_2013_num_71_1_4989).

2. **Incorrect fiscal explanation — high confidence.**  
   > “Each Christian, Jew or Zoroastrian who converted was a taxpayer lost.”

   Conversion did not eliminate all tax liability. Converts were theoretically exempt from the non-Muslim poll tax, but land taxes remained, Muslim obligations applied, and exemptions were inconsistently enforced. This matters because the sentence supplies the main explanation for official resistance to conversion. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Jezya”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jezya/).

3. **Doubtful account of how the armies formed — high confidence that revision is needed.**  
   > “Tens of thousands of armed men, skilled at fast movement across open country, had lost their occupation.”

   > “The first expeditions into Syria and Iraq were raids of the traditional kind, for livestock and captives, and they were directed outwards because raiding inwards was no longer allowed.”

   These sentences turn an interpretation into an established recruitment history. Early forces included settled townspeople, and former Arabian rebels were initially excluded from the conquest armies, then recruited under Umar. Political organisation and religious aims also mattered. Outward raiding can remain part of the explanation, but the evidence does not establish this simple sequence of mass unemployment followed by redirected raids. [Fred Donner, *The Early Islamic Conquests*, conclusions](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/med/donner.asp).

4. **Misleading origin of Sunnism — high confidence.**  
   > “The majority, who accepted the caliphs as they came, are called Sunnis, after the *sunna*, the established practice of the Prophet and the community.”

   This makes Sunnism sound like the immediate name for acquiescence in Umayyad succession. Sunni identity developed over subsequent centuries through disputes about religious authority, doctrine and tradition; it was not simply the party accepting whichever caliph prevailed. [Patricia Crone, “The Sunnis”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/medieval-islamic-political-thought/sunnis/C37B2E8F6D3391067D41EF3167A6E854).

5. **Incorrectly uniform language reform; internal inconsistency — high confidence.**  
   > “From then on, a clerk who wanted to keep his post had to learn Arabic.”

   The preceding paragraph places this change in the 690s, yet Basilios is receiving official Greek correspondence around 710. Administrative Arabisation proceeded unevenly; Greek continued in Egyptian fiscal documents through the eighth century. Distinguish central policy from its gradual local implementation. [University of Edinburgh, surviving Greek fiscal documents](https://blogs.ed.ac.uk/caliphalfinances/2024/08/01/the-greek-of-egyptian-abbasid-fiscal-documents/).

6. **Overgeneralised surrender terms — high confidence.**  
   > “The terms offered to Egypt were those offered everywhere, and they were simple.”

   > “In return each adult man paid a yearly tax, and the community acknowledged Muslim rule.”

   Agreements and assessments varied by locality, treaty and period. Early tribute arrangements cannot everywhere be reduced to the later standard account of an individual poll tax, and exemptions make “each adult man” inaccurate. “Everywhere” also directly breaks the style rule against fragile absolutes. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Jezya”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jezya/).

7. **Inaccurate summary of scripture — high confidence.**  
   > “The Qur'an itself named Jews and Christians as ‘people of the book’, who had received true scriptures from God and were to be left to follow them.”

   The final clause makes the Qur’an’s position sound unconditional. Verse 9:29 explicitly calls for fighting People of the Book until they pay tribute and submit. Continued religious practice under Muslim rule needs to be explained together with that subordinate status. This is a problem of reporting the text accurately, rather than a clear instance of the narrator endorsing religious belief. [Qur’an 9:29](https://quran.com/9/29).

8. **False claim of complete Arab unity — high confidence.**  
   > “The two empires had each lost a generation of soldiers, and the Arabs, for the first time in their history, were all on the same side.”

   Christian Arab contingents fought on the Byzantine side at Yarmuk. The new state united much of Arabia; it did not unite every Arab behind the conquest. “All” is both historically wrong and a prohibited absolute. [*Encyclopaedia of Islam*, “Yarmūk”](https://ketab3.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/the_encyclopaedia_of_islam_w-z_vol_11_encyclopaedia_of_islam_new_edition__2002.pdf).

9. **Unsupported superlative — high confidence.**  
   > “The Arab conquests moved faster than any before them.”

   Alexander is an obvious counterexample: he invaded the Persian Empire in 334 BC and had taken its principal royal centres by 330. Without defining speed or the territory being compared, this claim cannot stand. “Among the fastest” would preserve the historical point. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Alexander the Great”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/alexander-the-great-356-23-bc/).

10. **False stopping point — high confidence.**  
    > “The military conquests, meanwhile, ran on for a century and then stopped.”

    The first great expansion slowed and its fronts developed differently; conquest did not stop. The invasion of Sicily beginning in 827 is a clear later counterexample. Specify the end of the initial expansion rather than the end of conquest altogether. [University of Konstanz, study of Sicily’s conquest](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/transmed/index.php/tmh/en/article/view/20).

11. **Overstated demographic precision — medium-high confidence.**  
    > “The best estimates suggest that a century after the conquest, fewer than one in ten of the people of Iran were Muslims, and that Muslims became the majority there only after about two hundred and fifty years.”

    Such figures depend heavily on reconstruction from biographical genealogies, especially those of urban populations. They are not measurements of Iran’s entire population, and regional and rural trajectories could differ substantially. The secure conclusion is gradual conversion followed by substantial acceleration under the Abbasids; the numerical thresholds need clearer attribution or removal. [Elton Daniel on Bulliet’s method and its limitations](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/conversion-ii/).

12. **Misleading exclusion of conversion from above — high confidence.**  
    > “Conversion came, therefore, slowly and from below, over many generations.”

    “Therefore” makes fiscal reluctance explain the whole process, while “from below” excludes documented official promotion. Qutayba encouraged conversion in Central Asia, and Abbasid missionary activity also mattered. Gradual conversion is defensible; exclusively popular, unofficial conversion is not. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Conversion”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/conversion-ii/).

13. **Unsupported description of the surviving correspondence — high confidence.**  
    > “The governor's secretaries added an Arabic version for the files.”

    The evidence includes Arabic letters, bilingual documents and Greek letters with short Arabic summaries or dockets. That does not establish a routine Arabic translation retained “for the files.” Describe the attested bilingual practice without inventing its purpose or uniformity. [Tonio Sebastian Richter, “Language Choice in the Qurra Dossier”](https://www.academia.edu/145686433/Language_Choice_in_the_Qurra_Dossier).

14. **Incorrect geographical equivalence — high confidence.**  
    > “The empire of Darius had, for a moment, been put back together.”

    Khosrow’s conquests recovered important former Achaemenid territories, but did not recreate Darius’s empire, which included territories such as Thrace and the Indus valley. The sentence also leaves an unprepared reader unsure which Darius is meant. [Darius’s territories](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/darius-iii/); [Khosrow II’s campaigns](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khosrow-ii/).

15. **Compressed itinerary obscures a consequential event — high confidence.**  
    > “In 680, when that son came to the throne, Ali's younger son Husayn set out from Medina for Iraq, where supporters had promised to rise for him.”

    Husayn first left Medina for refuge in Mecca. He received the Kufan invitations there and subsequently departed from Mecca for Iraq. This was a separate decision after several months, not a direct journey from Medina in response to those promises. [Wilferd Madelung, “Ḥosayn b. ʿAli”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hosayn-b-ali-i/).

16. **Unreconciled presentation across chapters — high confidence about the textual tension.**  
    > “Ali's supporters held that Muhammad had named him as his heir.”

    Chapter 28 states: “He left no son, and he had named no successor.” These are not strictly contradictory propositions—chapter 29 attributes a belief—but chapter 28 has already presented one answer to the disputed succession question as settled fact. The reader needs a consistent distinction between what the competing traditions report and what the narrator establishes. The qualification belongs principally in chapter 28.

17. **Reader loses the reason for the conflict.**  
    > “Nearly all of them were Christians of the Egyptian church, which had rejected the formula about the nature of Christ that the emperor's council had laid down two centuries earlier.”

    Neither preceding chapter explains this dispute. “The formula about the nature of Christ” names the subject without explaining the disagreement. Because religious estrangement carries the explanation of Egyptian attitudes, give one plain sentence about the competing accounts of Christ’s humanity and divinity.

18. **Reader cannot follow the decisive political turn.**  
    > “The armies of Iraq and Syria fought, then agreed to arbitration, and the war dragged on.”

    Arbitration of what, and why did it fail? The following sentence introduces Ali’s killer as belonging to a faction that thought both sides had betrayed God, but never connects that conviction to the arbitration. A short explanation would make the assassination intelligible without adding another collection of names.

19. **Clear signpost sentence.**  
    > “The conquest of Egypt shows the other half of the explanation, which concerns the conquered.”

    This announces the explanatory structure instead of continuing the story. It directly conflicts with “No signpost sentences.” The following sentence about Egypt can begin the section.

I found no clear hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions, or body cross-references. Nor did I find an unequivocal sentence asserting or denying a religious belief in the narrator’s own voice. No files were edited.
