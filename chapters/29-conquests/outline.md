# Chapter 29 — How did the Arabs conquer an empire so quickly?

**Line under the title:** From Spain to Central Asia, from 602 to about 750.
**Length:** 4,400 to 5,000 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

The Arabs came out of the desert just after Rome and Persia had spent twenty-six years ruining each other. They were united for the first time, forbidden to raid one another, and paid in plunder. They won a handful of battles, and what turned those into an empire was how little they asked. A town that surrendered kept its churches, its laws and its officials, and paid a tax that was often lighter than the old one. The conquerors lived apart in camp cities on salaries and left the clerks at their desks; for sixty years the accounts stayed in Greek and Persian and the coins still showed crosses and fire altars. The conquest took a few decades. Becoming Arabic-speaking and Muslim took the conquered lands several centuries, and the conquerors were in no hurry, because converts paid less tax. Meanwhile a quarrel over who should lead the community after Muhammad turned into civil war and left the division between Sunni and Shia.

## The story

1. **Opening: letters to Basilios.** Answer first. About 710, a Christian headman on the Nile gets tax demands from an Arab governor, in Greek. Seventy years after the conquest, the same offices, the same language.
2. **The last great war of antiquity.** 602 to 628: Persia takes Syria, Jerusalem and Egypt; Constantinople besieged; the emperor's counter-march; Persia collapses into palace murder. Plague. Both sides stop paying their Arab frontier guards.
3. **An outlet.** The successor and the tribes that stop paying. Why a peace inside Arabia needed a war outside it. The two battles of 636. The carpet cut into pieces.
4. **Four thousand men.** Egypt. Why a Christian country hardly resisted: the emperor's bishops and the hidden patriarch. The terms of surrender.
5. **Camp cities.** Soldiers on a register, paid from taxes, kept off the land. The clerks stay. Borrowed coins. Sixty years later: Arabic in the offices, a coin with only words, the Dome of the Rock.
6. **The party of Ali.** The succession: four leaders, three murdered. The civil war, the dynasty at Damascus, and Karbala. What Sunni and Shia mean.
7. **Not as a tax collector.** Why the rulers did not want converts. Clients. The slow curve of conversion and the slower one of language. The limits: Constantinople, the Franks, and a river in Central Asia.
