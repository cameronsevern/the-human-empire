# 30. Where do our numbers come from?

*India, Baghdad and Spain, from about the year 500 to about 1200*

They come from India, by way of Baghdad. Indian astronomers worked out how to write any number with nine signs and a mark for nothing. Scholars writing in Arabic took the method up, built on it, and passed it to Europe along with a great deal else.

Try multiplying forty-seven by twenty-nine in Roman numerals. The sum looks like this: XLVII times XXIX. There is no obvious way to begin. The signs cannot be lined up in columns, because a Roman numeral has no columns. X means ten wherever it stands, V means five, and the number is found by adding and subtracting the signs. A Roman who needed the answer did not work it out in writing at all. He pushed pebbles or counters along the grooves of a board, as people had for thousands of years, and wrote down the result at the end.

Now write the same sum as 47 times 29. A child of ten can do it with a pencil, and the reason is an idea so familiar that it is hard to see. In our numbers, a sign's value depends on where it stands. The 4 in 47 means four tens, and the 4 in 4,700 means four thousands, and nobody has to say so. Because of that, a sum can be broken into small steps, column by column, and each step needs only the multiplication table up to nine times nine.

The scheme has one requirement. If position carries meaning, there must be a way to show a position with nothing in it. Four hundred and seven has no tens, and something has to hold the tens place open, or the number collapses into forty-seven. That something is the zero. With it, ten signs will write any number however large, and the writing does the work of the counting board.

The clerks of Babylon had used position in their numbers nearly four thousand years ago, and the Maya of Central America worked out a sign for nothing on their own. The system that the whole world now uses, however, with its ten digits and its zero that can be added and multiplied like any other number, was put together in India.

## Fortunes and debts

Between about 320 and 550, most of northern India was ruled by a dynasty called the Guptas, from the old capital on the Ganges where Ashoka had once reigned. Their empire was looser than his and their age is remembered less for conquest than for what was made in it: poetry and plays in Sanskrit, the old sacred language, along with temples of stone, and mathematics.

Indian mathematics was the servant of astronomy, and astronomy was the servant of the calendar. Priests needed to know the right day for a ritual and astrologers the positions of the planets at a birth, and both needed to predict eclipses. That meant long calculations with very large numbers. Indian thinkers were comfortable with large numbers in any case, since their accounts of the universe dealt in cycles of millions and billions of years.

In 499 a young astronomer named Aryabhata, working in the Gupta capital, finished a short book in Sanskrit verse. It gives methods for finding square roots and cube roots that only make sense if numbers are being written by position, in tens. It also gives a value for the ratio of a circle's edge to its width that is correct to four decimal places, and it states that the stars appear to move because the Earth is turning.

By then, Indian calculators were marking an empty position with a dot, and they had a word for it, *shunya*, which means "empty". The next step was stranger. In 628 another astronomer, Brahmagupta, set out rules for doing arithmetic with the empty place itself. He treated it as a number in its own right, with numbers greater than it and numbers less. He called the greater ones fortunes and the lesser ones debts. A debt taken away from nothing, he wrote, becomes a fortune. A fortune multiplied by a debt is a debt. Nothing added to a number leaves the number unchanged, and a number multiplied by nothing is nothing.

No Greek mathematician had allowed nothing, or less than nothing, to count as a number. To Brahmagupta it was simple bookkeeping. A man with three coins who owes five has a debt of two, and a man who owes exactly what he has is left with nothing.

Most Indian writing of that age was done on palm leaves and birch bark, which rot, so most of the oldest zeros that can be dated are carved in stone. One of them is on the wall of a small temple inside the hill fort of Gwalior, in central India. An inscription there, dated to the year 876, records a gift of land to the temple for a flower garden, and gives the plot's length as 270 measures. It also promises the god fifty garlands a day. The zeros in 270 and 50 are small circles, the same shape as the ones on a modern keyboard.

## Rags

An idea needs something to travel on, and for most of history writing materials were scarce.

The Romans and Greeks had written on papyrus, a sheet made from a marsh plant that was harvested and worked in Egypt. In the early Middle Ages, when papyrus became hard to get, the Mediterranean turned to parchment, which is the scraped and stretched skin of a sheep or calf. Parchment lasts for a thousand years and costs a fortune. A large book could use up the skins of two hundred animals. Under those conditions a library of a few hundred volumes was a wonder, and a scholar might never own a book.

The Chinese had a better answer. By about two thousand years ago, they had learned to make a writing surface from waste. Old rags, worn-out fishing nets and the inner bark of the mulberry tree were soaked and beaten in water until they fell apart into a soup of loose fibres. A worker dipped a flat screen of fine bamboo strips into the vat and lifted it out level. The water drained through, and a thin mat of tangled fibres stayed behind on the screen. Peeled off, pressed and dried, that mat was a sheet of paper. In the year 105 an official at the Han court presented the emperor with an improved method, and within a few centuries paper had replaced the bamboo strip throughout China.

Paper moved west along the caravan road with the silk. By the 700s it was being made in Samarkand, in Central Asia, where the conquering Arabs met it. In the 790s a paper mill was working in Baghdad.

The government of the caliphs took to the new material at once, and for a reason that any bureaucrat will understand. Ink can be scraped off parchment and the text altered. Paper soaks up ink, and a changed figure shows. Before long every office wrote on paper, and the mills that supplied the offices supplied everyone else. The price of a book fell to a fraction of what it had been. A geographer who described Baghdad in the ninth century counted more than a hundred shops of booksellers and stationers in one quarter of the city.

## The round city

Baghdad itself was new. In 750 a revolution that began in eastern Iran had overthrown the caliphs of Damascus, and a new dynasty, the Abbasids, descended from an uncle of the Prophet, took their place. The second Abbasid caliph wanted a capital of his own. In 762 he chose a site on the Tigris, where the river comes closest to the Euphrates, a short ride from the ruins of the old Persian capital. He laid the city out as a perfect circle, more than two kilometres across, with his palace and mosque at the centre.

Within fifty years Baghdad had spilled far beyond the circle and held several hundred thousand people, more than any city in the world outside China. The taxes of an empire that ran from Egypt to the borders of India flowed into it.

The move from Syria to Iraq changed the character of the empire. Damascus had looked towards the Mediterranean and the Arabian desert. Baghdad stood on Persian ground, and the Abbasids took over the Persian idea of what a great king was. In that tradition a king was a patron of learning, and the old kings of Persia were remembered for collecting the books of every nation. The Abbasids were conscious that they ruled Greeks, Persians, Syrians, Egyptians and Indians, and they meant to be the heirs of all of them.

They also had practical needs, and so did their subjects. The court would not start a war or lay a foundation stone without an astrologer, and astrology requires exact astronomy. The faith itself set problems in mathematics. A Muslim prays five times a day at hours fixed by the sun, facing Mecca, and so every mosque from Spain to Samarkand needed someone who could work out the time and the direction. The Qur'an's rules of inheritance divide an estate into fractions among wives, sons, daughters and parents, and applying them to a real family with debts and bequests can be seriously difficult. The caliphs and the rich wanted the best doctors, too. All of this knowledge existed, in Greek, Persian and Sanskrit books, and with paper now cheap it could be put into the language of the empire.

## The translator's price

The work went on for about two hundred years, and by the end of it nearly everything of Greek science, medicine and philosophy that could still be found had been turned into Arabic. That included the geometry of Euclid, the astronomy of Ptolemy, the medicine of Galen, and almost all of Aristotle.

Most of the translators were Christians. The churches of Syria and Iraq had been teaching Greek medicine and logic in their own schools for centuries, in Syriac, a language close to Arabic, and their scholars were the natural bridge. The most celebrated of them was a physician named Hunayn, the son of a pharmacist from a town on the Euphrates. Hunayn left an account of his working life that reads like the memoir of a rare book dealer. To find one treatise of Galen, he says, he searched Iraq, Syria, Palestine and Egypt, and came upon about half of it at last in Damascus. He would collect as many Greek copies of a work as he could, compare them line by line to establish a sound text, and only then translate. He translated according to the sense of a sentence instead of word by word, which earlier translators had not always managed.

The people who paid for this were the caliphs, their ministers, and wealthy private families who competed with one another in sponsoring scholars. One family of three brothers, themselves mathematicians, is recorded as paying a team of three translators five hundred gold coins a month. A later story claims that the caliph paid Hunayn the weight of each finished book in gold. A tale like that is told only where translators are known to be very well paid.

The caliphs also kept a palace library, known as the House of Wisdom, and the most intellectually curious of them, al-Ma'mun, who reigned from 813 to 833, attached astronomers to it. He had them check the figures in the Greek books. On his orders, two teams of surveyors went out onto a flat plain in northern Iraq and walked north and south, measuring the ground with rods and cords, until their instruments showed that they had moved one degree of latitude. From the distance, they calculated the size of the Earth. Their result, about forty thousand kilometres round, is right to within a few per cent.

## Restoring and balancing

One of the scholars who worked under al-Ma'mun came from Khwarazm, an oasis region south of the Aral Sea, in what is now Uzbekistan. He was known as al-Khwarizmi, "the man from Khwarazm". He wrote two short books that between them changed how the world calculates.

The first explained the Indian way of writing numbers. Indian astronomical tables had reached Baghdad half a century earlier, with an embassy from the Indus valley, and the court astronomers had learned the nine signs and the dot from them. Al-Khwarizmi wrote a manual for everyone else. It showed, step by step, how to write numbers by position, what the small circle was for, and how to add, subtract, multiply and divide with them. The Arabs called the empty sign *sifr*, a straight translation of the Sanskrit word. Both "cipher" and "zero" come from it.

The second book had a long title in which one word, *al-jabr*, was destined for fame. It means "restoring", and in ordinary speech it was the word for setting a broken bone. In the preface, al-Khwarizmi says that the caliph encouraged him to write a short work on calculation that would be useful to people in cases of inheritance, lawsuits and trade, and in measuring land and digging canals.

What he produced was a method for finding an unknown quantity. Al-Khwarizmi calls the number being sought "the thing". He writes out, in words, a statement about the thing, an equation, although he has no symbols and no equals sign. Then he applies two operations to tidy it. Restoring means moving a quantity that is being subtracted to the other side, where it is added. Balancing means cancelling equal amounts from both sides.

Take a small case. A man leaves a sum of money such that when a third of it has gone to his widow and two coins to the gravedigger, eight coins remain. In al-Khwarizmi's manner this is written: the thing, less a third of the thing, less two, equals eight. Restoring puts the two coins back on the other side, so that the thing less a third of the thing equals ten. Two thirds of the thing are therefore ten, and the thing is fifteen.

Harder problems, in which the thing is multiplied by itself, need more steps. Al-Khwarizmi shows that every problem of the kind he deals with comes down to one of six standard forms, and for each form he gives a recipe for the answer, with a diagram to prove that it works.

Greek and Indian mathematicians had solved problems of the same kind. The novelty was the attitude. Al-Khwarizmi did not treat each puzzle as a separate feat of cleverness. He classified every equation of a certain type, and showed a routine that would solve any of them without thought. The name of the subject, algebra, is his word. The name for any fixed routine of calculation, algorithm, is his own name, as garbled by Latin translators.

## A dark room

The scholars who wrote in Arabic respected the Greeks enormously, and the best of them tested what the Greeks had said.

How do we see? The question sounds childish, and the Greek authorities disagreed about it. The mathematicians, Euclid and Ptolemy among them, held that the eye sends out rays in straight lines, which touch objects as a blind man's stick touches the pavement. That theory explained the geometry of vision beautifully. The followers of Aristotle held that something passes from the object into the eye, which seemed more sensible and explained none of the geometry.

The man who settled the matter was Ibn al-Haytham, who was born in Basra, in southern Iraq, about 965, and spent most of his life in Cairo. He began with observations that anyone can repeat. Look at the sun, and the eye is hurt. Stare at a bright window and then shut your eyes, and the shape of the window remains. Those are things done to the eye by light. Nothing that the eye sends out could cause them.

He then went into a dark room. He made a small hole in the shutter, and outside he set several lamps in a row. On the wall inside appeared a row of spots of light, one for each lamp, each exactly in line with its lamp and the hole. When he covered one lamp, its spot vanished and the others stayed. The light from each lamp was therefore travelling through the hole in a straight line, without mixing with the light from the others, even though all the beams crossed in the same tiny opening.

From such experiments he built a theory that is correct in its essentials. Light travels in straight lines from every point of a lit object in every direction. Some of it enters the eye, and that is seeing. He kept the mathematicians' geometry and reversed the direction of the rays.

More lasting than the result was the manner of reaching it. Ibn al-Haytham set down, in so many words, a rule that few scholars had ever stated. A person who studies the books of the ancients in search of the truth, he wrote, must make himself the enemy of everything he reads, and attack it from every side. He should also be suspicious of himself while he does so.

## Meat on hooks

The Greeks had left a vast body of medical writing. The cities of the caliphate built the largest institutions yet seen in which to practise it.

From about the year 800, the caliphs and their ministers founded hospitals in Baghdad, and later rulers built them in every large city of the Islamic world. They were paid for by endowments of land and shops, on the same principle as the gift that had kept Indian monks in robes. By the tenth century, a great hospital of this kind was open to anyone and charged nothing. It had separate wards for fevers, for eye diseases, for surgery and for the insane, with a pharmacy and a library. A staff of salaried physicians made their rounds with students in tow. After a patient died of a doctor's mistake in 931, the caliph ordered that nobody should practise in Baghdad without passing an examination, and more than eight hundred physicians presented themselves.

The most famous hospital director was a Persian named al-Razi, who took up medicine in his thirties after a youth spent on music and alchemy. A story told about him says that when he was asked to choose the site for a new hospital in Baghdad, he had pieces of fresh meat hung up in different quarters of the city and picked the place where the meat was slowest to rot.

Al-Razi wrote one of the first clear accounts of how to tell smallpox from measles, which is still accurate. He kept notes on his own cases and compared them with what the books said should happen. When they disagreed, he sided with the cases. He wrote a whole treatise called *Doubts about Galen*. In its preface he calls Galen his master, and explains that reason must all the same be put above any authority, which is what Galen himself had taught.

## Dixit Algorizmi

The Islamic world stretched to the Atlantic, and books written in Baghdad were being read in Spain within a generation. Córdoba, the capital of Muslim Spain, was in the tenth century the largest city in western Europe by a wide margin, and one of its rulers assembled a library whose catalogue alone was said to fill forty-four volumes.

At that time the Christian kingdoms to the north owned hardly any Greek science. The western half of the Roman world had lost its Greek along with its cities, and what its monasteries had preserved was a small shelf of Latin summaries. In 1085 a Christian king captured Toledo, in the middle of Spain, with its libraries and its Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews intact. Scholars from all over Europe made their way there to translate.

The most tireless of them was an Italian called Gerard, from the town of Cremona, who came to Toledo looking for a single book, Ptolemy's astronomy, which he knew existed and could not find in Latin. He learned Arabic, translated it, and then stayed for the rest of his life and translated more than seventy others. Among them were al-Khwarizmi's algebra, Euclid's geometry, and the great medical encyclopaedia of a Persian physician known in Europe as Avicenna, which was still a set text in European medical schools five hundred years later. In this way western Europe received much of its Aristotle and Euclid from Arabic, a language into which they had been put in Baghdad, largely by Christians, at the expense of Muslim caliphs and their ministers.

Al-Khwarizmi's manual on the Indian numbers was translated as well. The Arabic original is lost, and the Latin version begins with the words *Dixit Algorizmi*, "Thus spoke al-Khwarizmi".

The figures themselves reached European merchants by a shorter road. In the 1180s, a boy from Pisa named Leonardo was taken to a port on the coast of Algeria, where his father was the customs official for the Pisan traders. The father had him taught to calculate by a local master. Leonardo was enchanted by the nine Indian figures, as he calls them, and later travelled around the Mediterranean learning everything that merchants knew about reckoning. In 1202 he published a thick book showing how to use the new numbers for pricing, profit, interest and the exchange of currencies. He is now known as Fibonacci.

Not everyone was pleased. In 1299 the guild of money changers in Florence forbade its members to keep their accounts in the new figures. A zero, after all, can be turned into a six or a nine with one stroke of the pen. For another two centuries, cautious Europeans went on writing the totals of their ledgers in Roman numerals, having worked them out on scrap paper in Indian ones.

The route can still be traced in the words. Algebra, algorithm, zero and cipher are Arabic. So are zenith, alkali and alcohol, and so are the names of most of the bright stars in the sky.
