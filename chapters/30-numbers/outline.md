# Chapter 30 — Where do our numbers come from?

**Line under the title:** India, Baghdad and Spain, from about the year 500 to about 1200.
**Length:** 4,400 to 5,000 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

The ten digits were worked out in India, where astronomers under the Gupta kings and their successors learned to write any number with nine signs and a mark for nothing, and then to treat that nothing as a number. They reached the rest of the world through Baghdad. There, for two centuries, a new empire with a great deal of money, a new and cheap writing material from China, and practical needs of its own paid to have the science of the Greeks, Persians and Indians put into Arabic. The scholars who received it did not just store it. They built algebra, showed by experiment how sight works, and invented the hospital. Europe got the numbers, and its own ancient science, back from them in Latin translation, and still uses their words.

## The story

1. **Opening: a sum in Roman numerals.** Answer first. Why XLVII times XXIX cannot be done on paper. What place value does, and why it needs a sign for an empty place.
2. **Fortunes and debts.** India under the Guptas. The astronomer's shorthand. The rules for nothing, written in verse. A temple wall and the number 270.
3. **Rags.** What books were written on, and what that cost. How paper is made. Samarkand, then a mill in Baghdad.
4. **The round city.** The new caliphs and their capital. What they wanted from learning: the stars, the direction of Mecca, medicine, and a claim to be the heirs of every older empire.
5. **The translator's price.** A Christian doctor hunts Greek manuscripts. Who paid, and how much. How a translation was made.
6. **Restoring and balancing.** The man from Khwarazm: a book on the Indian way of reckoning and a book on inheritance sums that founded algebra. An unknown called "the thing".
7. **A dark room.** Do the eyes send out rays? The man who settled it with a hole in a shutter, and what he said about trusting old books.
8. **Meat on hooks.** The hospital: wards, a pharmacy, students, examinations. The doctor who doubted Galen.
9. **Dixit Algorizmi.** Spain and Toledo. A customs officer's son in an Algerian port. The bankers who banned the new figures. The words left behind.
