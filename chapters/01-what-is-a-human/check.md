## 1. Factual flags

1. **“A chimpanzee knows roughly what one clever animal can work out in one lifetime, plus a few tricks picked up from its mother.”**  
   Chimpanzees learn from individuals besides their mothers and maintain community traditions in tool use, food processing and social behaviour. Human cumulative culture is vastly more extensive, but this description substantially understates chimpanzee culture.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

2. **“Put one of us alone in a forest … we will most likely be dead within weeks”; “humans were never clever enough, one at a time, to survive in the wild.”**  
   Survival depends on the environment, the person’s skills and the duration of isolation; solitary survival is possible. The defensible point is that humans depend heavily on culturally acquired knowledge, which an isolated person still possesses—not that individual humans are inherently incapable of surviving outdoors.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

3. **“Nothing about the body explains this. No bone or muscle makes it possible.”**  
   Human anatomy contributes materially to our capabilities: brains, hands capable of precise manipulation, and adaptations for speech and locomotion all matter. Culture cannot be explained by anatomy alone, but ruling out bodily contributions is false.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

4. **“Most animals cool down by panting, and they cannot pant and gallop at once.”**  
   This is too broad even as a comparison with other mammals: cooling mechanisms vary, and some mammals, notably horses, sweat extensively. Galloping constrains breathing and can limit respiratory cooling; it does not support a universal prohibition on panting while galloping.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

5. **“Walking upright had narrowed the hips.”**  
   Bipedalism reshaped the pelvis; the human pelvis is not simply a narrowed ape pelvis. The difficulty of childbirth involves the dimensions and shape of the birth canal relative to the baby, and the account that locomotion forced narrowing and therefore early birth is not an established complete explanation.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

6. **“Grandmothers matter most of all.”**  
   Grandmothers can make substantial contributions to children’s nutrition and survival, including among the Hadza. The evidence does not establish that they universally matter more than fathers, siblings or other helpers; their relative importance varies across societies and circumstances.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

7. **“Humans did not start depending on each other as hunters or toolmakers. We started as infants.”**  
   Social living and dependence on other group members predate the human lineage. Extensive infant care by multiple helpers may have strengthened human cooperation, but it cannot be presented as the known starting point of mutual dependence or group living.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

8. **“A stone flake … has to be made … by someone who can see the finished shape before the first blow. And it has to be taught.”**  
   Producing a useful sharp flake does not necessarily require visualising a finished shape, and basic flaking can be discovered individually. Teaching can improve skill and transmit techniques, but neither explicit teaching nor this degree of advance planning is a necessary condition for making a flake.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

9. **“By 400,000 years ago hearths are common wherever people lived.”**  
   Evidence for habitual fire use becomes substantially stronger around this period in some regions. It remains geographically uneven, so “wherever people lived” falsely implies a securely established worldwide pattern.  
   **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

10. **“It is a real vocabulary, and it has three words.”**  
    The three familiar vervet alarm categories are not the animal’s entire vocal repertoire. Their calls also cannot simply be equated with three human words; the example illustrates differentiated alarm communication, not a complete three-word language.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

11. **“By … about 300,000 years ago … [a spear point] is fixed on with glue, cooked from plant gum and mixed with powdered red earth … and then bound with sinew.”**  
    This places a very specific composite weapon at a date when that complete construction is not established. Stone-point hafting has early evidence, but the well-known evidence for compound plant-gum-and-ochre adhesives is much later, around 70,000 years ago; the full recipe and sinew binding should not be asserted for 300,000 years ago.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

12. **“Nobody thought of all of them. Nobody could have.”**  
    It is plausible that this technology accumulated through many people’s contributions, but it is not impossible for one person to devise several components of a composite tool. The chapter turns a persuasive account of cumulative invention into an unsupported claim about individual cognitive limits.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

13. **“Raw nardoo contains a poison that destroys a vitamin … The Yandruwandha knew a way of washing and baking it that made it safe.”**  
    Nardoo contains thiaminase, an enzyme that destroys vitamin B1, and thiamine deficiency is a proposed contributor to the explorers’ deaths. Its precise contribution, alongside starvation and other factors, and the effectiveness of particular preparation methods are not settled sufficiently to present this as a demonstrated cause-and-remedy story.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

14. **“Everyone has known everyone else for most or all of their lives”; “That is the whole human world”; “built to live among thirty people it had known all its life.”**  
    Mobile forager camps often have changing membership and relationships extending well beyond the residential group through marriage, visits and exchange. The hypothetical camp can illustrate one possible arrangement, but the repeated claim that humans evolved in essentially fixed lifelong groups of thirty misrepresents that social flexibility.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

15. **“Nothing has been put by, because nothing keeps.”**  
    Many foods keep naturally or can be preserved through drying, smoking or other processing, and hunter-gatherers have used storage. Mobility can limit storage, and a particular camp might have none, but universal perishability is a false explanation.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

16. **“Bodies and instincts change slowly, over thousands of generations.”**  
    Evolutionary change does not invariably require thousands of generations. Strong selection can produce consequential changes within hundreds of generations or fewer, including during the period since agriculture; the passage overstates how completely modern humans retain an unchanged ancestral biological configuration.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

17. **“We fear what killed our ancestors”; “Both halves [of status behaviour] come from the camp.”**  
    These assert specific evolutionary explanations as established causes. Ancestral selection plausibly influenced fear, affiliation and status behaviour, but their present forms also involve learning and cultural conditions, and the particular causal stories offered here are not demonstrated. The later warning about speculative explanations does not undo these categorical assertions.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: major.**

18. **“Within minutes they favour their own side and think less of the other.”**  
    Arbitrary group assignment can produce favouritism toward one’s own group. It does not necessarily produce a worse opinion of the other group: favouring insiders and derogating outsiders are distinct outcomes.  
    **Confidence: high. Severity: minor.**

## 2. Clarity flags

- **“Some seven million years ago … there lived an ape that was the ancestor of both of us.”**  
  A novice may picture one identifiable individual rather than an ancestral population. “An ancestral ape population from which both lineages descended” would make the intended relationship clearer.

- **“But the walkers were not people”; “their kind”; “our own species.”**  
  The chapter never clearly establishes how it uses *human*, *people*, *ancestor* and *our species*. Naming Lucy’s species and briefly distinguishing it from *Homo sapiens* would help readers follow who belongs where.

- **“Over the next two million years the brain roughly tripled in size.”**  
  The comparison lacks explicit endpoints. Readers may not know whether this compares Lucy with living humans, early members of *Homo* with living humans, or some other pair.

- **“A young chimpanzee is finding most of its own food by the time it is weaned.”**  
  “Weaned” needs a brief explanation for readers unfamiliar with the term: no longer nursing.

- **“The flake and the hearth had grown into a kit.”**  
  The metaphor briefly obscures the point that people possessed a collection of technologies and practical skills. “People had accumulated a range of tools and techniques” would be more immediately clear.

- **“Bound with sinew.”**  
  Explain sinew as animal tendon used as a strong binding material.

- **“Then show them how someone else solves a problem, and the child leaves the chimpanzee far behind.”**  
  “Leaves … far behind” does not identify what is being measured: copying actions, learning a solution, or solving the original puzzles. The transition also makes separate kinds of experiment sound like consecutive stages of one test.

- **“That makes no sense for a single deal.”**  
  Specify that rejecting the offer does not maximise the recipient’s immediate monetary gain. As written, “no sense” is confusing because the preceding sentence has already supplied an understandable motive: refusing unfair treatment.

## 3. Overall

The chapter’s broad chronology is serviceable, but several categorical claims exceed what the evidence supports.  
The most consequential problems concern chimpanzee culture, the origins of cooperation, the spear reconstruction at 300,000 years ago, and the explanation of Burke and Wills’s deaths.  
Its treatment of foragers acknowledges their diversity, yet repeatedly makes a fixed camp of thirty people stand for humanity’s ancestral social world.  
The prose is generally easy to follow, although undefined categories and abrupt transitions between experiments can leave beginners with mistaken connections.
