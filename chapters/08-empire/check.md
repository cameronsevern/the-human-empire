## Factual flags

1. **Major — Governors were not uniformly outsiders.**  
   > “They were ‘sons of Akkad’, which is to say men of his own city.”

   “Sons of Akkad” designated royal followers and beneficiaries, including people of southern origin. Moreover, Meskigala of Adab remained governor across Sargon’s conquest. The accompanying claim that governors necessarily “had no roots in the place” is therefore false. **Correct fact:** Sargon established provincial government through loyal appointees, while also retaining or incorporating local elites. [Ingo Schrakamp, *The Kingdom of Akkad: A View from Within*, pp. 622–23, 631–32](https://www.academia.edu/116031575/The_Kingdom_of_Akkad_A_View_from_Within).

2. **Major — Defeating Uruk did not replace separate city campaigns.**  
   > “So when Sargon marched south, he did not have to conquer a dozen cities separately. He had to beat one man who had already conquered them.”

   Sargon’s inscriptions describe additional conquests of Ur, Eninmar, Lagash and Umma, including destruction of their walls. The chapter itself subsequently mentions thirty-four battles. **Correct fact:** defeating Lugalzagesi removed the principal rival, but further campaigns were needed to subdue the south. [Schrakamp, pp. 620–21](https://www.academia.edu/116031575/The_Kingdom_of_Akkad_A_View_from_Within).

3. **Minor — The agricultural calendar is wrong.**  
   > “It existed for a few weeks in the summer, and then it went home for the harvest.”

   This places the main grain harvest after summer campaigning. **Correct fact:** southern Mesopotamian barley was normally harvested in April–May, with wheat following into early summer. Seasonal military service must be described around that calendar. [Riehl and colleagues, “Changing growing conditions for crops during the Near Eastern Bronze Age”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440307001458).

4. **Minor — Naram-Sin’s great rebellion is misplaced.**  
   > “Early in his reign, almost the entire empire rebelled at once.”

   **Correct fact:** the Great Revolt is placed around the middle of his reign in the specialist chronology, rather than at its beginning. This matters because the passage connects it to the vulnerability of new rulers. [Schrakamp, pp. 632–33, especially note 82](https://www.academia.edu/116031575/The_Kingdom_of_Akkad_A_View_from_Within).

5. **Major — The northern abandonment is made universal.**  
   > “About four thousand two hundred years ago, those towns were abandoned.”

   The preceding sentence makes “those towns” encompass Akkad’s northern fortified centres. Their histories differed: Tell Leilan was abandoned, but Tell Brak continued at reduced size, and Tell Hamoukar flourished. **Correct fact:** some northern settlements were abandoned while others contracted or continued. This correction does not require abandoning the chapter’s drought interpretation. [“Much ado about nothing,” *Antiquity*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/much-ado-about-nothing-assessing-the-impact-of-the-42-kya-event-on-human-subsistence-patterns-in-northern-mesopotamia-using-stable-isotope-analysis/4F4BF5789FABE111AD46903F8D2E3FE7).

6. **Major — The income explanation contradicts both evidence and the chapter.**  
   > “Once it stopped growing, the cost remained and the income did not.”

   Earlier, the chapter correctly says conquered farmers supported the army. That recurring income did not require continuous territorial expansion. **Correct fact:** royal estates and taxation provided continuing resources; ending conquest removed new booty, not all income. [Schrakamp, pp. 632, 667](https://www.academia.edu/116031575/The_Kingdom_of_Akkad_A_View_from_Within).

7. **Major — The claim that nothing survived Sargon contradicts the institutional argument.**  
   > “Because the empire was not held together by anything that survived the man.”

   The chapter has just described governors, soldiers, accounting practices and a priestess whose tenure extended into his grandson’s reign. **Correct fact:** those institutions survived; succession exposed their inability to prevent widespread revolt. Personal loyalty can remain central to the interpretation without erasing the institutions already described. Source: the chapter’s own “Keeping it” and “The priestess” sections.

8. **Major — The conclusion contradicts the earlier account of Sargon’s predecessor.**  
   > “Before him, a king’s ambition ran as far as his own city and perhaps a victory or two over the neighbours.”

   Earlier, Lugalzagesi conquers the southern cities and claims dominion between two seas. **Correct fact:** extensive territorial ambitions preceded Sargon; his achievement concerned their implementation and durability. Lugalzagesi’s royal inscription explicitly expresses that wider ambition. [“Royal Inscriptions of Lugalzagesi and Sargon”](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/orient/40/0/40_3/_pdf/-char/en).

## Clarity flags

- **An unstable definition:** “A kingdom is one people under one king.” The next sentences distinguish an empire through cities, gods and memories. A general reader needs to know whether “peoples” means language groups or separate city communities; otherwise the distinction shifts underneath the argument.

- **An unnamed recurring character:** “He was the king whose army had burned the temples of Lagash and put an end to its reforming ruler.” Without prior knowledge, both figures are obscure. Lugalzagesi recurs sufficiently often to qualify for a name under the style sheet’s own exception.

- **An unexplained administrative practice:** “Each city had … its own way of naming the years.” Explain that years could be identified by memorable royal events. Otherwise readers may picture different calendar systems or numerical year counts.

- **Chronological ambiguity:** “The empire did not outlast his son” and “Akkad lasted about a century and a half” sit beside an opening span of roughly two centuries. Distinguish the collapse of extensive imperial control from the survival of a reduced Akkadian kingdom. The king list continues after the four rivals with Dudu and Shu-Durul. [Oxford’s translation, lines 266–296](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.2.1.1).

- **Explicit signposting:** “That brings us to the great weakness of what Sargon had made…” and “Underneath all three causes lay a weakness in the thing itself, and it is easy to state.” Both announce an explanation rather than give it, contrary to “No signpost sentences.”

- **Repeated explanatory structure and wording:** “The first problem… The second problem… The third problem…” is followed later by “The first… The second… The third…”. Together they make the narrative resemble the point-by-point case the style sheet discourages. Likewise, “They had been waiting” and “Every one of them was still waiting” repeat the same explanation without advancing it.

## Overall

The chapter is vivid and accessible, and its confident interpretation can stand. The main revisions needed concern provincial government, the economics of collapse, and contradictions between the institutional story and its sweeping conclusions. No files were edited.
