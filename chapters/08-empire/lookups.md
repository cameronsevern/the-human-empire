# Chapter 8 lookups

Verdict key: Confirmed / Wrong / Overstated / Disputed / Could not verify. Sources are mainly the ETCSL (Oxford), the CDLI-hosted RIME texts, Louvre-linked/French Ministry of Culture pages, and Wikipedia articles that cite the Assyriological literature (Frayne RIME 2; Sallaberger and Schrakamp; Black; Weiss; Cullen). Primary Penn Museum, Britannica, Smarthistory and Louvre collection pages returned 403/404 or the wrong object, so scholarly detail is taken from ETCSL and CDLI plus Wikipedia's cited summaries.

1. "Sharru-kin means 'the king is the true one'; a throne name; birth name unknown; the name of someone who is not the real king" - OVERSTATED.
   Sharru-ken/Sharru-kin is usually rendered "legitimate (true) king". Wikipedia (Sargon of Akkad): "some scholars think it was a throne name meant to make people forget he was a usurper"; others argue it was his birth name (perhaps "the King is just"). His birth name is not recorded. The usurper reading is one view, not settled. Chapter paragraph 9 ("The only person who needs to call himself True King is one who is not") states the usurper reading as fact.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad
   Replacement for lines 7-9: "We do not know that man's real name. The name we know him by is Sharru-kin, 'the legitimate king', and many scholars think he took it when he seized power. A man born the son of a king has no need to announce that he is the real thing. The only person who needs to call himself True King is one who has no royal blood to point to."

2. "Akkad is the first empire; c. 4,350 to 4,150 years ago; lasted about a century and a half" - CONFIRMED for "first empire" and dates; OVERSTATED for the duration.
   Wikipedia (Akkadian Empire): "often seen as the first empire in world history"; dated c. 2334-2154 BC (middle chronology), i.e. 180 years. Header "about 4,350 to about 4,150 years ago" matches. "A century and a half" is short.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire
   Replacement for line 121: "So Akkad lasted a little under two centuries from Sargon's rise, and for much of that time it was fighting its own subjects."

3. Sargon birth legend (priestess mother forbidden a child; reed basket sealed with bitumen; Euphrates; Akki the water-drawer; gardener; "written down long after") - CONFIRMED.
   Neo-Assyrian autobiographical text; tablets from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh (found 1867), 7th century BC, i.e. about 1,600 years after Sargon. Mother is an en-priestess who conceives and bears him in secret, sets him in a basket of rushes sealed with bitumen on the river; Akki the water-drawer lifts him out, raises him, makes him a gardener. The text may have been composed earlier than the surviving copies; the copies are 7th century BC. Chapter says "written down long after his death": true; "two thousand years" of stories: fine.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad ; https://www.worldhistory.org/article/746/the-legend-of-sargon-of-akkad/ (search summary)
   Optional precision: "The best known was written down about sixteen hundred years after his death."

4. "Cupbearer to the king at Kish; how he took power is not recorded" - CONFIRMED.
   Sumerian King List (ETCSL t.2.1.1, lines 266-296): "Sargon, whose father was a gardener, the cupbearer of Ur-Zababa, became king, the king of Agade, who built Agade; he ruled 56 years" (mss 55, 54). The Sumerian Sargon Legend (Nippur, found 1974, ed. 1983) has Ur-Zababa make him cupbearer and then send him to Lugalzagesi with a tablet asking for his death; the text breaks off. The Weidner Chronicle also calls him Ur-Zababa's cupbearer. No source records how he took the throne.
   https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.2.1.1&display=Crit&charenc=gcirc ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ur-Zababa
   Note: the King List says his father was a gardener, which is relevant to the "not the son of anyone important" line (fine).

5. "He then built a new city of his own to rule from, a little way off, and called it Akkad" - OVERSTATED.
   The King List says "who built Agade" (one ms: "under whom Agade was built"). But Akkad already existed: a year name of En-shakushanna of Uruk (c. 2350 BC) records that he "defeated Akkad", and the non-Akkadian form of the name suggests earlier settlement. Wikipedia: later tradition says Sargon founded Akkad, but it was already a city of little earlier importance.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkad_(city) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad
   Replacement for lines 25: "How he went from the king's elbow to the king's seat is not recorded. One way or another, he took power in Kish, and gave himself his new name. He then made a smaller town a little way off, Akkad, his capital and built it up, and later tradition said he had founded it."

6. Lugalzagesi (burned the temples of Lagash and ended its reforming ruler; defeated the southern cities; "sea in the south to the sea in the west") - CONFIRMED (with one overstatement).
   Wikipedia: king of Umma then Uruk, c. 2358-2334; beat Lagash; annexed much of its territory; his Nippur vase inscription says Enlil gave him "all the lands between the upper and the lower seas" (Lower Sea = Gulf, Upper Sea = Mediterranean); scholars debate how far his reach actually extended. The Lagash lament condemns him (the ruler of Umma) for sacking Lagash's temples; Urukagina's reign ended in that conflict. "defeat every other city in the south" is Lugalzagesi's boast and the debated extent. 
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugalzagesi
   Replacement for the sentence on the boast: "he boasted that Enlil had given him all the lands from the lower sea to the upper sea." ("Sea in the south to the sea in the west" is fine as a gloss.) Suggest: "he went on to bring the south under his rule, and he claimed the whole land from the lower sea to the upper sea."

7. "Sargon defeated the king of Uruk, tore down walls, led him in a neck-stock to the temple of the chief god at Nippur" - CONFIRMED.
   Wikipedia: "three battles"; Uruk's walls razed; Lugalzagesi taken to Nippur's Enlil sanctuary in a neck stock. These are known from Old Babylonian copies (found at Nippur) of Sargon's inscriptions. "His own inscriptions say" is fine; "as copied by later scribes" is more exact. "gate of Enlil" not checked in text.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad

8. "Sargon claimed thirty-four battles; washed his weapons in the sea" - CONFIRMED.
   RIME 2.01.01.11 (CDLI): "Sargon, king of the world, was victorious in 34 battles" and destroyed city walls as far as the sea. Several RIME 2.01.01 texts have him wash his weapons in the (Lower) Sea. Wikipedia: inscriptions claim he washed his weapons in the Lower Sea (Gulf).
   https://cdli.earth/artifacts/461937 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad

9. "Sons of Akkad" held governorships; walls of conquered cities torn down - CONFIRMED with nuance.
   Wikipedia: Sargon appointed "sons of Akkad" as governors; historians debate whether this means Akkadian-born officials or loyal men of any origin. RIME 2.01.01.11 says he destroyed the city walls as far as the sea.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad
   Replacement for line 45: "They were 'sons of Akkad', which scholars read as men of his own city or men loyal to it."

10. "5,400 men ate bread daily before him"; "first standing army" - CONFIRMED for the number; OVERSTATED for "first standing army".
   RIME 2.01.01.11 (CDLI): "5,400 men, daily, before him, he let eat bread". Wikipedia reads this as likely a professional royal force and notes most army details come from his successors. "As far as we know, it is the first standing army" is a common claim but not a settled one.
   https://cdli.earth/artifacts/461937 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad
   Replacement for line 51: "One of his inscriptions says, with evident pride, that 5,400 men ate bread before him every day. Most scholars take them to be a permanent royal force, the earliest one we have a record of."

11. Standardised weights/measures and year names; Akkadian in administration alongside Sumerian - OVERSTATED as to Sargon; CONFIRMED in general.
   Wikipedia (Akkadian Empire) credits the empire with metrological reform and uniform royal standards. A course handout (McMahon, Bryn Mawr) says most of these innovations (standard weights and measures, year-date formulae, new accounting) were not introduced or did not become widespread until Naram-Sin's reign; the EBSCO overview credits Sargon, adding it may be successors' work. Only four year names survive for Sargon. Akkadian was written before the empire (e.g. at Mari) but its use for royal inscriptions and administration spread under Sargon's dynasty, alongside Sumerian. "Each city had its own way of naming the years" is not checked.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire ; https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/akkadians
   Replacement for line 55: "Under the kings of Akkad, and above all under Naram-Sin, royal standard measures and weights spread across the empire, and the clerks dated documents by named years. They also wrote more and more in Akkadian, the language of the conquerors, alongside the older language of the southern cities."  (If the year-name claim is kept, say "dated documents by royal year names" without "single way".)

12. Ships from Meluhha, Magan and Dilmun moored at the quay of Akkad - CONFIRMED with caveat.
   Wikipedia (Akkad city; Sargon): an Old Babylonian copy of a Sargon inscription says ships of Meluhha (Indus), Magan (Oman) and Dilmun (Bahrain) moored at the quay of Agade; a Manishtushu inscription also has ships crossing the Lower Sea and mooring at Agade's quay. Historians say these lands were not conquered. "He boasts" is fine; the text survives in later copy.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkad_(city) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad

13. Sumerian and Akkadian different languages; Inanna = Ishtar - CONFIRMED.
   Sumerian (a language isolate) and Akkadian (East Semitic) are unrelated; Inanna is the Sumerian name, Ishtar the Akkadian. Scholars generally hold that the two goddesses were originally separate and merged under Sargon's dynasty, with Enheduanna's work central to the merger. ETCSL texts use "Inana".
   https://www.worldhistory.org/Inanna/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inanna (search summary)
   Note: the chapter's "different language" is correct; "different names" correct.

14. Enheduanna - MIXED.
   a. Sargon's daughter, en-priestess of Nanna at Ur, god's wife: Confirmed (Wikipedia: appointed by her father or possibly a brother; Brown Univ. Boomer 2008: began in Sargon's reign, continued through her brothers' reigns, ended in nephew Naram-Sin's).
   b. "held the office for about forty years": OVERSTATED / could not verify. No source gives her tenure; popular sources say "over 40 years" by inference from reign lengths (Sargon 56 + sons 24 + part of Naram-Sin); Wikipedia states no length.
   c. Lugal-ane rebellion, expelled, restored: Confirmed in outline. Wikipedia: Lugal-ane seized Ur and demanded she confirm him; she refused, was expelled (probably to Girsu), composed the Exaltation of Inanna, and "probably" returned after Naram-Sin crushed the rebellion; the poem itself credits Inanna with restoring her. The identification of the revolt with Naram-Sin's Great Revolt is a scholarly reconstruction. "wander in the wilderness" is from the poem.
   d. Temple Hymns: DISPUTED. Colophons name her as compiler of the 42 Temple Hymns, but Black argues at most she wrote the final hymn.
   e. First author known by name: Confirmed ("earliest known named author"; Weigle 1978: "the first known author in world literature").
   f. Copied about 500 years later: Confirmed/slightly low. Surviving manuscripts are Old Babylonian copies made more than five centuries (Black: at least six centuries) after her death.
   g. Scholarly doubt: Civil suggested "Enheduanna" might be an office; Black doubted her authorship; Hallo, Westenholz and Zgoll accept it.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enheduanna ; https://archaeology.brown.edu/sites/default/files/papers/Boomer2008.pdf
   Replacements: line 67: "She held the office for decades, through her father's reign and into her nephew's." Line 65-69: "From that position she did something no governor could have done. She wrote hymns, or hymns were written in her name." (keep "wrote": most scholars accept authorship of the Exaltation of Inanna.) Line 71: "Clerks in the schools of the plain were still copying them out more than five hundred years later." Optionally limit to hymns to Inanna rather than "the temple hymns".

15. "He ruled for more than fifty years; at his death the cities rose in revolt" - OVERSTATED (length); CONFIRMED (revolt, with nuance).
   Sumerian King List: 56 years (mss 55, 54; other copies 40; Wikipedia says later sources range from 34 to 56). Modern dates c. 2334-2279 = 55 years. Rimush's inscriptions record revolts against his rule on his accession; but the Chronicle of Early Kings (ABC 20) puts a general revolt and siege of Akkad in Sargon's old age ("In his old age, all the lands revolted against him, and they besieged him in Akkad"). So revolt began before he died.
   https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.2.1.1 ; https://livius.org/sources/content/mesopotamian-chronicles-content/abc-20-chronicle-of-early-kings ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rimush
   Replacement for lines 79: "The King List gives him a reign of about fifty-five years. Late in his life the cities rose against him, and when he died they rose again, nearly all together."

16. Rimush: crushed the revolt; inscriptions count tens of thousands killed and captured; captives sent to labour; nine years; later tradition says courtiers killed him; Manishtushu likewise - CONFIRMED with two gaps.
   Wikipedia (Rimush): c. 2279-2270 BC; King List nine years (some copies 7 or 15); reconquered Ur, Umma, Adab, Lagash, Der, KI.AN, Kazallu; inscriptions claim about 56,041 killed and about 29,438 captured/enslaved (plus about 25,685 "expelled"). A first-millennium omen text says his courtiers killed him "with their cylinder seals". Manishtushu (about 15 years) "may also have been assassinated": that rests on later tradition (omen literature), not documented here. "Captives marched away to labour": not in the sources found (inscriptions record "captured"); a dedication of 360 slaves to Enlil is attested.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rimush ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire
   Replacement for line 83 and 85: "His inscriptions count the dead and the captives city by city: about fifty-six thousand killed and almost thirty thousand captured. It bought him nine years. Then, according to a later omen text, his courtiers killed him with their cylinder seals. His brother Manishtushu, who followed him, is said by the same later tradition to have met the same end."  (Remove "Thousands more were marched away to labour." and "a plain that held perhaps a million people".)

17. "A plain that held perhaps a million people" - COULD NOT VERIFY.
   No source gave a population for southern Mesopotamia in the Akkadian period. Adams (Heartland of Cities, 1981) and Marchetti et al. 2024 (JAR, ~5,000 sites) model settlement and population, but no total was retrievable.
   https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10814-024-09197-3
   Replacement: delete the phrase. "On a plain of small cities, that was a slaughter." or just: "In his own count, the dead alone came to about fifty-six thousand."

18. Naram-Sin: Great Revolt; nine battles in a year; citizens ask gods to make him god of Akkad; divine determinative; first deified in lifetime; first to use "King of the Four Quarters" - CONFIRMED.
   Wikipedia (Naram-Sin; Bassetki Statue; Akkadian Empire): coalition led by Iphur-Kish of Kish and Amar-girid of Uruk; Bassetki inscription: after he defeated the revolt "the people of Akkad asked the gods to make him god of their city" and built a temple to him; victorious in nine battles in one year; he put the divine determinative before his name; "first Mesopotamian king known to have claimed divinity during life"; first definitive claim of "King of the Four Quarters". Sources conflict only on whether Sargon used the title; reference works credit Naram-Sin. Chapter: "cities in the north chose one rebel king and cities in the south another" matches Kish (north) and Uruk (south). "captured the rebel kings": plausible, inscriptions say he captured them. 
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naram-Sin_of_Akkad ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bassetki_Statue ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire

19. Victory Stele - MOSTLY CONFIRMED; bow Could not verify.
   About 2 m tall (original up to about 3 m) pinkish limestone; horned helmet (Louvre-linked description: conical helmet with horns, symbol traditionally associated with the gods); climbs a mountain over the Lullubi (king Satuni begs for mercy); found at Susa by de Morgan in 1898; taken to Susa by the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte in the 12th century BC, who had his own inscription added; now in the Louvre. Time gap: about a thousand years (stele c. 2250 BC, taken in the 12th century BC). Wikipedia says he holds an arrow; I could not confirm a bow in any retrieved description. Chapter's "twice the size of anyone else" and "one foot on their bodies" not confirmed. "Taller than a man": 2 m is about right.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victory_Stele_of_Naram-Sin ; https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/jacques-morgan/en/media/view/6478
   Replacement for line 95: "Naram-Sin climbs the slope at the top, far larger than anyone else, armed and with his weapons in hand. On his head is a helmet with horns..." Or simply: "Naram-Sin strides up the slope at the top, larger than anyone else, in a helmet with horns, and on the plain horns were worn only by gods." Line 133: "A thousand years after it was made, a king from the east, Shutruk-Nahhunte of Elam, raided the plain, saw the stone at Sippar, and carried it home to Susa... He had his own name carved on it beside the god-king's." (Stone was at Sippar; "raided the plain" fine.)

20. Extent: Gulf to Anatolia/Mediterranean - CONFIRMED.
   Wikipedia: under Naram-Sin the empire reached the Zagros, Anatolia and the Mediterranean region; about 800,000 km2 around 2250 BC, with influence from the Mediterranean to Iran and Anatolia to the Gulf. "To the mountains of Turkey and the edge of the Mediterranean" fine; say "its reach" since some of this was raiding/influence (Wikipedia notes historians say distant lands were not permanently conquered).
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naram-Sin_of_Akkad

21. Sumerian King List: "Who was king? Who was not king?"; four kings in three years; after Shar-kali-sharri - CONFIRMED.
   ETCSL t.2.1.1: "Then who was the king? Who was not the king?" followed by Irgigi, Imi, Nanum, Ilulu; "the four of them ruled for only three years." Shar-kali-sharri (Naram-Sin's son) is the last named king before the passage. Note the chapter calls the list "a list of kings ... each king of Akkad" which is fine; it is the King List, written by Old Babylonian scribes (not "in later years" strictly, but fine). The text says "four of them"; the chapter says "claimed the throne within three years": accurate.
   https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.2.1.1&display=Crit&charenc=gcirc

22. Gutians from the Zagros ruled parts of the plain - CONFIRMED.
   Wikipedia (Gutian people): homeland in the Zagros between the mountains and the Tigris; helped bring down Akkad; held hegemony over Sumer under the Gutian dynasty (c. 2141-2050 BC per Sumerian King List, capital at Adab) until Utu-hengal of Uruk expelled them c. 2050 BC. Modern historians view the Mesopotamian blame on the Gutians with some skepticism.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gutian_people ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire
   Replacement for line 109 (optional): "As the empire weakened, their raids turned into invasion, and later Gutian rulers held parts of the plain themselves for several generations."

23. Northern Mesopotamia rain-fed granary; fortified towns; abandoned about 4,200 years ago; dust layer (Weiss et al. 1993); Gulf of Oman core (Cullen et al. 2000); scholarly disagreement - OVERSTATED as written ("those towns were abandoned").
   Weiss et al. 1993 (Science 261:995-1004): Habur Plains rain-fed agriculture civilization; Tell Leilan abandoned after the Akkadian-era occupation and covered with about a metre of windblown silt, interpreted as abrupt aridification (and shamal winds) after a volcanic eruption; they link it to regional desertion and the Akkadian collapse. Cullen et al. 2000 (Geology): Gulf of Oman core M5-422 shows a dolomite/carbonate dust spike at about 4,200 years ago, which they tie to Tell Leilan by dating and tephra. Critique: Tell Hamoukar continued to flourish through the supposed drought; Soltysiak and Fernandes 2021 (Antiquity) find the isotope and plant data show continuity; Peltenburg says desertion of many sites need not mean depopulation. So Tell Leilan was abandoned (and some other sites); not "those towns" generally. I could not verify "second granary" or "fortified towns" in a scholarly source retrieved here; Tell Leilan had an Akkadian administrative building; Tell Brak a Naram-Sin palace.
   https://leilan.yale.edu/sites/default/files/publications/article-specific/cullen2000_0.pdf ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/much-ado-about-nothing-assessing-the-impact-of-the-42-kya-event-on-human-subsistence-patterns-in-northern-mesopotamia-using-stable-isotope-analysis/4F4BF5789FABE111AD46903F8D2E3FE7 ; https://www.discovermagazine.com/empires-in-the-dust-14474
   Replacement for line 111: "The northern part of the empire, in what is now Syria, was different from the south. Its fields were watered by rain, not by canals, and Akkad held it through administrative centres such as Tell Leilan and Tell Brak. About four thousand two hundred years ago the great city at Tell Leilan was abandoned. In the soil above its last floors, archaeologists found a metre of windblown dust with no trace of occupation in it. Mud from the sea floor of the Gulf of Oman shows a great surge of dust at the same date, blown from a land that had dried out."  Line 113: keep "Scholars disagree" (it is correct and sourced).

24. Curse of Akkad - CONFIRMED (date: late 3rd millennium debated; copies Old Babylonian).
   ETCSL t.2.1.5: Naram-Suen, after a dream, two failed omens, destroys Enlil's Ekur with axes; Enlil brings the Gutians from the mountains; famine ("Fields, canals and orchards yield nothing, and the clouds do not rain"); Inana abandons the city; the gods curse Agade. Wikipedia: portrays Naram-Sin negatively, perhaps reflecting southern resentment. Date: composed probably in the Ur III period (c. 2100-2000 BC; often placed about 2100 BC) though earliest copies are Old Babylonian (c. 1900-1700 BC). I could not retrieve a source stating the composition date; treat "not long afterwards" cautiously. The ETCSL text says "no fish" etc.? The ETCSL summary mentions the famine; "canals without fish" not confirmed (the text has the canals yielding "no water/nothing"; I could not confirm "fish").
   https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.2.1.5&display=Crit&charenc=gcirc ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire
   Replacement for line 115: "...The poem then describes what the curse looked like: fields and canals that gave nothing, clouds that gathered and gave no rain, and mountain raiders loose in the land." And line 115 "written not long afterwards": "a Sumerian poem composed within a few generations (copies survive from about five hundred years later)" only if the date is confirmed; safer: "a poem the scribes of the plain went on copying for centuries".

25. The city of Akkad has never been located - CONFIRMED.
   Wikipedia (Akkad city): "Its location is unknown." Proposals near the Tigris/Diyala confluence (Tell Muhammad excavated without Akkadian-era remains), Ishan Mizyad, Tell Sinker (Kawakami 2022 proposal), others.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkad_(city)
   Chapter says "somewhere under the mud of central Iraq": fine. "archaeologists are still looking for it": fine.

26. En-priestess office at Ur held by kings' daughters for about 500 years - CONFIRMED (approximately).
   Wikipedia (Enheduanna): royal daughters held the post in the Ur III and Isin-Larsa periods; Enheduanna's successor was probably Enmenanna, Naram-Sin's daughter; Heidelberg source: custom ended after En-ane-du, sister of Rim-Sin of Larsa (r. 1822-1763 BC), died. 2250 to about 1760 BC = about 500 years. Wikipedia notes the office then fell out of use until Nabonidus revived it in the 6th century BC. A continuous line is not documented for the Gutian gap.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enheduanna
   Replacement for line 129: "Even the office that Enheduanna had held at Ur went on being given to the daughters of kings for about the next five hundred years."

27. Sargon II of Assyria (722 BC), "a general", usurper, took the name; "more than fifteen hundred years after his death" - OVERSTATED ("general"); name and date CONFIRMED.
   Wikipedia (Sargon II): became king January 722 BC; most scholars think he was a usurper, though Elayi argues he inherited legitimately; likely son of Tiglath-pileser III and probably brother of Shalmaneser V; "nothing is known" of his life before the throne (not described as a general). Name Sharru-kin ("the faithful/legitimate/true king") most likely taken from Sargon of Akkad; first king in more than a thousand years to bear it. Sargon of Akkad's death c. 2279 BC; 2279 - 722 = about 1,557 years. "Later and far larger empire on the same river": the Assyrian empire was centred on the Tigris; "on the same river" is loose (Akkad on the Euphrates/Tigris plain); safer to say "in the same land".
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_II
   Replacement for lines 131: "More than fifteen hundred years after his death, a man took the throne of Assyria, a later and far larger empire in the same land. He was not next in line, and when he had to choose a name to rule under, he called himself Sargon."
