I read the style guide and outline first, then chapter 27 and chapters 23–25. Findings below are ranked by seriousness. Line numbers refer to [chapter 27](chapters/27-fall/chapter.md). No files were edited.

1. **Factual error and internal contradiction — line 131.** “It never had to hand a province to an armed people in exchange for protection.”

   The eastern government settled Goths inside its territory in exchange for military service in 382—the very arrangement described at lines 73–75. Whether this constituted handing over a *province* is questionable, but that distinction cannot sustain the categorical contrast here. The defensible distinction concerns the eastern government’s survival and retention of its principal tax base. **Confidence: high.** [Theodosius and the Limits of Empire, discussion of the settlement](https://ebrary.net/252932/history/_focdus_).

2. **Factual overstatement — line 73.** “The trained army of the east was gone, and replacing it would take a generation.”

   Adrianople destroyed much of **Valens’s assembled army**, not every trained eastern force. Ammianus reports that two-thirds of the army engaged died; he also describes Roman troops fighting afterwards. “A generation” is an unsupported recovery timetable. This matters because the sentence makes the subsequent settlement appear mechanically inevitable. **Confidence: high.** [Ammianus, Book 31, especially 13.18 and 16.5–6](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Ammian/31%2A.html).

3. **Disputed reconstruction presented as fact — line 75.** “An armed nation with its own leaders now lived within the borders, and the emperor could ask for its help but had no power to order it.”

   The precise terms of the 382 settlement do not survive. Gothic autonomy and military organisation remain disputed; the evidence does not establish an independent nation exempt from imperial orders. Military service was an obligation, and Theodosius actively managed and commanded Gothic participation. Retain the difficulty of controlling these forces, but not the claim that he possessed no authority. **Confidence: high that this exceeds the evidence; moderate on the exact constitutional arrangement.** [Theodosius and the Limits of Empire](https://ebrary.net/252932/history/_focdus_).

4. **Factual error — line 59.** “The two powers fought every generation or so over the fortress towns of northern Iraq and the mountain kingdom of Armenia, and neither could do the other serious harm.”

   Neither permanently conquered the other during this period, but both inflicted serious harm. Persia’s capture and destruction of Antioch in 540, with deportation of its inhabitants, falls squarely within the chapter’s dates. Territorial losses and the captured emperor already described also undermine this wording. **Confidence: high.** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Antioch”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/antioch-1-northern-syria/).

5. **Unsupported addition to the opening’s source — line 11.** “The monk who tells the story, and who had known people who lived through it, adds only that the garrison then broke up, as the others had.”

   Eugippius does **not** add that ending. He explains the general disappearance of paid garrisons, says the Batavis troop held out, and recounts the pay party’s death and discovery. The immediate breakup of this particular garrison is the chapter’s inference, not his report. Because the chapter builds its governing analogy on this ending, the distinction matters. **Confidence: high.** [Eugippius, *Life of Severinus*, chapter 20](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/severinus_02_text.htm).

6. **Factual overstatement — line 93.** “It was also almost ungarrisoned, because it lay behind the sea, far from any frontier.”

   Roman Africa had a southern land frontier and military forces under Boniface. Those forces fought the Vandals; eastern reinforcements subsequently fought them too. Carthage’s vulnerability should not become an almost undefended North Africa. **Confidence: high on the correction; exact effective troop strength is uncertain.** [Procopius, *Vandal War* I.3](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Procopius/Wars/3B%2A.html).

7. **Factual error / exception-breaking absolute — line 59.** “A large part of its army had to stand there in all seasons, and it could not be marched off to deal with trouble elsewhere.”

   Eastern troops could and did move away from the Persian theatre. During the Gothic emergency, Valens sought a diplomatic settlement with Persia and sent troops from Armenia towards the Balkans. Persia constrained troop deployment; it did not make redeployment impossible. **Confidence: high.** [Ammianus 31.7.1–3](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Ammian/31%2A.html).

8. **Misleading account of monetary collapse — line 37.** “That left the question of how to pay for it without a working coinage, and his answer shaped the lives of ordinary people for centuries.”

   Coinage continued to function, despite severe instability. Diocletian himself introduced a substantial monetary reform, including a high-silver coin, around 294. The expansion and organisation of taxation in kind did not mean that money had stopped working altogether. **Confidence: high.** [Bank of Italy’s monetary-history collection, cases 45–46](https://www.bancaditalia.it/servizi-cittadino/musei-e-visite/museo-moneta/moneta-coniata/index.html?com.dotmarketing.htmlpage.language=1).

9. **Overgeneralised legal claim — line 41.** “A tenant farmer was bound to the estate where he was registered, and his children after him.”

   This makes a developing and differentiated legal status sound universal. Certain registered agricultural populations became subject to hereditary restrictions; ordinary tenancy did not everywhere mean hereditary attachment to an estate. The surrounding paragraph also moves too readily from laws prescribing restrictions to their comprehensive enforcement. **Confidence: high that the universal formulation is unsafe; the development and reach of the colonate are debated.** [Boudewijn Sirks, *The Colonate in the Roman Empire*, introduction](https://assets.cambridge.org/97810091/72608/excerpt/9781009172608_excerpt.pdf).

10. **Factual overstatement and style violation — line 121.** “Pots were made by hand at home, and nobody built in stone or struck coins.”

    “Nobody built in stone” is false: post-Roman Tintagel had stone buildings. Its fifth- and sixth-century imported Mediterranean pottery also prevents the surrounding passage from treating all pottery as domestic handmade production. These exceptional, wealthy sites do not disprove widespread impoverishment; they do disprove the absolutes. **Confidence: high.** [Historic England’s Tintagel record](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1014793/?section=official-list-entry), [archaeological study of Mediterranean imports](https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/archiveDS/archiveDownload?t=arch-281-1%2Fdissemination%2Fpdf%2FRR157_Continental_and_Mediterranean_Imports.pdf).

11. **Unsupported military composition — line 107.** “Its commander was a Roman general, and most of the men who fought in it were Goths.”

    Aëtius fought with a coalition containing Roman forces, Visigoths, Alans and other contingents. Reliable numbers establishing a Gothic majority do not survive. The preceding “Roman in name only” also confuses the soldiers’ ancestry with the army’s institutional identity—a particularly awkward distinction after chapter 23’s account of Rome’s strength through incorporation. That is conceptual tension, rather than a direct contradiction. **Confidence: high that “most” is unsubstantiated.** [Jordanes, *Getica* 191 and 197–198](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Jordanes/Getica/C%2A.html).

12. **Factual error / absolute — line 47.** “He issued a new coin of pure gold, struck at seventy-two to the pound, and he and his successors never debased it.”

    Byzantine successors did debase the gold coinage, conspicuously in the eleventh century. The following sentence’s roughly seven centuries of stability is the sustainable claim; “never” is not. **Confidence: high.** [Kelsey Museum, Byzantine coinage](https://lsa.umich.edu/kelsey/exhibitions/special-exhibitions/big-object-spotlights/big-object-spotlight-4/coins.html).

13. **Incorrect starting figure — line 15.** “Under Augustus and for two centuries after him, the army numbered roughly four hundred thousand men, citizens and provincials together, strung out along frontiers ten thousand kilometres long.”

    Augustus’s standing forces are generally estimated around **250,000–300,000**, depending on what is counted. The larger figure belongs to later imperial expansion, not a constant establishment beginning under Augustus. **Confidence: high on the chronological correction; moderate on any single total.** [*The Impact of the Roman Army*, discussion of Augustus’s approximately 300,000-man establishment](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1163/j.ctv2gjwsqg).

14. **Conflated denominations — line 25.** “By 270 it was a copper disc with a silver wash on the surface, and the silver in it amounted to a few parts in a hundred.”

    The continuous “it” implies that Augustus’s denarius simply became this coin. The heavily debased principal denomination of the later third century was the **antoninianus**, introduced in 215 and replacing the denarius in widespread production from the 240s. Explain the replacement briefly; technical names need not dominate the story. **Confidence: high.** [Chartered Institute for Archaeologists, Roman coinage guide](https://www.archaeologists.net/sites/default/files/2025-03/CIfA-Toolkit-PDF-guide.pdf).

15. **Wrong monetary unit — line 43.** “A day's farm labour, with meals, was to cost no more than twenty-five of the debased coins.”

    The edict quoted prices in **denarii communes**, accounting units, not twenty-five identifiable debased coins. Different circulating denominations represented different numbers of these units. The wage figure is sound; its explanation is not. **Confidence: high.** [“Denarius and Sestertius in Diocletian’s Coinage Reform,” *Journal of Roman Studies*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-studies/article/abs/denarius-and-sestertius-in-diocletians-coinage-reform/DF125BFA286373A6D9560502E5D30B19).

16. **Numerical error — line 103.** “At its height the payment was about a tonne of gold a year.”

    The reported annual tribute was **2,100 Roman pounds**, approximately **690 kilograms**. “About seven hundred kilograms” or “roughly two-thirds of a tonne” preserves the intended scale more accurately. **Confidence: high.** [“Roman Gold and Hun Kings,” Cardiff research repository](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/21635/1/ATTWHQM4.pdf).

17. **Uncertain biography treated as established — line 33.** “He was the son of a freed slave, he had risen through the ranks, and he approached the empire as a problem in organisation.”

    Diocletian’s precise parentage is uncertain. Eutropius reports competing traditions: a scribe’s son, or himself a senator’s freedman. “The son of a freed slave” should not be stated as settled fact. His obscure origins are enough for the story. **Confidence: high that the detail is uncertain.** [Eutropius, *Breviarium* 9.19, translated excerpt](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781474471909-015/html).

18. **Internal inconsistency / absolute — line 41.** “The empire's subjects had all become citizens a few decades earlier, and now a large share of them were no longer free to change their work or their address.”

    “All” includes enslaved subjects. Line 29 correctly limits the citizenship grant to **free inhabitants**. Preserve that distinction here. **Confidence: high; the chapter itself supplies the correction.**

19. **Reader orientation — line 83.** “The emperor, safe behind the marshes of a city on the Adriatic coast, refused every offer.”

    The reader has just learned about Rome and Constantinople as imperial centres, then encounters an unnamed third seat of government. Identify **Ravenna** and explain briefly that the western court had moved there for protection. The “few names” rule allows names needed to follow the story.

20. **Reader orientation — line 57.** “One Roman emperor kneels in front of him with his arms stretched out, and the king holds another by the wrist.”

    Only one captured emperor has been introduced. The unexplained second emperor makes the reader wonder whether both were captured together. Identify the kneeling figure as Philip the Arab seeking terms and the standing captive as Valerian, or explain that the relief combines separate victories. **Confidence: high on the need for explanation.**

21. **Signpost sentence — line 29.** “One act of those years deserves notice for what it shows about the treasury's state of mind.”

    This announces the significance of the coming example instead of stating it. It directly breaks the prohibition on signpost sentences. Beginning with the citizenship grant would let the example make the point.

22. **Signpost sentence — line 53.** “The eastern emperors had a neighbour that the west never had to think about, and it helps to explain the choices that they made.”

    “It helps to explain the choices” announces an explanation. State Persia’s strategic effect directly. “Never had to think about” is also broader than the actual point that the western government had no Persian frontier.

23. **Stacked statistics — line 43.** “A pound of pork was twelve, and a haircut two.”

    Together with the preceding twenty-five-unit wage, this stacks three prices for one point—the precise pattern prohibited by “One number per point.” Keep one comparison that makes the edict tangible.

I found no clear direct contradiction with chapters 23–25, and no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions, or prohibited chapter cross-references. The references to Cannae and the earlier Italian alliance are legitimate narrative callbacks under `style.md`.
