# 27. Why did the Roman Empire fall?

*The Roman world from Britain to Syria, from about the year 235 to 565*

Half of it fell, and the other half lasted another thousand years. The western half fell because it lost the provinces that paid for its army, and a government that cannot pay its soldiers soon has none. The eastern half kept its richest provinces, kept its taxes and kept its army.

Some time in the 470s, the Roman soldiers who guarded a small town on the upper Danube, in what is now Bavaria, noticed that their pay had stopped arriving. They were among the last of their kind. For four hundred years a line of forts along the river had marked the northern edge of the empire, and the men in them had been fed, clothed and paid from the emperor's treasury. One by one, in the years before, the other garrisons along that stretch had gone unpaid and had drifted apart.

The men of this garrison decided to find out what had happened. They chose a few of their number and sent them over the Alps to Italy, to collect the wages that were owed to the whole unit.

Nobody heard from the party again. Some days later, their bodies came floating down the river and washed up on the bank beside the town. They had been killed on the road by raiders. The story is told by a monk who had known people who lived through it. That garrison had been the last on its stretch of the river, and before long it too was gone.

Nobody defeated those soldiers in battle. They stopped being a Roman army on the day they understood that the money was never going to come. The western empire ended in the same way, over a period of about seventy years, as its income shrank and the people who depended on that income made other arrangements.

Seen from the treasury, the Roman Empire was an arrangement for moving wealth from farmers to soldiers. In the two centuries after Augustus, the army grew to roughly four hundred thousand men, citizens and provincials together, strung out along frontiers ten thousand kilometres long. Paying, feeding and equipping them consumed well over half of everything the government collected. The rest of what the empire was known for, including its roads, its law courts and the long peace inside its borders, depended on that payment being made on time, every year.

## Bad silver

For two hundred years it was. Then, in the year 235, a group of soldiers on the Rhine murdered a young emperor who had tried to buy off an enemy instead of fighting him, and put one of their own officers in his place. In the fifty years that followed, more than twenty men were recognised as emperor. Nearly all of them were generals raised up by their own troops, and nearly all were killed by soldiers, their own or a rival's. Hardly any of them died in bed.

The danger that Augustus had contained had come back. A soldier's loyalty still followed his pay, and a general who promised a bonus could still be made emperor by the men who wanted it. One emperor of an earlier generation had put it plainly on his deathbed. A Roman historian who knew the court reports his advice to his two sons: stay on good terms with each other, enrich the soldiers, and despise everyone else.

Meanwhile the frontiers were under more pressure than at any time since Hannibal. In the east, a new dynasty called the Sasanians had taken over Persia and meant to recover everything that the old Persian kings had held. In 260 its king defeated a Roman army in Syria and captured the emperor alive, which had never happened before. Along the Rhine and the Danube, Germanic peoples who had learned a good deal from two centuries beside the empire broke through in large raids. At one point the western provinces and the eastern ones each set up a government of their own. During the same years, a plague emptied cities from Egypt to Gaul.

Every emperor needed money for soldiers at once, and raising taxes takes years. There was a quicker way. The standard coin in which the army was paid was silver, and the emperor controlled the mints. If he melted down a hundred coins, mixed in some copper, and struck a hundred and twenty, he had made money out of nothing, or so it seemed. Each emperor did it a little more than the last. In the time of Augustus the coin had been almost pure silver. By 270 the coin that had taken its place was a copper disc with a silver wash on the surface, and the silver in it amounted to a few parts in a hundred.

People are not fooled by this for long. Shopkeepers looked at the new coins and asked for more of them. Prices rose, the soldiers demanded more pay to keep up, and the mints turned out worse coin still. Within two generations, the money that had served the Mediterranean since the wars with Carthage was all but worthless, and the government had destroyed its own means of collecting taxes in anything of value.

In 212 an emperor declared that every free inhabitant of the empire was from that day a Roman citizen. The status that the Italians had fought a war to get, and that had once protected a man's back from the rods, was handed to tens of millions of people in a single sentence. A senator who lived through it wrote that the emperor's real purpose was to raise money, since citizens were the ones who paid the tax on inheritances.

## Four emperors and a price list

The empire was put back together by a soldier from the Balkans named Diocletian, who became emperor in 284 and held the post for twenty-one years, longer than anyone in a century. He came from an obscure family, he had risen through the ranks, and he approached the empire as a problem in organisation.

His first conclusion was that the job was too big for one man. An emperor on the Rhine could not also be on the Euphrates, and a general left alone on a distant frontier with a winning army tended to become an emperor himself. Diocletian therefore took a colleague, and each of the two took a deputy. There were now four rulers, each near a threatened frontier with an army of his own, and none of them lived in Rome. A statue of the four, carved from a hard purple stone, is built into a corner of a church in Venice. They are shown in identical military dress, each with one hand on his sword and the other arm around a colleague's shoulder.

His second conclusion was that the army had to be larger. He raised it to something like half a million men. That left the question of how to pay for it without a coinage that anyone trusted, and his answer shaped the lives of ordinary people for centuries.

The government stopped relying on money and taxed in goods. Officials went through the provinces counting every field, vine, ox and labourer. Each district was then told how much grain, oil, wine, cloth and how many recruits it owed, and the goods were carted directly to the army's storehouses.

A system like that works only if people stay where they have been counted. A farmer who moves away leaves his village short on its quota. Over the next two generations, therefore, the law fixed people in place. A tenant farmer on the tax rolls was bound to the estate where he was registered, and his children after him. The son of a soldier had to be a soldier, and the son of a baker in the city of Rome had to be a baker. The town councillors, who were made personally liable for any tax their district failed to deliver, were forbidden to resign. The empire's free subjects had all become citizens a few decades earlier, and now a large share of them were no longer free to change their work or their address.

Diocletian also tried to command prices to stand still. In 301 he issued an edict that set the highest legal price for more than a thousand goods and services, and it was carved in stone in marketplaces across the eastern provinces, where pieces of it still turn up. A day's farm labour, with meals, was to cost no more than twenty-five of the small units in which prices were reckoned, and a haircut no more than two. The penalty for charging more was death. A Christian writer who lived through it reports the result, which a modern economist could have predicted. Goods vanished from the markets, since nobody would sell at a loss, and after some bloodshed the law was allowed to lapse.

Then Diocletian did something that no emperor had done before, and retired. He went home to the Balkan coast, to a palace so large that the old centre of the modern city of Split fits inside its walls, and grew vegetables.

His system of four rulers fell apart almost at once, and the man who came out on top of the civil wars was Constantine. Apart from his choice of religion, Constantine made two decisions that outlasted him by a very long time. He issued a new coin of pure gold, struck at seventy-two to the pound, and his successors left it alone. It held its weight for seven hundred years. He also built a new capital in the east, on the narrow strait where Europe and Asia almost touch, and named it after himself, Constantinople.

From then on the empire had two centres of gravity. The east had the larger cities and the trade, and most of the gold. It spoke Greek. The west spoke Latin, and outside Italy and North Africa it was a land of great estates and small towns. After 395 the two halves had separate emperors, courts and armies for good, although on paper there was still one empire.

## The other empire

The eastern emperors had a neighbour that the western ones did not.

Persia was no barbarian kingdom. The Sasanian state had cities, a tax system, a professional heavy cavalry in armour from head to foot, and a state religion with its own priesthood, the old Persian faith of Zoroaster. Its kings called themselves "king of kings", and they addressed the Roman emperor as a brother. A Persian envoy once told a Roman court that the two empires were like two lamps, or two eyes, by which the world was lit.

Near the tombs of the old Persian kings, the Sasanian ruler who captured a Roman emperor had the event carved into a cliff. The king sits on his horse. One Roman emperor, who had bought peace from him some years before, kneels in front of the horse with his arms stretched out. The king holds the captured one by the wrist.

The two powers fought every generation or so over the fortress towns of northern Iraq and the mountain kingdom of Armenia, and neither could conquer the other. For Constantinople, however, the Persian frontier was a fixed cost. A large part of its army had to stand there in all seasons, and moving it elsewhere was always a risk. When trouble came on the Danube, the eastern empire would usually prefer to pay it to go away.

## The river crossing

The trouble began far outside the empire, on the grasslands north of the Black Sea. About the year 370 a people of mounted archers called the Huns arrived there from the east. They fought as the Xiongnu had fought on the Chinese frontier, and the farming peoples in their path had no answer to them.

The largest of those peoples were the Goths, who lived north of the Danube in what is now Romania and Ukraine. In 376 a great mass of them, with their families and wagons, appeared on the north bank of the river and asked to be taken in. They offered to farm empty land and to serve in the army.

The eastern emperor agreed, and was pleased to. Recruits were always scarce, and here were tens of thousands of warriors asking to enlist. The crossing took days, on boats and rafts and hollowed tree trunks.

What happened on the other bank was recorded by a Roman officer who was ashamed of it. The officials in charge had been given grain to feed the newcomers, and they sold it instead. When the Goths were starving, the officers rounded up dogs and offered them as meat, at the rate of one dog for one Gothic child, to be taken as a slave. Before long, the Goths rebelled.

Two years later the emperor marched against them in person, without waiting for the western army that was coming to help him. Near the city of Adrianople, on a hot August afternoon in 378, the Gothic cavalry caught his army in the flank. The emperor and two thirds of his men were killed. It was the worst Roman defeat since Cannae, six centuries before.

After Cannae, Rome had simply raised new legions from its allies. This time there was no such reserve. The best of the eastern army was gone, and trained soldiers are slow to replace. The next emperor therefore made a peace of a kind that Rome had never made. The Goths were given land inside the frontier. In return they would fight for the empire when asked, but as a people, under their own chiefs, and not as recruits scattered through Roman units.

On paper it resembled the old bargain with the Italian allies, soldiers in exchange for a place inside. The difference was that the Italians had been absorbed town by town, and each had dealt with Rome alone. The Goths stayed together. An armed nation with its own leaders now lived within the borders, and the emperor could never be sure that it would do as he ordered.

## Three days in Rome

The man who showed what that meant was a Gothic noble named Alaric. He was a Christian, he had commanded Gothic troops in a Roman civil war, and what he wanted was a career. He asked for the rank of a Roman general, regular pay and grain for his followers, and a province to settle them in. For fifteen years he pressed these demands on both imperial courts, moving his people from the Balkans into Italy and back, sometimes as an ally and sometimes as an invader.

The western court in those years was run by a general who was himself the son of a Vandal officer, and who understood that Alaric could be hired more cheaply than he could be fought. In 408 that general's enemies persuaded the young emperor to have him executed. Mobs in the Italian towns then massacred the wives and children of the barbarian soldiers in Roman service. As a result, some thirty thousand of those soldiers walked out and joined Alaric.

Alaric marched on Rome and blockaded it. The emperor refused every offer. He was safe behind the marshes of Ravenna, a city on the Adriatic coast to which the western court had moved. Alaric lowered his price twice. In the end he asked for nothing more than land in a poor frontier province and a supply of grain, and was still refused. On an August night in 410, someone opened a gate, and the Goths spent three days looting the city. They left the great churches alone.

Rome was no longer the seat of government, and the damage was soon repaired. All the same, no foreign enemy had entered the city in eight hundred years, and the news shook people who had never seen it. A Christian scholar who was living in Bethlehem wrote that the city which had taken the whole world was itself taken.

Meanwhile the frontier in the north had given way. On the last day of 406, several peoples, the Vandals among them, crossed the Rhine and spread through Gaul and into Spain. The troops that might have stopped them had been pulled back to defend Italy. A few years later the army left Britain and did not return, and the Britons were left to defend themselves.

## The grain ships stop

Even then the western empire could have lived. It had lost control of provinces before and recovered them. The court's method in these years was to settle one barbarian people on land in exchange for its fighting another, and to wait for better times. What made recovery impossible was the loss of one particular province.

Roman North Africa, the country around Carthage that is now Tunisia and eastern Algeria, was the richest part of the west. Its wheat and olive oil fed the city of Rome, and its taxes were the largest single item in the western treasury. It was also lightly defended, because it lay behind the sea, far from the Rhine and the Danube.

In 429 the Vandals, who had fought their way down through Spain, crossed the strait into Africa. There were about eighty thousand of them, counting women and children. Ten years later their king took Carthage without a fight, on a day when most of its people were at the races.

The Vandals now owned the west's best farmland and its main fleet. The grain ships stopped sailing to Rome except on the Vandal king's terms, and the tax money stopped altogether.

The effect shows in the western government's own words. Five years after Carthage fell, the emperor issued a law creating a new sales tax. In its preamble, he admits that the income from the exhausted taxpayers is not enough to feed and clothe the soldiers he already has, let alone new recruits.

With fewer soldiers, the court could defend fewer provinces. The provinces it failed to defend then paid no tax, and so it could afford fewer soldiers again. Everyone in authority understood that Africa was the key. In 468 the two halves of the empire joined in one last effort to retake it, and Constantinople paid for a fleet of more than a thousand ships, at a cost greater than its whole income for a year. The Vandal king asked for five days' truce to consider terms. He used them to wait for a favourable wind, and then sent burning ships in among the anchored fleet at night.

In the middle of these years came the Huns themselves. Under their king Attila they had built an empire of subject peoples north of the Danube, and Attila ran it as a business whose product was fear. He raided the Balkans until the eastern emperor agreed to pay him gold every year, and then raised the price. At its height the payment was about seven hundred kilograms of gold a year. Constantinople grumbled and paid, since that was still cheaper than the campaigns it would otherwise have had to fight.

A Roman diplomat who visited Attila's camp met a man there in Hun dress who greeted him in Greek. He turned out to be a merchant from a Roman town on the Danube who had been captured, had won his freedom by fighting well, and had chosen to stay. Life was better among the Huns, he told the diplomat. In the empire, the taxes were crushing, the law was for sale, and a poor man could not get justice. The diplomat argued with him, and records that the man wept and said that the laws were good, but that the people in charge were ruining them.

When Attila turned west and invaded Gaul in 451, the army that stopped him was commanded by a Roman general, and a large part of it consisted of Goths and other peoples fighting under their own kings. Attila died two years later, and his empire broke up within a few seasons of his death.

For the landowners of the western provinces, these decades posed a practical question. The emperor could no longer protect their estates. A Gothic or Burgundian king, living nearby with several thousand warriors, could. The price was usually a share of the land, or of its rents, to support the king's followers.

Most of them paid it, without enthusiasm. The best witness is a nobleman from central Gaul named Sidonius, the son and grandson of high imperial officials. As a young man he wrote a friend an admiring account of a Gothic king's day, from morning prayers to an evening game of backgammon, and he was careful to let the king win. Later he lived among Burgundian soldiers billeted on his property. In a poem, he complains that he cannot write verse with seven-foot giants around him who grease their hair with rancid butter and reek of garlic and onions from early in the morning. He ended his life as a bishop under a Gothic king, having led the defence of his city against that king's army and lost.

## A boy sent to the country

By the 470s the western emperor controlled little more than Italy, and his army consisted of barbarian troops under barbarian officers. In 476 those troops asked for what their cousins had been given in Gaul, a third of the land of Italy. The government refused. Their commander, a soldier named Odoacer, thereupon removed the emperor.

The emperor was a boy in his teens, the son of a general. Odoacer did not kill him. He gave him a generous pension and sent him to live in a villa on the Bay of Naples. He then packed up the imperial robes and crown and sent them to Constantinople, with a message that the world needed only one emperor, and that he would be happy to govern Italy on that emperor's behalf.

Hardly anybody at the time treated this as the end of anything. The Senate went on meeting in Rome, and consuls were still named each year. Odoacer and the Gothic king who replaced him kept the Roman tax officials and law courts running, and employed learned senators to write their letters in elegant Latin. The kings of the Goths, Franks and Burgundians were Christians, and they thought of themselves as holding office in a Roman world. Some historians, looking at this continuity, prefer to speak of a transformation and avoid the word "fall".

The ground tells a harsher story. Archaeologists can measure the wealth of ordinary people by what they threw away, and across the west it shrank for two centuries. In the Roman period, a peasant in Britain or Gaul ate from factory-made pottery shipped hundreds of kilometres, lived under a tiled roof, and handled small change. By about 500, in Britain, all three had disappeared. Most pots were made by hand at home, building in stone had all but ceased, and no coins were struck. Even the cattle were smaller, back to the size they had been before the Romans came. The city of Rome, which had held perhaps a million people, held a few tens of thousands by the middle of the next century.

The reason is the one that brought down the palaces at the end of the age of bronze. The Roman economy had been a web of specialists, each relying on distant strangers, held together by safe roads, a common coin and the spending of the army. When the web was cut, a potter could no longer sell beyond his valley, and a farmer had no reason to grow more than he could eat. Most people in the west were living more simply in 600 than their ancestors had in 300, and far fewer of them could read.

## The half that lasted

None of this happened in the east.

The eastern empire faced the same Goths and the same Huns, and Persia besides. It survived them for reasons that had little to do with courage. Constantinople stood on a peninsula, and in the early fifth century it was closed off on the land side by a triple line of walls and a moat that no enemy broke through for a thousand years. Behind the city lay the strait, and beyond the strait lay Turkey, Syria and Egypt, which an army coming from the Danube could not reach without a fleet. The provinces that paid the most tax were therefore the ones that invaders could not get at.

As long as Egypt's grain and gold kept arriving, the eastern government could pay its soldiers, and it could pay its enemies when that was cheaper. It never lost the provinces that paid for everything else. When the western emperor was pensioned off, the eastern treasury was in surplus, and a careful emperor a generation later left his successors a reserve of more than a hundred tonnes of gold.

The man who spent it was Justinian, who reigned from 527 to 565. He was a peasant's son from the Balkans, he slept little, and he intended to restore the Roman Empire in full.

Some of what he did has lasted to the present. He set a commission of lawyers to work on a thousand years of Roman law, an enormous heap of statutes and of opinions by legal experts, many of which contradicted one another. In five years they reduced it to a single ordered collection in a few volumes. Six centuries later that collection was rediscovered in Italy, and most of the legal systems of modern Europe and Latin America grew from it. He also built, in the middle of Constantinople, a church with a dome thirty-one metres across that seems to hang over the floor without support, and it still stands.

His armies retook Africa from the Vandals in a single campaign, and then spent twenty years taking Italy from the Goths, in a war that did more damage to the country than all the invasions of the previous century.

Then, in 541, a disease arrived in Egypt on the grain ships and reached Constantinople the following spring. It was bubonic plague, the same infection that would return eight centuries later as the Black Death, and it has been identified from traces in the teeth of people buried at the time. A historian who was in the city wrote that at its height the dead were counted at ten thousand a day, until the counting was given up. The plague came back every dozen years or so for two centuries.

Justinian caught it and recovered. He ruled for twenty-three more years over an empire that stretched again from Spain to Syria, with fewer taxpayers than before and fewer recruits, and with every frontier to defend. Three years after his death, another people crossed the Alps into Italy and took most of it.

His subjects called themselves Romans, and they went on doing so until 1453. To the east of them, the king of kings was preparing for the next war.
