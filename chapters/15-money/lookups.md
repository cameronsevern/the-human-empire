# Chapter 15 fact-check lookups

Verdict key: correct / wrong / imprecise / could not verify. Sentences are quoted from the first draft; "Action" says what was changed in chapter.md.

## Opening (Ephesus and the lion coin)

1. "In 1904, a team from the British Museum was digging at Ephesus ... the ground was so wet that the water had to be pumped out of their trenches"
   - Verdict: correct, imprecise on the year. D. G. Hogarth dug for the British Museum in 1904 to 1905; the low levels were flooded and worked "with the help of a powerful steam-pump" in 1905.
   - Source: Hogarth, Excavations at Ephesus: The Archaic Artemisia (1908), https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/hogarth1908bd1/0040 ; https://sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/latw-kroll-coins-of-sardis
   - Action: "In 1904 and 1905"; steam pump named.

2. "There were about three thousand of them: pins and brooches of gold, small figures carved in ivory, and beads of amber and glass."
   - Verdict: could not verify the figure 3,000. Sources give "more than a thousand items" for the foundation deposit, in gold, electrum, ivory, amber, glass, crystal.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Artemis ; Encyclopaedia Britannica 1911, "Ephesus"
   - Action: changed to "more than a thousand".

3. "Among these gifts were ninety-three small lumps of pale yellow metal."
   - Verdict: correct. "ninety-three electrum coins that had been deposited as offerings during the latter part of the seventh century BCE" (Kroll). Hogarth's own report catalogues 87; later counts give 93.
   - Source: https://sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/latw-kroll-coins-of-sardis ; Robinson, "The Coins from the Ephesian Artemision Reconsidered", JHS 71 (1951), https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-hellenic-studies/article/coins-from-the-ephesian-artemision-reconsidered/4F59EA2644CF7D541BD9961A481F7003

4. "Some were plain. Most of them, however, had a picture stamped into one side: a goat, a cock, a pair of lions, the head of a seal."
   - Verdict: correct for plain pieces (nine typeless, Kroll), goat, cock and seal head (Robinson 1951; Head's catalogue in Hogarth 1908). "A pair of lions" not confirmed as worded.
   - Action: list changed to "a goat, a cock, a stag, the head of a seal" (the stag is the Phanes type, found in the deposit per https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Artemis).

5. "They are the oldest coins that have been found anywhere."
   - Verdict: correct as usually stated; the Guanzhuang spade coins (item 38) overlap in date but are not older. Kroll: late seventh century BCE; the 2021 Antiquity paper notes the Ephesus coins are older than the Guanzhuang ones.
   - Source: Kroll (above); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spade_money

6. "a thick, slightly lumpy pellet about the width of a shirt button, and it weighs a little less than five grams ... head of a roaring lion ... a knob like a small sun on its forehead ... two rough square dents."
   - Verdict: correct. Sardis third-stater: 4.75 g, 12 mm, roaring lion head right, two incuse punches on the reverse. The forehead knob is the usual "sunburst" or "wart".
   - Source: https://sardisexpedition.org/en/artifacts/latw-17

7. "The lion was the badge of the kings of Lydia, who ruled from the city of Sardis, three days' walk inland from Ephesus."
   - Verdict: correct. Kroll: the lion head marks the royal Lydian coinage. Herodotus 5.54: Ephesus to Sardis is 540 stades, three days.
   - Source: Kroll; https://cts.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0016/tlg001/perseus-eng2/5.54.2

## Money without coins

8. "The standard weight was the shekel, about as much as a small coin weighs today ... two shekels for a knocked-out tooth, ten for a foot."
   - Verdict: correct and consistent with chapter 9 (laws of Ur). Shekel about 8.3 g.
   - Source: chapters/09-law/lookups.md

9. "It concerns a woman who keeps a tavern and gives a customer a jar of beer on credit. She is to be paid back, the rule says, in grain at harvest time."
   - Verdict: correct. Laws of Hammurabi 111: an innkeeper who gives a vat of beer on credit receives 50 sila of grain at the harvest. Innkeepers in laws 108 to 111 are women.
   - Source: https://archive.org/details/codeofhammurabi00hammrich (Harper), law 111; Roth, Law Collections from Mesopotamia and Asia Minor (1997), p. 101

10. "One scholar who reviewed all the reports concluded that 'no example of a barter economy, pure and simple, has ever been described'."
   - Verdict: correct. Caroline Humphrey, "Barter and Economic Disintegration", Man 20 (1985), p. 48.
   - Source: https://voidnetwork.gr/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Barter-and-economic-disintegration-by-Caroline-Humphrey.pdf
   - Action: "all the reports" softened to "the reports".

11. "hoards of silver from this period are full of chopped rings, bent wire and broken scraps."
   - Verdict: correct. Hacksilber hoards of the Near East, about 1200 to 586 BCE and earlier, hold cut ingots, jewellery and wire.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hacksilver ; Thompson, "Sealed silver in Iron Age Cisjordan and the 'invention' of coinage", Oxford Journal of Archaeology 22 (2003)

## White gold

12. "the Greeks said that its sands were golden" (the Pactolus); "which the Greeks called white gold".
   - Verdict: correct. Herodotus 5.101 (gold dust carried down from Tmolus by the Pactolus through the market of Sardis); Herodotus 1.50 "white gold" for electrum.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pactolus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrum
   - Note: Sardis Expedition analyses found Pactolus gold itself nearly pure, so the chapter does not say the coin electrum came from that river. https://sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/latw-greenewalt-gold-silver-refining

13. "One lump may be three quarters gold, and another of the same size barely half."
   - Verdict: correct. Natural electrum varies widely; Anatolian electrum is usually given as 65 to 90 per cent gold, with poorer examples known.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrum ; https://coinweek.com/ancient-coins/ancient-electrum-coins/
   - Action: reworded to "three quarters gold, and another ... much less".

14. "The largest in common use was the piece with the lion's head, which was a third of a unit that the Lydians called a stater. Below it came sixths, twelfths and smaller fractions still, each one half the weight of the one above. The smallest is a ninety-sixth of a stater, a speck of metal about the size of a lentil."
   - Verdict: correct. Kroll: largest lion-head denomination 4.7 g, a third of a stater; fractions to 1/48 (lion head) and 1/96 (lion paw, 0.15 g, about 4 mm). "Stater" is the Greek word.
   - Action: "that the Lydians called" changed to "called".

15. "By one estimate, a piece weighing a twenty-fourth of a stater would buy a sheep"
   - Verdict: correct as an estimate (Kroll: a 1/24 stater "could buy a sheep or a bushel of grain").

16. "every one held nearly the same mixture: a little more than half gold ... poorer than the electrum of the hills, which is about three quarters gold ... kept the difference, which came to perhaps a sixth of every payment."
   - Verdict: correct. Kroll: coins about 54 per cent gold, 44 silver, 1 to 2 copper; natural electrum averaged about 73 per cent gold; the state profited by roughly 15 to 20 per cent. Analyses of 21 coins: 55.3 ± 1.6 per cent gold.
   - Source: Kroll; https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:72947465-11ae-42b1-9f2a-fc79b05d1d88

17. "Nearly all of them come from Lydia itself and from the lands close to it."
   - Verdict: correct. Kroll: the overvalued lion-head coins "did not circulate much outside Lydian territory"; easternmost find a hoard of about 45 at Gordion.

## Two dies and a hammer

18. Seals pressed into clay for thousands of years.
   - Verdict: correct; stamp seals from the seventh millennium BCE, cylinder seals from about 3500 BCE. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_seal

19. Blank weighed and melted to a bean shape before striking; die of bronze or iron cut hollow and reversed; lower die set in an anvil; blank often heated; punch struck with a hammer; punch mark on the back of early coins.
   - Verdict: correct. Britannica, "Coin: Ancient minting": lower (obverse) die fixed in an anvil, blank placed on it, upper die struck with one or more hammer blows; preheating practised; surviving dies mostly bronze, iron also used.
   - Source: https://www.britannica.com/money/coin/Ancient-minting ; https://www2.lawrence.edu/dept/art/BUERGER/ESSAYS/production7.html

20. "Within a few generations, somebody thought of carving a picture into the end of the punch as well."
   - Verdict: correct. Reverse types appear in the later sixth century BCE (Athens, Corinth), about a century after the first coins.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_coinage

21. "Every coin made since has had two sides for that reason."
   - Verdict: wrong as an absolute (one-sided medieval bracteates). Action: rewritten as "That is why a coin has two faces."

22. "A blank could be struck in a few seconds, and a small team could make many hundreds of coins in a day."
   - Verdict: correct. Britannica: one man could strike about 100 coins an hour.

23. "Scholars today can lay a hundred coins side by side and say which of them were struck from the same die."
   - Verdict: correct (die studies). Robinson 1951 already links Ephesus coins by shared dies.

24. Plated forgeries with a copper core; test cuts with a chisel.
   - Verdict: correct. Plated (fourrée) coins exist from the earliest electrum onward; test cuts are common on archaic and classical silver.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourr%C3%A9e

## Croesus divides the metal

25. "Croesus, who came to the throne of Lydia about 2,580 years ago."
   - Verdict: correct on the usual dating (about 560 BCE).  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croesus

26. "In 1968, however, archaeologists digging beside the Pactolus at Sardis found the workshop ... rows of small furnaces and hearths, broken clay pots, and, trodden into the floor, hundreds of scraps of gold foil and droplets of gold."
   - Verdict: correct on place, date and furnaces. Excavated 1968 and 1969 under A. Ramage; "hundreds of cupels" (bowl hearths), cementation furnaces, pots. Gold foil and globules are reported in Ramage and Craddock, King Croesus' Gold (2000), but the count "hundreds" for foil is not confirmed.
   - Source: https://sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/about-pn ; https://sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/latw-greenewalt-gold-silver-refining
   - Action: "hundreds of small bowl-shaped hearths ... and scraps of gold foil".

27. Sheets packed in salt in clay pots, heated for many hours below gold's melting point; silver absorbed and recovered by a second smelting.
   - Verdict: correct. Greenewalt: thin sheets, layers of salt (with brick dust), about 800 °C "for many hours, perhaps days"; silver chloride absorbed by the clay; recovered by smelting with lead and cupellation. Gold melts at 1,064 °C.

28. "the front half of a lion facing the front half of a bull. A fixed number of the silver coins was worth one of the gold."
   - Verdict: correct. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croeseid ; Kroll.

29. "These are the first coins of pure gold and pure silver"
   - Verdict: correct. Kroll; Herodotus 1.94.

30. "the Greeks called such a stone a Lydian stone."
   - Verdict: correct. Touchstone = Lydia lithos (Bacchylides, Theophrastus, On Stones). Greenewalt describes touchstone assay at Sardis. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touchstone_(assaying_tool)

31. "more than a century later the city of Athens still held some in its treasury."
   - Verdict: correct. Kroll: Athens still held gold Croeseids in 440/439 BCE.

32. "about 2,570 years ago an army of Persians ... captured Sardis ... for some thirty years they went on striking the lion and the bull ... a king, running, with a bow in his hand."
   - Verdict: correct. Kroll: Cyrus took Sardis in the mid-540s BCE; minting of the lion and bull continued about thirty years; about 515 BCE Darius introduced the daric and siglos with the royal archer.

## Turtles and owls

33. "They lived in several hundred small cities"; coastal Greek cities struck electrum almost as early as the kings.
   - Verdict: correct. About a thousand poleis are known; Ionian electrum (Ephesus, Miletus, Phocaea) is in the Artemision deposit.

34. "During the reign of Croesus ... The island of Aegina ... thick silver coins with a sea turtle on them. Corinth followed with a winged horse, and Athens with the owl"
   - Verdict: correct. Aegina about 550 BCE (some say 570s); Corinth and Athens next; Athenian owls from about 515 BCE.
   - Source: https://www.britannica.com/topic/coin/Origins-of-coins ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_coinage
   - Action: "During the reign of Croesus" changed to "About the time of Croesus".

35. "Within a century of the lion's head, well over a hundred Greek cities were making coins, from the Black Sea to Sicily and the south of France."
   - Verdict: imprecise. Osborne, Greece in the Making, pp. 253 to 255, lists 115 poleis minting by 480 BCE, which is about 150 years after the first lion coins.
   - Source: van Alfen 2012, citing Osborne, https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/notae_numismaticae2012/0015
   - Action: "By 2,500 years ago, more than a hundred Greek cities were making coins, from the coast of Turkey to Sicily and the south of France."

36. Badges: crab (Akragas), bee (Ephesus), ear of barley (Metapontum), wine cup (Naxos). Verdict: correct.

37. "the obol, a scrap of silver not much larger than a lentil"
   - Verdict: imprecise. Obols are 8 to 10 mm and about 0.7 g (Fitzwilliam: 10 mm, 0.57 g). https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/233149
   - Action: "smaller than a fingernail".

38. Coins carried in the mouth; "an old man is paid three obols, pops them into his mouth, and finds that he has been handed fish scales."
   - Verdict: correct. Aristophanes, Wasps 787 to 795: Philocleon is given three mullet scales for obols and puts them in his mouth.
   - Source: https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/ancient-greece/aristophanes/wasps.asp?pg=39

39. Herodotus: "they were the first to sell by retail".
   - Verdict: correct. Herodotus 1.94, Godley: "the first men whom we know who coined and used gold and silver currency; and they were the first to sell by retail."
   - Source: https://cts.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0016/tlg001/perseus-eng2/1.94.1-1.94.7

## Spades and knives

40. "Seven thousand kilometres to the east"
   - Verdict: correct. Sardis to Xingyang, Henan is about 6,900 km in a straight line.

41. "Unlike the smiths of the west, they did not hammer it into shape. They poured it, molten, into moulds of baked clay"
   - Verdict: wrong as a contrast: western bronze was cast too (chapter 11 says so). Chinese piece-mould casting is correct.
   - Action: contrast removed.

42. Spade tools used in payment; coin spades thin, with a hollow socket.
   - Verdict: correct. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spade_money

43. "found at Guanzhuang, in the province of Henan, in a bronze foundry inside the walls of an ancient city ... finished spade coins and the clay moulds ... between about 2,650 and 2,550 years ago."
   - Verdict: correct on finds; imprecise on date and unverified on "inside the walls". Zhao et al., Antiquity 95 (2021): minting about 640 to 550 BCE; clay cores, outer moulds and two spade coins; "the world's oldest-known, securely dated minting site".
   - Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/radiocarbondating-an-early-minting-site-the-emergence-of-standardised-coinage-in-china/178ECC2B245A017BF684BE1EFC732BD1 ; https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/worlds-oldest-coin-factory-discovered-in-china
   - Action: "in the bronze foundry of an ancient city"; "from about 2,650 years ago".

44. Knife coins in the east and north-east with a ring at the handle; characters naming the issuing city.
   - Verdict: correct (Qi and Yan knives; spade inscriptions are mostly city names). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knife_money ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Chinese_coinage
   - Action: "which named" changed to "often the name of".

45. "About 2,400 years ago a simpler form appeared: a flat round disc with a hole through the middle ... made its own round coin, which had a square hole, the money of the whole country ... for the next two thousand years."
   - Verdict: correct. Round coins from about 350 BCE; Qin Ban Liang standardised 221 BCE; cash coins cast until the early twentieth century.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Chinese_coinage ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash_(Chinese_coin)

## There and back

46. "A soldier on campaign was paid about one such coin for each day's service."
   - Verdict: correct. Thucydides 3.17: hoplites at Potidaea drew two drachmas a day, one for the man and one for his servant; 6.31: a drachma a day to each seaman.
   - Source: https://fgh.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0003/tlg001/1st1K-eng1/3.17.3-3.17.4

47. Market fees, harbour duty, court fines paid to the city.
   - Verdict: correct for Greek cities in general (agora dues, the two per cent harbour tax, fines). "The fisherman ... owes a duty on what he lands at the harbour" not verified as worded.
   - Action: changed to a merchant bringing goods in by ship.

48. Sellers followed paid armies; markets beside the camp.
   - Verdict: correct. Thucydides 6.44; Xenophon, Anabasis 1.5.6 and passim.

49. "It is the view that the Greeks themselves held."
   - Verdict: correct. Aristotle, Politics 1257a. Action: "a view as old as the Greeks".

50. "the kings of Lydia are known to have hired foreign soldiers in large numbers."
   - Verdict: correct on hiring; "in large numbers" unverified. Gyges sent Ionian and Carian soldiers to Psammetichus of Egypt; Croesus's army of foreign mercenaries, Herodotus 1.77.
   - Source: https://www.livius.org/articles/person/gyges/
   - Action: "in large numbers" cut.

51. "The merchants of Babylon and the sea traders of the Lebanese coast went on weighing out silver for generations after their neighbours had coins."
   - Verdict: correct. Phoenician cities first struck coins about 480 to 450 BCE; Babylonia used weighed silver through the Persian period.
   - Source: https://coinweek.com/coinweek-ancient-coin-series-coinage-of-the-phoenicians/ ; Jursa, Aspects of the Economic History of Babylonia in the First Millennium BC (2010)

52. Trade or state first: which way the evidence leans.
   - Verdict: the state-payment view (Cook 1958, Kraay 1964, Price 1983) is the majority one; Kroll stresses the testing problem and retail use. The chapter states the lean and keeps the role of trade.
   - Source: Kraay, "Hoards, Small Change and the Origin of Coinage", JHS 84 (1964); Kroll.

## A price on everything

53. "Building accounts from Athens show free men, foreigners and slaves working side by side on the same temple, each paid the same single coin for a day's work."
   - Verdict: imprecise. Erechtheion accounts (409 to 406 BCE): citizens, metics and slaves side by side; one drachma is the day wage, but much work was paid by the piece.
   - Source: https://www.atticinscriptions.com/inscription/IGI3/476
   - Action: "and a day's work earned one silver coin".

54. "About 2,460 years ago, in a tragedy staged at Athens, a king speaks ... 'Nothing so evil as money ever grew to be current among men.' Money, he goes on, lays cities low and drives men from their homes."
   - Verdict: correct. Sophocles, Antigone 295 to 297, Creon, Jebb's translation: "Nothing so evil as money ever grew to be current among men. This destroys cities, this drives men from their homes". Play dated about 441 BCE.
   - Source: https://cts.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0011/tlg002/perseus-eng2/280-330
   - Action: "lays cities low" changed to "destroys cities".

55. Midas: the golden touch, bread and wine turning to gold, washing in the Pactolus.
   - Verdict: correct. Aristotle, Politics 1257b (Midas starving amid gold); Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85 to 145 (the wish, the food, the Pactolus). Midas was king of Phrygia, Lydia's neighbour; the river is in Lydia.
   - Action: the text says the story ends in the country where coins began, and names Midas as a king of the land next to Lydia.
