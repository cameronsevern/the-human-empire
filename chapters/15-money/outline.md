# Chapter 15 — Who invented money?

**Line under the title:** Western Turkey, Greece and northern China, about 2,600 years ago.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,500 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Money is much older than coins. For two thousand years people on the plain of Iraq priced things in barley and silver, wrote down who owed what, and weighed out metal when a debt had to be settled. That worked among neighbours and among merchants who knew each other. It worked badly between strangers. About 2,600 years ago the kings of Lydia, in western Turkey, began stamping lumps of a gold and silver mixture with a lion's head. The stamp turned a lump that had to be weighed and tested into an object that could be counted, and it was worth what it said because the king who made it would take it back. Within a century the Greek cities and the Persian kings were striking coins, and the states of northern China had arrived at cast bronze coins on their own. Rulers made coins mainly because they had people to pay. Once coins existed, markets grew around them, and with markets came daily wages and the habit of putting a price on everything.

## The story

1. **Under the temple floor.** Answer first. The dig at Ephesus in 1904 and the lion's head coin.
2. **Money without coins.** Barley, silver by weight and written debts. The beer bought on credit. Why nobody has found the village that lived by barter. What weighed silver could not do between strangers.
3. **White gold.** Lydia and its pale metal. Why a mixture cannot be weighed out honestly. What the lion promised, who stood behind it, and what the king made on each coin.
4. **Two dies and a hammer.** How a coin is struck, step by step, and why a struck coin is hard to copy.
5. **Croesus divides the metal.** The refinery beside the stream at Sardis. The first coins of pure gold and pure silver. The Persians keep the mint.
6. **Turtles and owls.** The Greek cities take up silver. Small change, and coins carried in the mouth.
7. **Spades and knives.** Bronze coins cast in northern China with no knowledge of Lydia. Round coins on a string.
8. **There and back.** One coin from the mint to a soldier, to a fish stall, to the tax collector. Trade or soldiers: which came first, and which way the evidence leans.
9. **A price on everything.** Small markets, daily wages, and the complaint that money can buy anything. The king who starved among gold.
