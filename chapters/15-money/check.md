1. **Contradiction with chapter 11 — high confidence.** “As a result, trade between strangers was the hardest kind of trade to do, and it stayed small.” Chapter 11 has already described international cargoes, merchant colonies and extensive supply chains, explicitly saying: “None of these people knew most of the others.” Coins simplified payments; they did not make substantial trade between strangers possible for the first time. See [chapter 11, “The donkeys of Ashur”](chapters/11-bronze/chapter.md).

2. **Factual overreach in the central argument — high confidence that the certainty is unwarranted.** “The first coins were struck by kings, and the kings of Lydia are known to have hired foreign soldiers in large numbers.” The royal lion series is identifiable, but the issuers of many other early electrum types are uncertain. Greek cities and potentially other wealthy authorities also issued them. Royal military expenditure is a defensible explanation, but this sentence makes one of its disputed premises sound established. [John Kroll, “The Coins of Sardis”](https://www.sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/latw-kroll-coins-of-sardis).

3. **Doubtful generalisation about pre-coin payments — high confidence.** “This is how most buying and selling was done before coins, and it is worth setting beside a story that is often told about where money came from.” Hammurabi’s provision for beer supplied on credit establishes that this arrangement existed; it cannot establish how *most* transactions across pre-coin societies worked. His neighbouring tavern provision also discusses silver payments. The same problem affects “Very little of that silver ever changed hands, however.” The safe fact is that credit, payment in goods and actual silver payments coexisted. [Hammurabi’s laws, §§108–111](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp).

4. **Misleading claim of innovation — high confidence.** “The second was the daily wage.” In the surrounding argument, this makes daily wages a consequence of coins. Hammurabi’s §273 already specifies daily remuneration in silver, more than a millennium earlier. Chapter 9 also introduces a hired worker’s pay in shekels. Coins changed the convenience and reach of wage payments; they did not introduce daily wages. [Hammurabi’s laws, §273](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp).

5. **Factual error and direct contradiction with chapter 11 — high confidence.** “Unlike the smiths of the west, they did not hammer it into shape.” Western smiths cast bronze too. Chapter 11 explicitly explains pouring bronze into moulds and follows a smith casting a sword. The distinction should concern Chinese casting techniques or the casting of coins, not a supposed absence of western bronze casting. [The Metropolitan Museum’s account of Greek bronze casting](https://www.metmuseum.org/es/essays/the-technique-of-bronze-statuary-in-ancient-greece).

6. **Doubtful financial reconstruction presented as fact — medium confidence.** “It then paid the coins out as if they were the natural metal, and kept the difference, which came to perhaps a sixth of every payment.” Artificial adjustment of the alloy is supported; the official valuation and resulting profit are reconstructed and disputed. “Perhaps” qualifies only the amount, leaving the mechanism certain. Jacques Melitz specifically challenges the inference of large sustained profits and distinguishes the premium over metal value from minting costs. [Melitz, “The profitability of early coinage”](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/profitability-early-coinage).

7. **Factual error and exception-breaking absolute — high confidence.** “Anybody who loses a case in court owes a fine.” In Athens, losing a private suit could entail compensation paid to the successful litigant, not a fine paid to the city. Public penalties also varied. This matters because the sentence supplies evidence for the coin’s supposedly necessary return to the treasury. Use a specific public fine instead. [Harvard Center for Hellenic Studies, Athenian law](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/discussion-series-athenian-law-lectures-15/).

8. **Overstated anthropological finding — high confidence.** “They did not find potters and fishermen haggling over swaps.” Anthropologists have documented barter. The finding quoted immediately afterwards concerns the absence of a *pure barter economy* from which money demonstrably emerged, not the absence of bargaining or direct swaps. Those are different claims. [Caroline Humphrey and Stephen Hugh-Jones, “Introduction: Barter, exchange and value”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/barter-exchange-and-value/introduction-barter-exchange-and-value/830E999E12CAEA351FE269D093A8BAFB).

9. **Unsupported global priority — medium-high confidence.** “The coin itself was invented about 2,600 years ago in Lydia, a kingdom in the west of Turkey.” This gives Lydia an uncontested worldwide priority that the later Chinese section undermines. Guanzhuang’s coin production began within the dated interval approximately 640–550 BCE, overlapping early Anatolian coinage. Lydia is a sound starting point for the western tradition; a single securely established global birthplace is harder to defend. The same qualification applies to “They are the oldest coins that have been found anywhere.” [Zhao and colleagues’ Guanzhuang study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/radiocarbondating-an-early-minting-site-the-emergence-of-standardised-coinage-in-china/178ECC2B245A017BF684BE1EFC732BD1).

10. **Factual error and absolute — high confidence.** “Every coin made since has had two sides for that reason.” Coins already had two physical sides; adding a second engraved die supplied a second design. Moreover, cast coins did not acquire their faces through two-die striking—the Chinese coins described shortly afterwards are the chapter’s own counterexample. [American Numismatic Association, traditional Chinese money](https://www.money.org/money-museum-virtual-exhibits-silk-case2/).

11. **Contradiction and unclear scope — high confidence.** “The Greeks were the first people to live with this, and they were of two minds about it.” If “this” means comparing labour, goods, injuries and people through monetary prices, chapter 9 and this chapter’s own shekel explanation have already demonstrated it in Mesopotamia. If it means pervasive everyday coin use, that narrower claim needs stating. The present wording erases the distinction the opening correctly establishes between money and coins.

12. **Misleading evidence for workers’ freedom — high confidence.** “Building accounts from Athens show free men, foreigners and slaves working side by side on the same temple, each paid the same single coin for a day's work.” The comparable rates are supported, but payment included piecework, and slaves’ earnings formally went to their owners. Following the claim that a coin-paid worker could change employer or city, this example misleadingly extends that freedom to slaves. Describe equal rates for the same work, while making clear who controlled the earnings. [Erechtheion building accounts, with commentary](https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/IGI3/476).

13. **An omitted stage makes the technical account inaccurate — high confidence.** “The silver had soaked into the clay, and it was recovered afterwards by a second smelting.” Recovery required smelting the silver-bearing material with lead, followed by separating the silver from that lead through cupellation. Calling the whole operation a second smelting leaves out the stage that produces usable silver. This can be explained briefly without introducing the technical name. [Sardis excavation project, gold and silver refining](https://sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/latw-greenewalt-gold-silver-refining).

14. **Geographical conflation in the ending — high confidence.** “The Greeks also told a story about it, and they set the story in the country where coins began.” Midas was king of **Phrygia**, not Lydia. His eventual washing in the Pactolus connects the story to Lydia, but does not make his kingdom Lydian. Preserve the river connection without conflating the two countries. [Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, Book XI](https://ovid.lib.virginia.edu/sandys/11.htm).

15. **A general reader cannot identify the money being followed.** “A soldier on campaign was paid about one such coin for each day's service.” The example introduces only “a silver coin”; the last named Greek denomination was the much smaller obol. Specify the city and denomination, and relate it to the obol if necessary. Otherwise “one coin” sounds like a standard unit of value despite the preceding explanation of fractions.

16. **The minting explanation cannot produce its stated result.** “On the Lydian coins that mark is just the rough square end of the bar, which is the reason for the two dents on the back.” One square-ended punch in the described sequence explains one depression, not two. Explain the additional impression or relevant punch arrangement. The preceding sentence, “Under the blow, the metal of the blank had nowhere to go but down into the hollow of the die,” also prevents the reader from picturing the blank spreading sideways as it is flattened.

17. **The opening answer does not follow from its evidence.** “Nobody did, because money is far older than coins: people priced things in silver and kept written accounts of who owed what for two thousand years before the first coin was made.” Being older than coins does not establish that nobody invented money. The intended answer seems to be that money developed gradually and has no identifiable single inventor. State that distinction; “Nobody did” currently makes the question sound disposed of through a logical shortcut.

18. **Explicit signposting prohibited by `style.md`.** “To see why one of those kings began stamping his lion onto lumps of metal, and why that small act mattered so much, it helps to look first at how people had managed without it.” This announces the coming explanation rather than beginning it. It also implicitly describes the chapter’s organisation, although it does not literally say “this chapter.”

19. **Further signpost sentences.** “However, the coins themselves tell a more interesting story.” “Notice who needed the coin in that circuit, and why.” “Notice also what the city gained.” “This raises a question that historians have argued over for a long time.” Each announces a point or directs attention before stating the substance. These fit the style guide’s explicit prohibition; the issue is not their confidence or viewpoint.

20. **Outline structure showing through the prose.** “Coins changed the lives of ordinary people in three ways, and each one grew out of the one before.” This announces a numbered explanatory scheme rather than advancing the story. Its subsequent market–wage–comparison sequence also promises a causal progression the text does not establish. Under `style.md`, begin with the concrete change and let the relationships emerge.
