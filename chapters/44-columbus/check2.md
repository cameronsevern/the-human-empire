## Factual flags

1. **Minor — the new spice generalisation is false.**
   
   > “Chief among them were spices, and each spice came from its own small corner of Asia.”
   
   Not every spice Europeans wanted came from a restricted Asian growing area. Saffron was cultivated in Spain and, from the fourteenth century, in England. The correct claim concerns the particular Asian spices subsequently named, not spices collectively. [University of Cambridge, “Saffron: a Cambridge spice”](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/saffron).

2. **Major — the new claim about guns excludes documented commercial demand.**
   
   > “Guns were the one European product that counted for something in those waters.”
   
   The contemporary voyage account records the ruler of Calicut requesting gold, silver, coral, and scarlet cloth in exchange for his country’s goods. European merchandise was not universally worthless apart from weapons. The defensible distinction is that naval guns gave the Portuguese coercive power; it is not that guns were their sole valued product. [*A Journal of the First Voyage of Vasco da Gama*, translated by E. G. Ravenstein](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/46440/pg46440-images.html).

3. **Minor — the wind did change during Columbus’s crossing.**
   
   > “They could see that it never changed, and they did not believe it would ever carry them home.”
   
   This error survives the sentence restructuring. Columbus’s journal records a contrary wind on **22 September 1492**, which reassured him precisely because the crew feared there would be no wind for the return. The crew’s anxiety is supported; the supposedly unchanging wind is contradicted by the account. [Columbus’s journal, entry for 22 September](https://pressbooks.nvcc.edu/eng255/chapter/the-journals-of-christopher-columbus-during-his-first-voyage-1492-1493/).

4. **Major — Polynesian settlement is conflated with settlement of the whole Pacific.**
   
   > “With those skills, and in double-hulled canoes about as long as a caravel, they found and settled almost every island in the Pacific, across a third of the planet.”
   
   Polynesians did not settle almost every Pacific island. Much of Melanesia and Micronesia has distinct settlement histories; parts of the western Pacific were inhabited thousands of years before Polynesian expansion. The correct geographical scope is the widely scattered islands of Polynesia, together with Polynesian outliers farther west. This error was already present before the flow revision. [Geoff Irwin, “Pacific migrations,” Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/pacific-migrations/print).

## Clarity flags

1. > “The turn of the sea had made that an urgent matter, and at first he had only two tools.”
   
   Only one actual tool—the compass—is subsequently named; the second is a method of estimation. “Only two” also presents the following examples as an exhaustive inventory without defining the period or circumstances.

2. > “Their one check on the sky had disappeared just as their voyages were growing longer.”
   
   “Check on the sky” reverses the intended relationship: the sky supplied a check on the sailor’s estimated position. “One” adds an unnecessary absolute.

3. > “The headland that nobody could return from was the first barrier to go.”
   
   Earlier, inability to return is correctly presented as a belief. Here it becomes the narrator’s assertion, immediately followed by a ship returning. It needs to remain clearly an imagined barrier.

4. > “None of this equipment was the only way to cross an ocean.”
   
   Equipment is not itself a “way,” and “none … was the only” makes the reader untangle the sentence. The intended point is that ocean navigation did not require these instruments.

5. > “Instead, they did something that looked like madness.”
   
   > “Da Gama showed at once what that difference meant.”
   
   > “China could afford to turn away, and it could do so for two reasons.”
   
   These announce a surprise, demonstration, or numbered explanation before delivering it. They are the clearest conflicts with the style sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences.

6. > “Da Gama showed at once what that difference meant. Off the Indian coast da Gama captured a ship full of pilgrims returning from Mecca, among them women and children.”
   
   The added setup produces an immediate repetition of “da Gama,” as well as delaying the concrete event.

7. > “That was what it meant to buy pepper where it grew.”
   
   This repeats the preceding statement that the spices paid for the expedition many times over. It supplies neither a missing reasoning step nor useful orientation.

8. > “A stick chart from the Marshall Islands in the Pacific, a navigator's model of ocean swells made of sticks, in the Ethnological Museum in Berlin.”
   
   Placed directly beneath a passage about Polynesians navigating with “no charts,” this appears contradictory. The missing distinction is that the Marshall Islands are Micronesian; the caption does not explain why this different navigational tradition illustrates the passage.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions, or prohibited body cross-references. Most of the added explanatory transitions fit the style sheet’s instruction to give readers room.

## Overall

The revision preserves the chapter’s concrete particulars but is not entirely fact-neutral: several transitions introduce broader or more exclusive claims. Two new factual errors and two surviving errors need attention, along with a handful of signposts and confusing formulations. Previously fixed flags have not been repeated. No files were edited.
