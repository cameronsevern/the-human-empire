## Changed facts

No existing date, name, numerical figure, or quotation was removed or numerically altered. However, several additions introduce factual claims or change what existing facts mean. Below, **“added” means the old sentence remains**, rather than being replaced.

1. **Land restrictions become physical confinement.**
   - **Old:** “A law of 1913 forbade Africans, who were two thirds of the people, to buy or rent land outside reserves that made up seven per cent of the country.”
   - **New, added:** “Put another way, two thirds of the people were squeezed onto less than a tenth of the land.”
   - This changes a restriction on acquiring land into a claim about where the population lived. See factual flag 1.

2. **The forced-removal total acquires a narrower meaning.**
   - **Old:** “Between 1960 and 1983 three and a half million people were moved by force.”
   - **New framing:** “Putting people into their homelands was brutal work.” The old sentence follows, while urban removals now begin separately: “The cities were cleared as well.”
   - This makes the total appear to count homeland relocations, with urban removals additional. See factual flag 2.

3. **Declining foreign support becomes simultaneous abandonment.**
   - **Old:** “That argument was now dead, and the Soviet support on which the exiled Congress had lived was drying up.”
   - **New, added:** “Both sides, in other words, had lost their foreign backing at the same moment, and both now had reason to talk.”
   - This introduces both a completed cutoff and simultaneous timing. See factual flag 3.

4. **The limits on prosecutions become inability to punish.**
   - **Old:** “It had negotiated its own departure, and its police and army were still in place.”
   - **New, added:** “Trials on the scale of the crimes were therefore out of reach.”
   - **Further addition, with no old counterpart:** “The idea was that if the country could not punish, it could at least find out.”
   - The first is a defensible interpretation of practical constraints; the second goes further. See factual flag 4.

5. **Membership prohibition is clarified and broadened explicitly.**
   - **Old:** “To belong to it was now a crime.”
   - **New:** “To belong to either was now a crime.”
   - This correctly makes the sentence cover both banned movements.

6. **New explanations of resistance tactics.**
   - **Old:** “An Indian lawyer named Gandhi had worked out his method of resistance in South Africa in the years before 1914.”
   - **New, added:** “His method was to break an unjust law in the open, without violence, and to accept the punishment.”
   - **Old:** “The police opened fire.”
   - **New:** “The hope was that if enough people did so, the pass laws would break down. Instead, the police opened fire.”
   - These introduce explanations of the methods and intentions, without changing the existing events.

7. **The ANC receives explicit agency in launching armed action.**
   - **Old:** “In December 1961 a new organisation, called the Spear of the Nation, set off its first bombs.”
   - **New introduction:** “The Congress acted on that reasoning.”
   - This adds an institutional attribution. It is broadly consistent with the narrative, although the initial organisational relationship between the ANC and the new armed organisation was more complicated.

8. **Biko receives an additional attributed argument.**
   - **Old:** “Steve Biko, a former medical student, taught that Black people must first free their own minds from the belief that they were inferior.”
   - **New, added:** “A people who no longer believed that, he reasoned, could not be ruled in the old way.”
   - This adds a paraphrase of his reasoning; it is not merely a connective sentence.

9. **New claims about policing and voting rights.**
   - **Old:** “A quarter of a million people a year were arrested for not having it in order.”
   - **New, added:** “Every day, somewhere in the country, men and women were taken to the cells for a missing stamp.”
   - **Also added, without an old counterpart:** “Movement was policed most closely of all.”
   - **Old:** “They could then be treated as migrant workers in their own country.”
   - **New, added:** “A foreigner, after all, has no claim to a vote.”
   - These add a daily-frequency claim, a comparative ranking, and a general claim about electoral rights.

10. **New financial explanation.**
    - **Old:** “South African firms had borrowed heavily for short periods, and were suddenly asked to repay it all.”
    - **New, added:** “Short loans like these are normally renewed again and again, so once the lenders refused, the money had to be found at once.”
    - This supplies the missing mechanism. Strictly, repayment falls due as loans mature, rather than all immediately.

Other additions largely restate existing information or express the narrator’s interpretation: the four-in-five calculation, Africans’ exclusion from parliament, Mandela’s international celebrity, the political purpose of violence, and the significance of reconciliation and retirement. They do not alter the chapter’s existing numbers or events.

## Factual flags

1. **Major — the new land summary misstates residence.**

   > “Put another way, two thirds of the people were squeezed onto less than a tenth of the land.”

   The 1913 Act did not move or confine the entire African population to the reserves. Large numbers continued living on white-owned farms and in towns. Even in 1936, only about 45% of Africans lived in reserves. The correct distinction is between land reserved for African acquisition and the places Africans actually inhabited. This is a newly introduced problem, not a repetition of the earlier objection to “own.” [Colin Bundy’s account of the Land Act](https://sahistory.org.za/archive/centenary-1913-land-act-colin-bundy-18-april-2013)

2. **Major — the new framing misclassifies the forced-removal total.**

   > “Putting people into their homelands was brutal work. Between 1960 and 1983 three and a half million people were moved by force.”

   Followed by:

   > “The cities were cleared as well.”

   The approximately 3.5 million total covers several kinds of removal, including urban racial rezoning, farm evictions, and homeland relocation. It is not a homeland-only total to which city clearances should be added. The presidential land-reform report’s breakdown explicitly includes approximately 834,400 Group Areas removals, usually within cities. [Presidential Advisory Panel report, Table 2](https://www.thepresidency.gov.za/sites/default/files/2022-05/Panel%20Report%20on%20Land%20Reform.pdf)

3. **Major — simultaneous loss of foreign backing is false and internally contradictory.**

   > “Both sides, in other words, had lost their foreign backing at the same moment, and both now had reason to talk.”

   The preceding sentence says Soviet support “was drying up”; this sentence turns that into support already lost. Soviet representatives were still assuring the ANC of support in December 1990, and military training remained an issue in 1991. The chapter itself dates major Western financial pressure to 1985–86, not November 1989. The defensible point is that the Cold War’s ending changed both sides’ incentives, not that their backing disappeared simultaneously. This addition reintroduces the substance of an error the earlier wording had fixed. [History of Soviet–ANC relations](https://sahistory.org.za/article/union-soviet-socialist-republics-ussr-and-anti-apartheid-struggle)

4. **Minor — the country retained the ability to prosecute.**

   > “The idea was that if the country could not punish, it could at least find out.”

   This turns the practical difficulty of comprehensive trials into an inability to punish at all. Prosecution remained possible and helped make conditional amnesty attractive. Eugene de Kock’s prosecution is a concrete counterexample; the TRC itself said it encouraged amnesty applications. The correct distinction is between selective prosecutions and prosecuting crimes on their full scale. [TRC final report, Volume 6, Section 3, Chapter 1](https://sabctrc.saha.org.za/reports/volume6/section3/chapter1/subsection2.htm?t=%2BUnrest+%2BInvestigation+%2BUnit&tab=report)

5. **Minor — South Africa was not Africa’s sole centuries-old European settlement.**

   > “South Africa was the one place on the continent where Europeans had settled in large numbers and stayed for centuries.”

   Angola provides a counterexample: Portuguese settlement dated from the sixteenth century, and approximately 300,000 Portuguese settlers left during the transition to independence. South Africa’s distinctive enduring settler society can be described without claiming continental uniqueness. [World Bank account of Angola’s settler departure](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/407741468210259445/pdf/multi0page.pdf), [historical account of Portuguese settlement in Angola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_history_of_Angola)

6. **Minor — unemployment has the wrong denominator.**

   > “A third of its workers have no job.”

   The statistic describes approximately a third of the **labour force**, which includes employed people and unemployed people seeking and available for work. It does not describe a third of employed workers or a third of all adults. The rounded magnitude is reasonable; the category needs correcting. [Statistics South Africa’s definitions](https://www.statssa.gov.za/publications/P0211/P02111stQuarter2026.pdf)

## Clarity flags

1. **The education explanation can sound like the narrator endorses the hierarchy.**

   > “A child taught for less would grow up fit for less, and that was the point.”

   “Fit for less” is ambiguous between having fewer opportunities and being worth less. Attribute the intended restriction to the planners and describe the opportunities or jobs being withheld.

2. **The new pronoun transition obscures Mandela.**

   > “The plan did not last long. He was caught in 1962, after seventeen months on the run.”

   The preceding paragraph’s subjects are the Congress and the new organisation. Reintroduce Mandela by name. “The plan did not last long” also leaves unclear whether the arrests ended sabotage, the avoidance of casualties, or the hope of negotiations.

3. **An unrestricted maxim replaces the regime’s specific reasoning.**

   > “A foreigner, after all, has no claim to a vote.”

   Specify that this was the apartheid government’s rationale for denying South African political rights. As a universal statement, it fails: some countries allow qualifying noncitizen residents to vote nationally. [New Zealand Electoral Commission eligibility rules](https://vote.nz/enrolling/get-ready-to-enrol/are-you-eligible-to-enrol-and-vote?gclsrc=aw.ds&lang=en-NZ)

4. **A breakable absolute contradicts the later prison account.**

   > “Nobody was sure what he now looked like.”

   His wife, visitors, prison staff, and officials who met him knew. The intended subject is the public or most of the waiting crowd.

5. **Several additions announce or repeat rather than connect.**

   > “Its roots went back a long way.”

   > “The land law, in effect, did two jobs at once.”

   > “What came out was hard to bear.”

   The first adds no information before the historical rewind; the second announces the explanation immediately following; the third announces the emotional force of evidence that already supplies it. These are the clearest padding/signpost candidates.

6. **Some added conclusions explain what the reader has just understood.**

   > “Black South Africans did not need the remark explained to them.”

   > “The vote itself was the moment the whole long struggle had been about.”

   > “After four years of killing, that calm was remarkable in itself.”

   These repeat the force of the preceding or following material without supplying a missing reasoning step.

7. **The same summary formula recurs.**

   > “In effect, the country had been forbidden to know him.”

   > “The land law, in effect, did two jobs at once.”

   > “He was telling the judge, in effect, that he would not plead for his life.”

   Together with “In other words,” “Put another way,” and “Both sides, in other words,” this creates a repeated rhythm of statement followed by explanatory restatement.

8. **Unexplained terminology remains.**

   > “What is the use, he asked, of teaching a Bantu child mathematics when it cannot use it in practice?”

   A general reader needs to know that “Bantu” here is the regime’s terminology for Black Africans. “Pass bureaux” likewise needs an immediate plain-language explanation.

9. **The unnamed officeholders become hard to track.**

   > “The prime minister of the day told white voters that they must adapt or die.”

   > “In August 1985 the president announced that he would make a major speech…”

   These refer to the same man, P. W. Botha, but the change of title is unexplained. He recurs through the speech, tea meeting, stroke, and succession, so naming him meets the style sheet’s own exception for recurring people.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions, or explicit chapter cross-references.

## Overall

The revision improves pacing but is not entirely fact-neutral. Correct the new claims about residence, forced removals, simultaneous foreign abandonment, and inability to punish. Several other additions can be trimmed because they repeat conclusions the narrative already makes clear. No files were edited.
