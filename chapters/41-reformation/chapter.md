# 41. Why are there Protestants and Catholics?

*Germany and western Europe, from 1450 to 1648*

Because in 1517 a German friar attacked the way the church sold forgiveness, and the church could not silence him as it had silenced others. Printing presses spread his words faster than they could be burned, and German princes protected him for reasons of their own.

The oldest piece of European printing from movable type that carries a date is not a book. It is a form, a single sheet of parchment printed on one side in the German city of Mainz in the autumn of 1454. The text is thirty-one lines long, and in the middle of it the printer left three gaps. A clerk filled these in by hand with a name and a date.

The form was an indulgence, and to understand it one needs to know what western Christians believed would happen to them when they died. A few saints would go straight to heaven, and the wicked would go to hell. Almost everybody else, the ordinary run of believers who had sinned and been forgiven, would first spend time in a place called purgatory, where the stain of their sins was burned away. The process was thought to be painful and long. Preachers spoke of thousands of years.

The church taught that it could shorten the sentence. The pope had charge of a store of merit, built up by Christ and the saints, and he could apply it to a sinner's account. A document that did this was an indulgence. In earlier centuries it had been given for going on crusade. By the 1400s it was given for a donation to a good cause, and the form of 1454 was issued to raise money for the defence of Cyprus against the Turks. The buyer paid, and the clerk wrote in the name.

Such forms had always been copied out by hand, one at a time. A press in Mainz turned them out by the thousand, each exactly like the last. It was almost certainly the press of a goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg, and indulgences were among the first paying jobs that his new invention found. Sixty years later the same invention would be turned against the same product, and the church would be split in two.

## Letters of lead

A European book before Gutenberg was a manuscript, written out by a copyist with a pen. A fast copyist could finish a few pages in a day, and a Bible took him a year or more. Books were therefore rare and costly, and most were owned by monasteries, cathedrals and the very rich.

The Chinese had been printing for seven hundred years by carving a whole page into a block of wood, and had experimented with separate characters that could be rearranged. The Koreans had gone further, and were printing books from cast metal characters before Gutenberg was born. Chinese writing, however, uses thousands of different characters, which makes a set of movable pieces enormous. An alphabet needs only a couple of dozen letters, used over and over. Gutenberg's achievement was a way of making those letters in metal, cheaply and by the thousand.

He began with a rod of hard steel. On its tip he carved a single letter, back to front, as fine as the best handwriting. This was the punch. He hammered it into a small bar of soft copper, which left a hollow in the shape of the letter. He then clamped the copper into the bottom of a little hand-held box and poured in molten metal, a mixture of lead and tin that melts on a kitchen fire and sets in a second. Out dropped a small stick with the letter standing up on its end. A workman could cast them one after another all day, all identical.

To print a page, a second workman picked the letters out of a case one by one and set them in rows in a frame, with blank pieces between the words. The frame was locked tight and dabbed with a sticky ink made from lamp soot and linseed oil. A damp sheet of paper was laid over it, and the whole thing was slid under a heavy screw press of the kind that vineyards used for squeezing grapes. One pull of the lever made one page. When enough copies had been taken, the letters were sorted back into the case and used again.

Two men at a press could print more in a day than a copyist wrote in a year. Gutenberg's first great book, a Bible in two volumes, came out around 1455 in about a hundred and eighty copies. It still cost about three years of a clerk's pay. Printing was an expensive business, since a printer had to buy metal, presses and a mountain of paper before he sold a single copy, and Gutenberg himself was ruined by his debts and lost his workshop to the man who had lent him the money.

The method spread all the same, carried by his workmen from town to town. By 1500 there were presses in more than two hundred European cities, and they had produced something like twenty million books, which was more than all the copyists of Europe had written in the thousand years before. The price of a book fell to a fraction of what it had been.

## Back to the sources

Much of what those presses printed was old. For about three hundred years the universities of Europe, at Paris, Oxford, Bologna and elsewhere, had trained their students on the Bible and on the Greek philosopher Aristotle. Their professors had built a vast and careful system that reconciled the two. Everything was read in Latin, including the Bible, which the church used in a Latin translation made eleven centuries earlier.

In the 1400s a new kind of scholar appeared, first in Italy. These men loved the writers of ancient Rome and Greece, and they wanted to read them in the original words, free of the errors that centuries of copying had let in. They hunted through monastery libraries for old manuscripts and compared them line by line. Printing suited them perfectly, because once a text had been corrected, a thousand identical copies could be sent out and no copyist could spoil them.

The same methods could be turned on the church's own documents. For centuries the popes had claimed the right to rule central Italy on the strength of a charter in which the emperor Constantine, on becoming a Christian, had given them the western half of the Roman empire. In 1440 an Italian scholar examined its Latin and showed that it was full of words and titles that did not exist in Constantine's day. It was a forgery, made four or five hundred years after his death.

In 1516 the most famous of these scholars, a Dutchman named Erasmus, printed the New Testament in its original Greek, with a fresh Latin translation beside it. Readers could now see for themselves where the church's old version had gone astray. In one place, where the old Latin had Jesus telling people to "do penance", which sounded like an instruction to go to a priest, the Greek said something closer to "change your hearts".

## The coin in the chest

In 1514 a young German nobleman named Albrecht wanted to become archbishop of Mainz, the most important church office in Germany. He was already archbishop of another city, he was twenty-three, which was below the legal age, and holding two such posts together was forbidden. The pope was prepared to overlook all three difficulties for a very large fee.

Albrecht borrowed the fee from the Fuggers, a family of bankers in Augsburg who were the richest merchants in Europe. To help him repay it, the pope allowed him to sell a new indulgence throughout his lands. Officially the money was for rebuilding the church of St Peter in Rome. Privately it was agreed that half would go to Rome and half to Albrecht, to pay off the Fuggers, and an employee of the bank travelled with the collection chest and held one of its keys.

The selling was put in the hands of a friar named Tetzel, who was good at it. He entered each town in procession, with the pope's document carried on a velvet cushion, and preached in the market square. He told his listeners that this indulgence would work for the dead as well as the living, so that for a few coins they could release a dead mother or father from the fire. A rhyme went around that summed up the offer: as soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs.

Tetzel was not allowed into Saxony, the territory next door, whose prince did not want his subjects' money leaving the country. People crossed the border to buy from him anyway. Some of them brought their certificates home to the town of Wittenberg and showed them to their priest, as proof that they no longer needed to confess.

The priest was Martin Luther. He was a miner's son, thirty-three years old, a friar, and the professor of Bible at Wittenberg's small new university. For years he had been tormented by the fear that nothing he did could make him good enough for God. Reading the letters of Saint Paul, he had come to a conclusion that calmed him. A person is not saved by anything he does or pays. He is saved by trusting God, and that trust is itself a gift. If this was true, the men preaching the indulgence were doing real harm, because they taught people to put their faith in a receipt.

On 31 October 1517 Luther wrote out ninety-five points for debate, in Latin, and sent them to Archbishop Albrecht with a polite letter. By tradition he also nailed a copy to the door of the castle church, which served as the university's notice board. One of the points asked why the pope, if he really had power to empty purgatory, did not do it for love and not for money. Another observed that the pope was richer than the richest man of ancient Rome and might build St Peter's with his own money.

## Read aloud in the tavern

Nobody came to debate. What happened was that printers in three cities got hold of the ninety-five points and ran them off, and somebody put them into German. Within two weeks they were being talked about all over Germany, and within a month they had reached the rest of Europe. Luther was astonished, and a little alarmed. Then he saw what the press could do, and for the rest of his life he used it better than anyone else alive.

He stopped writing only for scholars. He wrote short, in German, in the language of the market, with jokes and insults. A typical piece was a pamphlet of eight or sixteen pages with no cover, which a printer could set and run off in a day or two and which a craftsman could afford. Printers loved him, because he sold out. In the first few years he produced a new title every couple of weeks, and by one count a fifth of everything printed in German in those years carried his name, more than the next seventeen reformers put together.

Most Germans could not read. A pamphlet was therefore read aloud, in a tavern, a workshop or a spinning room, by the one person present who could, and it was then argued over by everyone. For those who could not follow the words there were pictures. Luther's friend, the painter Lucas Cranach, ran a workshop in Wittenberg, and it turned out cheap woodcuts in pairs. In one, Christ washes the feet of the poor, and in the one beside it, the pope holds out his foot to be kissed by kings.

The press had no preference for the truth. The same shops printed a picture of a monster said to have been found dead on the river bank at Rome, with the head of a donkey and the body of a woman covered in scales, and Luther's circle published it with an explanation that God had sent it as a portrait of the pope. Luther's opponents printed that he was the child of a demon. Late in his life Luther himself wrote a vicious book urging princes to burn the synagogues of the Jews and drive them out, and that was printed and reprinted too.

Rome moved slowly, and then it did what it had always done. The pope declared Luther a heretic and ordered his books burned. Luther burned the pope's order in public. In 1521 he was summoned before the emperor, Charles V, a young man of twenty-one. Charles was king of Spain and ruler of the Netherlands, Austria and much of Italy. In Germany he bore the old title of Roman emperor. Germany, however, was a patchwork of several hundred princedoms and free cities, and the emperor presided over their rulers more than he commanded them. Standing in front of the assembled princes, with his books piled on a table, Luther was asked whether he would take them back. He answered that unless he was shown to be wrong from the Bible, he could not, because his conscience was captive to the word of God.

A century earlier, a Czech priest named Jan Hus had said very similar things, been summoned to a very similar hearing, and been burned alive. Luther was not burned, and the first reason is that his writings were by now in circulation all over Germany, and he was the most famous man in Germany. The second reason was his prince.

The ruler of Saxony, Frederick, was one of the seven men who elected the emperor, and the emperor needed him. Frederick was a pious Catholic who owned one of the largest collections of holy relics in Europe. He was also proud of his university and tired of seeing German silver carried over the Alps. On the road home from the hearing, Luther's wagon was stopped by armed horsemen who dragged him off into the forest. They were Frederick's men. They took him to a castle on a hilltop, where he lived for ten months disguised as a knight.

He spent the time translating. Working from the Greek text that Erasmus had printed, he put the New Testament into German in eleven weeks. It went on sale in September 1522, and the first printing was gone within weeks. German Bibles had been printed before, in stiff, word-for-word versions that few people read. Luther's sounded like speech. A German farmer or shoemaker who could read, or who knew someone who could, was now able to find out for himself what the book said.

## Smite, slay and stab

One thing the book said, as some readers understood it, was that all Christians were equal before God. Luther had written that a Christian is a free lord of all and subject to no one. He meant the freedom of the soul. Many of his hearers took him to mean something more practical.

German peasants in the 1520s had real grievances. Their lords were raising rents, closing off the forests and streams where villagers had always cut wood and fished, and in some places forcing free farmers back into serfdom. In the spring of 1525, the villagers of southern Germany drew up a list of twelve demands and had it printed. Twenty-five thousand copies were sold in two months. The third demand said that serfdom must end, since Christ had bought all people with his blood, the shepherd as well as the lord. The list ended by offering to withdraw any demand that could be shown to be against scripture.

Well over a hundred thousand peasants took up arms across Germany. They burned castles and monasteries, and they looked to Luther to speak for them.

Luther was horrified. He believed that rebellion against rulers was rebellion against God, and he could see that if his movement became a peasants' revolt, the princes would crush both together. He wrote a pamphlet urging the lords to kill the rebels like mad dogs. Let everyone who can, he wrote, "smite, slay and stab".

The princes did not need telling. Their hired soldiers and cannon met the peasant bands in the open and butchered them. Something like a hundred thousand people were killed in a few months. The serfs of Germany stayed serfs. From then on Luther's reform was a movement of princes and town councils, and the poor did not forget it.

## The prince's opportunity

For a ruler, the new teaching had attractions that had nothing to do with salvation. The church was the largest landowner in Europe. It had its own courts and its own taxes, it was excused much of what other landowners paid to the king, and it sent a stream of money to Rome. Bishops and abbots were chosen with the pope's agreement. If Luther was right, however, the life of a monk was no holier than the life of a cobbler, and prayers could not buy anyone out of purgatory, so that monasteries had no purpose. Nor had the pope, since nobody stood between a believer and God. A prince who adopted the reform could close the monasteries in his territory, take their land, and appoint the clergy himself.

One after another the rulers of northern Germany did so, followed by the kings of Sweden and Denmark. In 1529, when the emperor tried to stop the process, a group of German princes signed a formal protest, and from this their party got the name Protestant.

The most thorough of them was not a follower of Luther at all. Henry VIII of England had written a book against Luther and received a title from the pope for it. In the 1530s, however, he wanted his marriage ended so that he could try for a son with another wife, and the pope would not oblige. Henry had Parliament declare that the king was head of the church in England. He then closed all of the country's monasteries, nunneries and friaries, some eight hundred of them, and took their estates, which together made up the largest landholding in the kingdom after his own. Most of it he sold to pay for his wars. The gentlemen who bought it had from then on the strongest possible reason never to let the old church come back.

In Germany the emperor fought the Protestant princes and beat them in battle, and still could not stamp out their church. He was at war with France and with the Ottoman sultan, whose army reached the walls of Vienna, and he could never give the matter his full attention. In 1555 the two sides made peace on a simple principle. Each prince would choose between the old church and Luther's, and his subjects would follow him or move away. Faith was to be decided by the ruler.

The old church, meanwhile, set its own house in order. A council of bishops met on and off for eighteen years in the Alpine town of Trent. It forbade the taking of money for indulgences and ordered every bishop to found a school for training priests. On doctrine it gave no ground at all. A new order of priests, the Jesuits, was founded to teach, argue and win back what had been lost, and the pope issued a list of books that no Catholic might print or read. From this point on there were organised camps, each sure that the others were leading souls to hell. The Protestants were themselves divided. A second reforming movement, begun in the Swiss cities and led from Geneva by a French lawyer named John Calvin, differed from Luther on important points, and it was spreading through France, the Netherlands and Scotland.

## Out of the window

The peace of 1555 lasted sixty years in Germany. Elsewhere the fighting went on. In France, Catholics and Protestants fought eight civil wars in a generation, and over a few days in 1572, soldiers and mobs in Paris murdered about three thousand Protestants. The Dutch rose against their Catholic king in Spain and fought him for eighty years.

The last and worst of these wars began in Prague in May 1618. A crowd of Protestant nobles, angry that their Catholic king was closing their churches, marched into the castle and threw two of his governors and a secretary out of an upstairs window. All three fell about twenty metres and lived. Catholics said that angels had caught them. Protestants said that they had landed in a dung heap.

The war that followed lasted thirty years and was fought almost entirely on German soil. Armies of hired soldiers, who were seldom paid, lived by stripping the countryside, and hunger and plague travelled with them. In 1631 a Catholic army stormed the Protestant city of Magdeburg, and of its twenty-five thousand people about five thousand were alive when the fires went out. By the end, Germany had lost about a third of its population, and some regions had lost half.

Many of those who died believed, sincerely, that the fate of their souls and their children's souls depended on the outcome. The men who directed the war had other things in mind as well. France was a Catholic kingdom, and its government was run by a cardinal of the Roman church. For the second half of the war, this cardinal paid the Protestant armies and then sent French troops to fight beside them, because the emperor was a rival of France and a weak, divided Germany suited him. By then the war was about which kings would dominate Europe.

The peace that ended it in 1648 admitted as much. It fixed the religious map of Germany roughly as it had stood before the worst of the fighting. Each prince kept charge of the church in his own territory, Calvin's followers were admitted alongside Catholics and Lutherans, and people who found themselves on the wrong side of a border were allowed to worship at home or to leave unharmed. The pope denounced the treaty, and nobody took any notice.

Western Christians had looked to one church for a thousand years, and they were now permanently divided. The division showed even in the date. In 1582 the pope had corrected the old Roman calendar, which had drifted out of step with the sun, by striking ten days from the month of October. Catholic countries adopted the change within a few years. Most Protestant ones refused to take the date from Rome. For the next hundred and twenty years, a traveller in Germany who walked from a Catholic town to a Protestant one, a morning's journey down the same road, arrived ten days before he had set out.
