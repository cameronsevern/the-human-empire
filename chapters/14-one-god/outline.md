# Chapter 14 — Where did the belief in one God come from?

**Line under the title:** Judah and Babylon, from about 2,850 years ago to about 2,450 years ago.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,500 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Accurate, even-handed and respectful: beliefs are described, neither mocked nor endorsed. No modern politics.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

In the ancient world a god belonged to a land, a people and a temple, and when a kingdom was destroyed its god usually faded with it. The small kingdom of Judah was destroyed by Babylon about 2,600 years ago: its king a captive, its temple burned, its leading families marched to Iraq. Its exiles reached a different conclusion. Their prophets had said beforehand that their god, Yahweh, would send Babylon as a punishment, and so the disaster became proof of his power. From there it was a short step to saying that a god who commands empires is the only god there is. In exile and in the two centuries after, scribes gathered the old laws, histories and prophecies into a set of scrolls, written in an alphabet of twenty-two letters that ordinary people could learn. A people that had lost its king, its land and its temple came to be defined by a text that could be copied and carried anywhere. Persia let some of them go home and rebuild; the faith no longer depended on it.

## The story

1. **Oil for the king of Judah.** Answer first. The ration tablets from Babylon and the captive king they name.
2. **A god for each land.** Israel and Judah among the small kingdoms. Yahweh as one national god among many: the Moabite king's stone, the jars that bless by "Yahweh and his Asherah", and the Bible's own complaints. Assyria destroys the north, and its deportees vanish.
3. **Twenty-two letters.** The alphabet in Judah. How a scroll is made, written and read. Why almost none survive, and why copying matters more than durability. The king who burned a scroll.
4. **We cannot see Azekah.** Babylon takes Jerusalem twice. The Babylonian chronicle and the Book of Kings side by side. The letters from Lachish, the ash layer, the baked sealings.
5. **Whose god lost?** The two usual explanations for defeat, and why total defeat normally ended a god's worship. The prophets' answer, and the step from "our god did this" to "there is no other god".
6. **Judah-town.** The exiles as farmers on the canals: the Al-Yahudu tablets. What can be kept without a temple. What Babylon left behind in the calendar and the script.
7. **The making of a book.** Gathering and editing. The seams that show. Where scholars disagree about dates and which way the evidence leans. A law that belongs to no king.
8. **The king of Persia.** The Cyrus Cylinder and the prophet: two peoples, one conqueror. The trickle home, the modest temple, the communities that stayed abroad.
9. **At the Water Gate.** A scribe reads a scroll aloud to a crowd. What lasted.
