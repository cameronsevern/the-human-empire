I read the style guide and outline, checked chapter 14 against chapters 9–11, and verified the points below. No files were edited. Findings are ordered by seriousness.

1. **Factual error: the central rule about conquered gods does not hold.**

   > “When a kingdom was wiped out, neither explanation could keep a god alive for long.”

   Ashur’s worship survived the destruction of the Assyrian kingdom by centuries; his temple was rebuilt under later rulers. Babylon’s destruction in 689 BCE was likewise explained as Marduk’s anger, without ending his worship. Judah’s survival needs explaining, but religious extinction cannot serve as the normal outcome against which it is measured. **Confidence: high.** Sources: [Karen Radner, “Assur’s ‘Second Temple Period’”](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/36940/1/Radner_Herrschaftslegitimation%20in%20vorderorientalischen%20Reichen%20der%20Eisenzeit%20%282017%29%20Assur%27s%20Second%20Temple%20Period.pdf); [Metropolitan Museum, Esarhaddon’s restoration inscription](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/321907).

2. **Doubtful causal claim: divine control of foreign kings does not establish monotheism.**

   > “In that case, there was nothing left for Marduk to be.”

   This does not follow from Yahweh commanding Babylon. A god could direct foreign rulers while other gods continued to exist—the chapter’s own Cyrus Cylinder passage demonstrates precisely that understanding of Marduk. The move from supreme power to sole existence is an additional theological claim, not the next necessary inference. The chapter should distinguish the exilic prophet’s declaration from the narrator’s reconstruction of how people reached it. **Confidence: high about the logical gap; moderate about the historical pathway.** [Esarhaddon’s inscription](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/321907) supplies another concrete example of divine punishment within a polytheistic framework.

3. **Doubtful factual claim: the northern exiles’ religious disappearance is asserted without evidence.**

   > “Within a few generations, the deported Israelites can no longer be traced.”
   >
   > “They had married their neighbours and taken up the gods of the places where they lived.”

   Assyrian documents identify Israelites and descendants through Yahweh-bearing names into the seventh century BCE. Eventual loss of identifiable records does not establish wholesale abandonment of Yahweh, or intermarriage as its cause. These sentences make an uncertain outcome carry the chapter’s central comparison. **Confidence: high that the stated conclusion exceeds the evidence.** Sources: [ORACC, Israel](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/aebp/Essentials/Countries/Israel/index.html); [Ran Zadok’s study of documentation through 602 BCE](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.5615/bullamerschoorie.374.0159).

4. **Doubtful factual claims: religious life in exile is presented as directly documented and uniform.**

   > “They rested on every seventh day.”
   >
   > “They gathered to pray and to hear the old words read.”

   The tablets establish economic activity and continuing Yahweh-bearing names much more securely than communal worship arrangements. Some Babylonian Judean transactions actually occurred on the Sabbath. Regular assemblies for scriptural reading during the exile should not be presented as established by this evidence. **Confidence: high about the evidentiary limitation; moderate about what particular communities practised.** Sources: [Laurie Pearce, “Judean Life in Babylonia”](https://thetorah.com/article/judean-life-in-babylonia); [Tero Alstola, “Everyday Life in Exile”](https://anetoday.org/alstola-exile-babylon/).

5. **Doubtful factual claim: alphabet size is made to prove widespread access to readers.**

   > “It can be practised wherever there is a copy and somebody who can read it, and twenty-two letters meant that there usually was.”

   Knowing twenty-two letters does not establish functional literacy, access to manuscripts, or the presence of a competent reader in most communities. The extent of literacy remains uncertain, and ancient Israelite culture was predominantly oral. The portable-text argument works without this unsupported demographic conclusion. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Posen Library, “Literacy and Literary Culture in Ancient Israel”](https://www.posenlibrary.com/guide/literacy-and-literary-culture-ancient-israel).

6. **Factual error and chronological confusion: Jehoiachin’s removal did not end the kingdom.**

   > “That was how a kingdom ended, and in the ancient world the end of a kingdom was normally the end of its god as well.”

   The immediate antecedent is Jehoiachin’s deportation in 597 BCE. Judah continued under Zedekiah until Jerusalem’s destruction in 587/586 BCE—as the chapter later explains. The opening merges two different defeats, making the later return to the first siege harder to follow. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Alstola’s account of Jehoiachin’s captivity and Zedekiah’s installation](https://anetoday.org/alstola-exile-babylon/).

7. **Unsupported absolute: worship abroad was not an entirely unknown possibility.**

   > “Nobody knew whether Yahweh could be worshipped at all outside his own country.”

   “Nobody” is indefensible. The biblical tradition itself depicts Naaman planning sacrifices to Yahweh after returning to Syria, using earth taken from Israel. That story does not establish how all exiles thought, but it shows that worship abroad and its practical requirements could be imagined. The historical question concerns how displaced communities maintained worship, not whether anyone knew it was possible. **Confidence: high that the absolute is unsustainable.** Source: [2 Kings 5:17–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Kings+5%3A17-19&version=NRSVUE).

8. **Tension with chapter 9, and an overbroad contrast.**

   > “Everything about the scene would have puzzled a priest of Marduk.”
   >
   > “Its contents are not a secret kept by priests, and are read out to farmers and their wives, who are expected to understand them and to live by them.”

   Chapter 9 already tells readers that Hammurabi invited wronged people to have his laws read aloud, and stresses that publicly accessible rules mattered even when people could not read. Public explanation of an authoritative text therefore cannot be presented as wholly alien to Babylonian religious culture. The second sentence is not itself false, but its placement creates that misleading contrast. This is the clearest tension with chapters 9–11; I found no comparably clear contradiction with chapters 10 or 11.

9. **Factual overstatement: the Babylonian tablet does not cover the whole reign.**

   > “A Babylonian clay tablet, now in the British Museum, lists the chief events of each year of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign.”

   The surviving tablet covers 605–594 BCE, ending in his eleventh regnal year, not his entire reign. **Confidence: high.** Source: [British Museum, “The Life and Times of Nebuchadnezzar II”](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/the-life-and-times-of-nebuchadnezzar-ii/6AFSiGg9EQkQ7Q?hl=en).

10. **Factual imprecision: Atenism did not end at Akhenaten’s death.**

    > “Some eight centuries earlier, a king of Egypt had ordered his country to worship the disc of the sun alone, and the order had died with him.”

    The reversal occurred under his successors, particularly Tutankhamun, rather than at the moment Akhenaten died. “Was overturned soon after his death” would preserve the point accurately. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Metropolitan Museum, “Tutankhamun’s World”](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/perspectives/tutankhamuns-world).

11. **Reader orientation: the scroll example silently jumps forward several centuries.**

    > “One famous scroll, found in a cave near the Dead Sea, is made of seventeen pieces of sheepskin sewn together.”

    The Great Isaiah Scroll dates to about 125 BCE, roughly four centuries after the exile. In a section describing writing in monarchic Judah, an undated physical example can easily be mistaken for a surviving scroll from that period. Identify it as a much later example. **Confidence: high.** Source: [Israel Museum’s Great Isaiah Scroll exhibition introduction](https://www.imj.org.il/sites/default/files/IMJ%20Great%20Isaiah%20Scroll%20Exhibition.pdf).

12. **Broken absolutes, contradicted by the chapter’s own example.**

    > “Because a scroll wears out, a text on a scroll survives in one way, which is by being copied.”
    >
    > “A text that nobody troubles to copy is gone within a few lifetimes.”

    The ancient Dead Sea manuscript just described survived physically for more than two millennia. Copying explains continuing transmission and use; it is not the only way a manuscript or its text can survive. These violate the explicit prohibition on absolutes that one exception breaks. **Confidence: high.** The [Israel Museum’s dating](https://www.imj.org.il/sites/default/files/IMJ%20Great%20Isaiah%20Scroll%20Exhibition.pdf) supplies the counterexample.

13. **Internal contradiction and broken absolute: writing was not the sole surviving bond.**

    > “The kingdom was gone, and the written memory of it was all that remained to hold the people together.”

    The preceding section supplies several other bonds: families, neighbouring settlements, inherited names and religious customs. The sentence also conflicts with the immediately preceding suggestion that people carried traditions “in their heads.” Written memory could become increasingly important without being all that remained.

14. **Reader confusion: residence and worship are conflated.**

    > “Nevertheless, most of the people who worshipped there lived, for most of the year or for all of their lives, somewhere else.”

    “There” means the Jerusalem temple, but people living elsewhere “for all of their lives” could not literally worship there. The sentence also introduces an unexplained majority claim without a defined period. Distinguish people who visited Jerusalem from those who remained abroad and regarded its temple as sacred.

15. **Reader orientation and the plain-words rule: the central religious role is never explained.**

    > “To most historians, they were the ordinary religion of the time, and the new thing was the demand, pressed by a party of prophets and priests, that Israel worship Yahweh alone.”

    “Prophets” first appears here without explanation. For a reader without biblical background, it can mean simply people who predict the future. Explain that these were people who claimed to speak messages from God; that makes Jeremiah’s political accusations and competing prophets intelligible. “Party” also risks suggesting a formal political organisation.

16. **Style: unverifiable claims about everyone’s beliefs and access to records.**

    > “Nobody in Judah doubted that Chemosh was real.”
    >
    > “Two accounts, written by enemies who never read each other’s records, agree on what happened to him.”

    Neither universal claim is needed. The first turns evidence of widespread religious assumptions into knowledge of every individual’s beliefs. The second turns agreement between accounts into certainty about their authors’ access to records. State the prevailing assumption and the agreement; “nobody” and “never” add unsupported claims.

17. **Style: explicit signposting and a small self-reference.**

    > “The reason lies in what the captives decided their defeat meant.”
    >
    > “The Book of Jeremiah tells a story that makes the point.”
    >
    > “Scholars think it was pronounced Yahweh, and that is the form used here.”

    The first two announce an explanation or illustrative story instead of delivering it, contrary to the signpost rule. The final clause of the third discusses the text’s own naming choice and can simply go. I found no hedging stacks or explicit chapter-number cross-references.
