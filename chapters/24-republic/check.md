I found no clear contradiction with chapters 22–23, and no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-reference, or body cross-references. The findings below are ranked by seriousness. No files were edited.

1. **Doubtful central premise — high confidence that the account is too settled.**  
   > “Rome was grinding up the class of people it depended on for soldiers, and the officials who held the count every five years could see the numbers falling.”

   The preceding farmer’s story turns a disputed explanation into an established process. Long service did not necessarily leave farms unworked: households could continue farming, and declining census registrations do not by themselves establish the disappearance of smallholders. Nathan Rosenstein’s *Rome at War* directly challenges this traditional account. The safe conclusion is that landholding and recruitment caused political anxiety; the chapter’s specific mechanism needs qualification or stronger evidence. [Rosenstein, *Rome at War*](https://uncpress.org/9780807864104/rome-at-war/).

2. **Doubtful military transformation — high confidence.**  
   > “What had been an emergency measure quickly became the normal way to fill a legion.”

   Marius recruited propertyless volunteers in 107 BCE, but this does not establish a permanent switch to predominantly landless professional volunteers. Conscription continued, and the idea of a single “Marian reform” creating armies personally dependent on generals is disputed. Even Sallust gives competing explanations for Marius’s decision, so the earlier assertion that he “could not find enough men with the property to qualify” is also too definite. [Sallust, *Jugurthine War* 86](https://lexundria.com/sal_jug/86/r); [François Gauthier, “The Transformation of the Roman Army in the Last Decades of the Republic”](https://www.academia.edu/129798932/Gauthier_F_2020_The_Transformation_of_the_Roman_Army_in_the_Last_Decades_of_the_Republic_in_J_Armstrong_M_Fronda_eds_Romans_at_War_Soldiers_Citizens_and_Society_in_the_Roman_Republic_London_New_York_283_296_Sample_page_).

3. **Overstated resolution of the chapter’s central problem — high confidence.**  
   > “The landless soldier now had his future guaranteed, and he no longer needed an ambitious general to win it for him.”

   Augustus established a more regular system of service and discharge rewards; he did not make its promises reliably enforceable. Immediately after his death, soldiers mutinied over prolonged service, inadequate rewards and poor retirement land. The military treasury also dates to AD 6, decades after his accession: the narrative makes a gradual settlement sound immediate and complete. [Tacitus, *Annals* 1.17](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Tacitus/Annals/1B%2A.html); [Cassius Dio 55.23–25](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/55%2A.html).

4. **False absolute in the opening — high confidence.**  
   > “An army in that position belongs to its commander, and from then on Rome was ruled by whoever commanded the most soldiers.”

   Military strength became decisive, but numerical superiority did not determine the winner. Caesar defeated Pompey’s larger army at Pharsalus; Caesar’s own figures give Pompey roughly twice his infantry. “Whoever commanded the most soldiers” therefore breaks the style rule against an absolute that one exception defeats, besides overstating the historical argument. [Caesar, *Civil War* 3.88–89](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Civil_Wars/3D%2A.html).

5. **Misleading account of Gaius’s citizenship proposal — medium-high confidence.**  
   > “He also proposed something that his brother had not dared: to give Roman citizenship to the Italian allies.”

   This collapses different categories of allies. The securely attested proposal offered Roman citizenship to the **Latins**; the treatment envisaged for other Italians is less certain and is commonly reconstructed as an extension of Latin rights. Plutarch and Appian specifically identify the Latins. Also, “had not dared” assigns Tiberius a motive the chapter has not established. [Plutarch, *Gaius Gracchus* 8](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Caius_Gracchus%2A.html); [Appian, *Civil Wars* 1.23](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Appian/Civil_Wars/1%2A.html).

6. **Wrong circumstances of Gaius’s death — high confidence about the surviving account.**  
   > “Gaius died in the street fighting that followed, and three thousand of his supporters were put to death afterwards without trial.”

   In Plutarch’s account, Gaius escaped the fighting and died in a sacred grove at the hands of his servant, who then killed himself. He was not killed fighting in the streets. The compressed sequence also obscures that the final confrontation occurred in 121 BCE, after his two tribuneships. [Plutarch, *Gaius Gracchus* 16–18](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Caius_Gracchus%2A.html).

7. **Misleading description of Egypt’s status — high confidence.**  
   > “The two killed themselves, and Egypt, the richest country of the Mediterranean, became Octavian's personal possession.”

   Egypt became a Roman province under Octavian’s unusually close control, administered through his appointed prefect. Calling the entire country his personal property confuses political control with ownership. Augustus himself describes adding Egypt to the empire of the Roman people; research on its administration also challenges the older picture of an entirely exceptional private domain. [*Res Gestae* 27](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Augustus/Res_Gestae/5%2A.html); [Dominic Rathbone, “Egypt, Augustus and Roman taxation”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ccgg_1016-9008_1993_num_4_1_1372?pageId=T1_82).

8. **Chronology obscured: auxiliary citizenship — high confidence.**  
   > “A provincial who served for twenty-five years was made a Roman citizen on his discharge, and his children with him.”

   In this placement, readers will attribute the regular entitlement to Augustus. Its regularization is generally associated with **Claudius**, and actual service could exceed twenty-five years. “The arrangement that grew out of this” hints at later development but never tells the reader when. Add that distinction. [Myles Lavan, “The Spread of Roman Citizenship, 14–212 CE,” especially the discussion of auxiliary service](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/12646/Lavan_2016_PandP_SpreadRomanCitizenship_RevisedAAM.pdf?sequence=1).

9. **Incorrect “first” — high confidence.**  
   > “He had himself named dictator, first for ten years and then for life, and his head appeared on the coins, which no living Roman had done before.”

   A living Flamininus appeared on Greek gold coins around 196 BCE. Caesar’s innovation needs the narrower scope of portraiture on **Rome’s own coinage**. This is exactly the sort of exception-sensitive absolute prohibited by `style.md`. [British Museum, gold stater of Flamininus](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/gold-stater-in-the-name-of-titus-quinctius-flamininus/sAFDd20xSH02QA?hl=en).

10. **Incorrect claim of no precedent — high confidence that the wording fails.**  
    > “Then Gracchus announced that he would stand for a second year as tribune, which was also without precedent.”

    Roman tradition supplied precedents: Livy describes Licinius and Sextius repeatedly securing re-election. Their early history is not beyond dispute, but that makes “without precedent” less defensible, not more. Tiberius’s bid was controversial and lacked a recent accepted precedent. [Livy 6.35](https://livy.hermitsh.ai/en/works/ab-urbe-condita-06/).

11. **Incorrect tax coverage — high confidence.**  
    > “To pay for it, Augustus created a separate military treasury and gave it two new taxes of its own, a twentieth of every large inheritance and a hundredth of every sale at auction.”

    “Every large inheritance” is wrong: Dio explicitly exempts very close relatives as well as poor recipients. A large inheritance passing within the exempt family relationship was not automatically taxable. The sentence also compresses the establishment and assignment of the taxes into one event. [Cassius Dio 55.25.5](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/55%2A.html).

12. **Misleading census denominator and timing — high confidence.**  
    > “Before the war, the count had found about four hundred thousand citizens.”  
    > “The next full count found more than nine hundred thousand.”

    These are counts of **adult male citizens**, not the entire citizen population. The roughly 910,000 figure belongs to 70/69 BCE, nearly twenty years after the main citizenship legislation. There was an intervening census in 86/85, whose coverage and transmitted total present problems. “Next full count” conceals the gap instead of explaining it. [Wolfgang Blösel, census table and discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/rome-and-the-making-of-a-world-state-150-bce20-ce/city-of-rome-scene-of-politics-and-growing-metropolis/6248904FDF5D1A1D83AB182CF5A3101B).

13. **Reader loses the geography at the Rubicon.**  
    > “The boundary of his province was a small river in northern Italy called the Rubicon.”

    The only province previously assigned to Caesar in the narrative is in southern Gaul. Readers have no way to understand why its boundary lies in northern Italy. Explain that he also governed **Cisalpine Gaul**, the Roman province in northern Italy, and that the Rubicon marked its boundary with Italy proper. [Suetonius, *Julius Caesar* 22](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Julius%2A.html).

14. **Reader loses the opposing side in Caesar’s civil war.**  
    > “The civil war lasted four years and was fought from Spain to Egypt.”

    Caesar fights unnamed “enemies in the Senate,” then wins. Pompey never appears, so the chapter’s crucial example of competing military power lacks the opposing commander and his army. One sentence identifying Pompey as Caesar’s former partner and now his principal military opponent would make the conflict intelligible without adding a survey. [Caesar’s *Civil Wars*, introduction](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Civil_Wars/Introduction%2A.html).

15. **The citizenship example appears to demonstrate the opposite of its introduction.**  
    > “What a citizen could expect instead is shown by a case from Sicily a few decades later.”

    What follows is a citizen being flogged and crucified—the very abuse citizenship supposedly prevented. The intended distinction is between lawful protection and a governor’s criminal violation of it, followed by prosecution. Make that distinction before the anecdote. Otherwise the reader must repair the argument while reading.

16. **Clear signpost sentence.**  
    > “The proposal that sank Gaius Gracchus deserves a closer look, because it concerned half the Roman army.”

    “Deserves a closer look” announces the exposition rather than delivering it. This directly breaks `style.md`’s prohibition on signpost sentences. The substantive point—that the citizenship dispute concerned men supplying half Rome’s army—can stand on its own.
