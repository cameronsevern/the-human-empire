The diff changes meaning in places and introduces a few weaker sentences. I found no clearly broken neighbouring antecedent, but “So much is flattering” loses the explicit setup it previously answered. Diff-specific findings are marked below. I read all seven chapters in order; no files were edited.

1. **Human self-domestication is presented as established history.** [04-war/chapter.md:78](chapters/04-war/chapter.md:78): “Slowly, as a result, the temper itself was bred out.” And [line 89](chapters/04-war/chapter.md:89): “We did it to ourselves, one dead bully at a time.”

   The preceding ethnographic killing does not establish hundreds of thousands of years of genetic selection. Even the theory’s proponent presents this as a hypothesis among competing explanations. [Wrangham, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31481917/).

   **Replace:** “One hypothesis is that generations of resistance to violent bullies helped make humans less quick to lash out.” Replace the second sentence with: “On this account, people helped tame one another.”

2. **Chapter 7 turns a reconstruction of kingship into an observed sequence.** [07-kings/chapter.md:27](chapters/07-kings/chapter.md:27): “Nobody in that arrangement was a king.” [Line 31](chapters/07-kings/chapter.md:31): “So when war threatened, a city chose a commander.”

   These categorical statements carry the entire temporary-commander-to-permanent-king story. The evidence does not securely establish that sequence; royal-looking figures already appear in Uruk-period art, before explicit textual descriptions of rulers. [Getty’s account of the evidence](https://www.getty.edu/art/exhibitions/mesopotamia/explore.html).

   **Replace:** “The earliest evidence does not tell us clearly how power was divided between rulers, temple households and councils.” Then: “War gave military leaders another source of power: command of armed men.” The subsequent paragraphs need to remain an explanation of how power could accumulate, rather than a documentary reconstruction.

3. **The opening answer about war exceeds the evidence.** [04-war/chapter.md:5](chapters/04-war/chapter.md:5): “Yes.” [Line 22](chapters/04-war/chapter.md:22): “A discovery like that settles an old argument, and it helps to know what the argument was.”

   Nataruk cannot establish that humans have *always* fought wars. Its interpretation as a single unburied massacre is itself disputed. This is an argument-bearing disagreement that style.md says to acknowledge. [Original report](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16477); [published challenge](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature19778).

   **Replace the opening:** “People fought long before there were states, although nobody knows when the first war began.”  
   **Replace the second sentence:** “The excavators read Nataruk as a massacre; other archaeologists dispute whether all the bodies belong to one violent event.”

4. **The farming opening confuses deliberate cultivation with unintended consequences.** [05-farming/chapter.md:5](chapters/05-farming/chapter.md:5): “They did not mean to.” [Line 18](chapters/05-farming/chapter.md:18): “People were harvesting grain and grinding it twelve thousand years before anyone farmed.”

   People could deliberately sow plants without intending the eventual transformation of their society. Moreover, Ohalo II—the chapter’s chosen demonstration—has published evidence interpreted as experimental cultivation. [Snir and colleagues](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-e81a-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b).

   **Replace:** “They meant to grow food; they did not foresee how completely it would change their lives.”  
   **Replace the second sentence:** “People were harvesting and grinding wild grain thousands of years before farming became their main way of life.”

5. **Göbekli Tepe is made to prove too much.** [03-believe/chapter.md:163](chapters/03-believe/chapter.md:163): “They had no farms.” [Line 176](chapters/03-believe/chapter.md:176): “Here the great shrine came before the farms, raised by people who still hunted for a living.”

   Wild plant remains do not exclude cultivation, and hunting does not exclude farming. The older account of an isolated shrine has also been revised by discoveries of domestic buildings. [UNESCO’s current description](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1572/); [excavation report](https://publications.dainst.org/journals/efb/article/download/2596/7095).

   **Replace:** “They still depended heavily on wild plants and hunted animals.”  
   **Replace the second sentence:** “Here people raised monumental communal buildings while the region was moving from gathering wild food towards farming.”

6. **Grave goods are treated as proof of a specific afterlife belief.** [03-believe/chapter.md:35](chapters/03-believe/chapter.md:35): “Second, it says that the person has gone somewhere, since nobody packs a spear for a man who has simply stopped.”

   This excludes grief, commemoration, social identity and other explanations without evidence. The Sungir research emphasises varied treatment and social meanings, not a recoverable statement of doctrine. [Trinkaus and Buzhilova](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2017.223).

   **Replace:** “A spear in a grave may have equipped the dead for another life, or marked who that person had been among the living.”

   The same correction is needed at line 16, particularly “Nobody works that hard for a corpse.”

7. **The extinction argument claims a decisive experiment that it does not have.** [02-everywhere/chapter.md:164](chapters/02-everywhere/chapter.md:164): “Islands settle the argument, because people reached them late, when the climate was steady.” [Line 171](chapters/02-everywhere/chapter.md:171): “Where people had not arrived, the animals lived on.”

   Wrangel Island’s mammoths eventually disappeared too, and the cause remains unresolved. Their survival demonstrates that extinction happened at different times; it does not settle every continental extinction’s cause. [Research on the final population](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38942016/).

   **Replace:** “Islands offer some of the clearest evidence of what human arrival could do, although they do not explain every extinction.”  
   **Replace the second sentence:** “Some isolated populations survived thousands of years after their mainland relatives disappeared.”

8. **Demographic explanations become unsupported claims that violence did not occur.** [02-everywhere/chapter.md:136](chapters/02-everywhere/chapter.md:136): “In each valley the newcomers raised a few more children than the people who were there before, and that is all it takes.” [05-farming/chapter.md:188](chapters/05-farming/chapter.md:188): “It would be easy to imagine a war of conquest behind that, but killing was not what did it.”

   The first is a model narrated as an observed event; the second wrongly treats population replacement as evidence excluding violence. Demographic modelling allows multiple explanations for Neanderthal extinction, while ancient DNA establishes ancestry changes rather than a universally peaceful mechanism. [Neanderthal modelling](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31774843/); [Neolithic population turnover](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06862-3).

   **Replace:** “Even a small difference in births and deaths could, over many generations, have helped the newcomers outnumber their neighbours.”  
   **Replace the farming sentence:** “The incoming farmers changed the population, but DNA alone cannot tell us how often they took land by force.”

9. **The childbirth explanation presents a disputed mechanism as settled.** [01-what-is-a-human/chapter.md:54](chapters/01-what-is-a-human/chapter.md:54): “The way out was to give birth early, long before the baby was ready for the world.”

   The claim that pelvic constraints shortened human gestation is contested; maternal energetic limits are another major explanation. [Dunsworth and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22932870/).

   **Replace:** “Human babies arrive with much of their brain growth still ahead of them.” This supports the ensuing account of dependence without requiring the disputed causal story.

10. **The loss of mobility changes date between chapters.** [04-war/chapter.md:178](chapters/04-war/chapter.md:178): “Whoever planted one would have to stay beside it, and be ready to fight for it.” But [06-taxes/chapter.md:48](chapters/06-taxes/chapter.md:48) says: “The farmers of the Middle East had grown grain for four thousand years and kept it, because they could still walk away.”

   The distinction is between abandoning a particular harvest and losing the practical ability to establish a livelihood elsewhere. Currently the chapters contradict rather than develop one another.

   **Replace the chapter 4 sentence:** “A field gave its owners more to lose by leaving, although many could still abandon it and start elsewhere.”

11. **A hypothetical bowl count becomes asserted evidence—partly through the diff.** [06-taxes/chapter.md:18](chapters/06-taxes/chapter.md:18): “A heap of ten thousand ration bowls means that somebody was feeding people by the thousand, day after day.”

   The diff removes “Think about,” making an illustrative quantity sound like a documented assemblage. Even ten thousand bowls would not establish how many people ate simultaneously or how regularly. Nor would rations establish that recipients never grew food themselves.

   **Replace the paragraph:** “The bowls suggest that food was being prepared or distributed on a large scale. That required people to collect supplies and organise who received them.”

12. **The chapter’s ‘unchanged body’ argument contradicts its own examples.** [02-everywhere/chapter.md:19](chapters/02-everywhere/chapter.md:19): “In other words, humans did not adapt to the world in the way other animals do.”

   Lines 124 and 205 subsequently describe inherited adaptations to altitude and sunlight. Cultural adaptation was crucial, but it did not replace biological adaptation.

   **Replace:** “Humans adapted through their bodies as well as their knowledge, but learned skills let them enter new environments far faster than evolution alone could.”

13. **The climate is made implausibly dependable.** [05-farming/chapter.md:74](chapters/05-farming/chapter.md:74): “We take a steady climate for granted, because it is the only kind that our written history has known.” [Line 78](chapters/05-farming/chapter.md:78): “Nothing now stood in the way of doing the same the next year, and the year after.”

   Relative Holocene stability is not an absence of abrupt change, drought or crop failure. [Holocene climate analysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-50886-w).

   **Replace:** “The climate became more stable than it had been during much of the ice age, though droughts and sudden changes still came.”  
   **Replace the second sentence:** “More dependable seasons made repeated sowing a better gamble.”

14. **The origin of writing is universalised from one regional history.** [06-taxes/chapter.md:146](chapters/06-taxes/chapter.md:146): “It was invented to keep accounts.”

   This is defensible as a compressed account of early Mesopotamian writing, not of every independent invention of writing. [British Museum curator Ilona Regulski’s overview](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/43506/chapter/364131674).

   **Replace:** “In southern Mesopotamia, writing first appears chiefly in accounts.”

15. **The animal-language contrast is too crude.** [01-what-is-a-human/chapter.md:109](chapters/01-what-is-a-human/chapter.md:109): “The closest that other animals come is a handful of calls.”

   Other animals combine calls in meaningful sequences; the contrast does not require denying that complexity. [Experimental evidence from Japanese tits](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms10986).

   **Replace:** “Other animals have calls and sometimes combine them, but human language lets us describe an extraordinary range of things that are absent, imagined or still to come.”

16. **The chimpanzee-learning claim needs updating.** [01-what-is-a-human/chapter.md:18](chapters/01-what-is-a-human/chapter.md:18): “Even so, their tricks mostly stay at about the level a clever animal could invent for itself.”

   “Mostly” prevents a strict contradiction, but the sentence presents a disputed account as the explanation of the human difference. Experiments show chimpanzees learning a complex skill they failed to innovate independently. [Van Leeuwen and colleagues](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01836-5).

   **Replace:** “They can learn difficult skills from one another, but human knowledge accumulates across generations on a far greater scale.”

17. **The American transport passage obscures llamas and repeats chapter 2.** [05-farming/chapter.md:138](chapters/05-farming/chapter.md:138): “The later peoples of the Americas had fields of maize and potatoes and almost nothing to pull a plough, carry a pack or ride into battle.”

   Llamas were consequential pack animals, not a negligible exception. The horse-and-plough point already appears at 02:151. [Linden-Museum’s account of llama domestication and use](https://ccc.deutsche-digitale-bibliothek.de/en/item/PT56P5JIBWZMJWITBBZWCSJYD2NVZ3QD).

   **Replace the repeated closing passage:** “In the Andes, llamas carried loads, but the Americas had no counterpart to the ox at the plough or the horse under a rider.”

21. **The Egyptian conquest is treated as a literal caption to a disputed historical reconstruction.** [07-kings/chapter.md:113](chapters/07-kings/chapter.md:113): “That conquest was recorded on a slab of dark stone, about two thirds of a metre tall, which was found in a temple in the south of Egypt.”

23. **The diff strengthens the claim about ethnographic analogy.** [01-what-is-a-human/chapter.md:174](chapters/01-what-is-a-human/chapter.md:174): “Still, they faced the same problems with much the same means, and they are the best guide we have.”
