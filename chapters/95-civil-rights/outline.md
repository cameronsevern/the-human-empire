# Chapter 95 — How did Black Americans win their civil rights?

**Line under the title:** The United States, from 1954 to 1968.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

They won them by organising. For ten years, ordinary people in the American South broke unjust laws in the open, without hitting back, and did it in places and in ways that made the violence of the system visible to the whole country on television. A national government that was competing with the Soviet Union for the good opinion of Africa and Asia could not afford the pictures, and in 1964 and 1965 it passed laws that ended legal segregation and gave Black southerners the vote. The method worked against laws. It worked much less well against poverty, bad housing and the police in the cities of the North, and the movement's leader was murdered while he was trying to turn it to those.

## The story

1. **Opening: a seat on a bus.** Answer first. Montgomery, 1 December 1955, and the women who had a plan ready.
2. **Separate.** What the South had built since the end of slavery: the signs, the vote, the rope. Six million leave. A war against a racist enemy.
3. **A doll.** The court ruling of 1954 and the lawyers behind it. "All deliberate speed." A boy from Chicago.
4. **Three hundred and eighty-one days.** The boycott: car pools, a young preacher, a bomb, a victory.
5. **Workshops.** The method, and where it came from: Gandhi, the churches, and rehearsals for being spat on. Four students at a lunch counter. Buses.
6. **Children.** Birmingham, 1963: a letter from a cell, fire hoses, and a president's change of mind. A murder in a driveway.
7. **The dream.** The march of August 1963 and who organised it. Four girls in a church.
8. **Two laws.** 1964: the act. Mississippi in summer; a sharecropper on television. Selma, a bridge, a film interrupted. 1965: the vote.
9. **By any means necessary.** Those who rejected the method. The North: Watts, Chicago, Detroit. What the police files held.
10. **Memphis.** The last campaign and the murder.
11. **What it changed.** The gains, the limits, and who borrowed the method.
12. **Ending: the bridge.**
