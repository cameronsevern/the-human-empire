## Factual flags

1. **Major — the new transition reverses the Montgomery chronology.**

   > “It indicted the leaders under an old law against boycotts. Then the attacks turned violent.”

   King’s home was bombed on **30 January 1956**; the boycott indictments followed on **21 February**. The preceding version placed the events in the same order, but “Then” newly makes that order an explicit chronological claim. Sources: [King Papers introduction documenting the bombing](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/introduction-volume-vi-advocate-social-gospel), [the indictment](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/indictment-state-alabama-v-m-l-king-jr-et-al-facsimile).

2. **Major — the new Northern comparison excludes something that demonstrably existed.**

   > “There the barriers were not posted on signs, so there was no law to break in public.”

   Northern activists could confront trespass laws, restrictions on demonstrations, and court orders while protesting discrimination. Chicago’s school authorities sought an injunction against the 1965 school boycott, for example. The absence of an explicit segregation sign did not remove opportunities for civil disobedience.

   This is a **new regression**, not a repetition of the earlier check’s already-fixed wording about Northern barriers never having legal foundations. Source: [National Archives, Chicago School Boycott](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/desegregation/chicago.html).

3. **Minor — filibustering was not the opponents’ sole remaining tactic.**

   > “The bill's opponents had one weapon left, which was talk.”

   Opponents also tried to weaken or block the bill through amendments. Southern senators continued that effort after cloture ended unlimited debate. The correct claim is that the filibuster was a major remaining obstacle, not their only weapon. Source: [U.S. Senate history of cloture and final passage](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/landmark-legislation/civil-rights-act-of-1964/cloture-and-final-passage-of-the-civil-rights-act-of-1964-essay.htm).

4. **Minor — enactment is presented as completed compliance.**

   > “The lunch counters were open now.”

   Some establishments continued refusing Black customers after 2 July 1964. In *Katzenbach v. McClung*, the record includes a refusal of counter service on **3 July**, the day after signing. The law required integration of covered establishments; it did not instantly achieve universal compliance. Source: [the government’s Supreme Court brief](https://clearinghouse-umich-production.s3.amazonaws.com/media/doc/90920.pdf).

5. **Minor — the sentence identifies the wrong word.**

   > “Southern politicians took the second word as their text, and moved as slowly as they could.”

   In the preceding quotation, “with all deliberate speed,” the second word is **“all.”** The intended word, **“deliberate,” is third**. Naming it directly would remove the error. Source for the wording: [National Archives, Brown decision timeline](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/brown-v-board/timeline.html).

6. **Minor — Johnson did not end his speech with the anthem.**

   > “He ended with the words of the movement's own anthem. We shall overcome, he said.”

   Johnson did say “And we shall overcome,” but continued for a substantial portion of the address afterward. His closing passage concerned God’s favour toward the undertaking. The quotation is accurate; its placement is not. Source: [LBJ Library, complete 15 March 1965 address](https://www.lbjlibrary.org/object/text/special-message-congress-american-promise-03-15-1965).

## Clarity flags

- **Explicit signposts prohibited by the style sheet:**  
  > “The truth was more interesting.”  
  > “What followed tested how far ordinary people would go.”  
  > “What it cost to try to vote can be seen in one life.”

  These announce the significance of the next passage instead of supplying context or stating the substantive point.

- **Checklist-style announcement:**  
  > “The principle was the same in both countries, and it works in three steps.”

  “It works in three steps” announces a numbered explanation. The following sentences already explain the mechanism.

- **Repeated setup:**  
  > “Outside the jail, the campaign was running short of people. The adults of Birmingham were running out of volunteers willing to lose their jobs.”

  “Running short” and “running out” repeat the same point immediately. The second sentence supplies the useful explanation.

- **Low-information padding:**  
  > “Its reach was wide.”  
  > “He took the hint. He put the pages aside.”

  The legislation’s provisions demonstrate its reach; putting the pages aside demonstrates King’s response. These additions do little to orient the reader.

- **An unexplained mechanism at a decisive moment:**  
  > “In the end the talking was stopped, and in July 1964 the Civil Rights Act became law.”

  The passive wording hides how the obstacle was overcome. A general reader needs to know that senators voted to limit debate.

- **An ambiguous pronoun:**  
  > “This time the cameras were there, and the commissioner had given them exactly what the movement needed. It led the television news across America that night.”

  “It” has no clear singular antecedent. The intended subject is the footage of police violence.

- **An overly broad comparison:**  
  > “His message was the opposite of King's.”

  The following passage contrasts methods, self-defence, and attitudes toward white America. “His message” also encompasses racial dignity and advancement, making the scope of “opposite” unclear.

- **An exception-vulnerable absolute:**  
  > “Children had no jobs to lose, so the organisers turned to the schools.”

  The relevant distinction is that schoolchildren generally faced less employment risk than adult breadwinners. “Children had no jobs” unnecessarily universalises it.

- **Competing formulations of the chapter’s own argument:**  
  > “The method depended on the other side behaving badly in public.”

  The conclusion says it “worked best” under those conditions. Dependence and favourable conditions are different claims. The Freedom Riders passage also explicitly says they had the law on their side, complicating the earlier instruction to “Break an unjust law openly.”

No hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-reference, or body cross-references found.

## Overall

The revision preserves the numerical and quoted material, but it is not wholly fact-neutral: several bridges introduce new claims, including a reversed chronology and a renewed error about Northern protest. Most earlier corrections remain intact. The main stylistic problem is repeated announcement and explanation of points the scenes already make. No files were edited.
