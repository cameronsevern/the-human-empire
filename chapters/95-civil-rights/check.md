1. **Factual error; high confidence.** “There the barriers had never been written into law.”

   Northern segregation also had legal and governmental foundations: court-enforced housing restrictions, discriminatory federal mortgage policies, and earlier legally sanctioned school segregation. Boston’s segregated schools, for example, were upheld in court before Massachusetts prohibited them in 1855. The distinction between Southern laws and Northern private prejudice is too clean and distorts the chapter’s explanation of the movement’s limits. Sources: [Massachusetts Archives](https://www.sec.state.ma.us/divisions/commonwealth-museum/exhibits/online/freedoms-agenda/freedoms-agenda-8.htm), [Federal Reserve history of redlining](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/redlining).

2. **Doubtful causal rule; high confidence that it is overstated.** “The injustice had to be written into a law that could be publicly broken.” “The opponent had to be someone who would answer with visible violence.” “And a higher authority had to exist that could be shamed into acting.”

   These describe one effective strategy, not necessary conditions for nonviolent action to succeed. Economic pressure, negotiation, litigation and withdrawal of cooperation also mattered. Birmingham deliberately targeted merchants during the Easter shopping season; declining business helped bring negotiations. Chapter 76 already explains non-cooperation through boycotting cloth and withdrawing Indian assistance, so this narrower rule creates a conceptual inconsistency unless explicitly confined to particular American campaigns. Source: [King Institute, Birmingham Campaign](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/birmingham-campaign).

3. **Misstatement of a ruling; high confidence.** “In 2013 the Supreme Court struck down the central provision of the Voting Rights Act, on the ground that it was no longer needed.”

   *Shelby County v. Holder* invalidated Section 4(b)’s coverage formula, which determined which jurisdictions needed advance federal approval for voting changes. The majority held that the formula did not reflect current conditions. It expressly left Section 5 itself undecided and Section 2’s nationwide prohibition intact. Explain the approval mechanism before explaining its disabling; otherwise readers cannot understand what was lost. Source: [the Court’s opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-96).

4. **Misleading chronology; high confidence.** “Six million Black southerners left for the cities of the North and West, where they could vote and where the factories were hiring.” “Then came a war against a regime founded on a theory of racial superiority.”

   The six million is the total for roughly **1910–1970**, not migration completed before World War II. “Then came” places most of that movement too early. Source: [National Archives, The Great Migration](https://www.archives.gov/research/african-americans/migrations/great-migration).

5. **Factual error; high confidence.** “The first ten rows were reserved for white passengers.”

   It was the first **ten seats**, not ten rows. Parks sat immediately behind those seats. Source: [National Archives’ account and bus diagram](https://www.archives.gov/press/press-releases/2006/nr06-08).

6. **Overstatement and continuity problem with chapter 64; high confidence.** “A government in Washington that was competing with the Soviet Union for the good opinion of the world could not live with the pictures, and in 1964 and 1965 it passed laws that ended legal segregation and gave Black southerners the vote.”

   “Gave … the vote” obscures the distinction between establishing a right and enforcing it. Chapter 64 has already explained the Fifteenth Amendment and Black men voting and holding office during Reconstruction. The 1965 act made existing constitutional protection effective against systematic obstruction. “Ended legal segregation” also suggests completion across every field; federal protection against most housing discrimination followed in 1968. Sources: [National Archives, Voting Rights Act](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/voting-rights-act), [HUD history](https://www.hud.gov/aboutus/history).

7. **Overbroad legal claim; high confidence.** “It banned segregation in hotels, restaurants, theatres and all public places, and discrimination in employment on grounds of race, religion or sex.”

   The 1964 act covered defined categories of public accommodations, with exceptions; it did not simply cover “all public places.” Its employment provisions also had coverage limits. “All” creates precisely the exception-vulnerable absolute forbidden by `style.md`. Source: [the act’s text, particularly Titles II and VII](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/civil-rights-act).

8. **Overbroad legal claim; high confidence.** “It abolished the tests and sent federal officials to register voters wherever local ones would not.”

   The 1965 act suspended tests and authorized federal examiners through specified coverage and enforcement mechanisms, not wherever registration was obstructed. The nationwide literacy-test ban followed in 1970. Sources: [National Archives, original act](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/voting-rights-act), [Justice Department legislative history](https://www.justice.gov/crt/history-federal-voting-rights-laws).

9. **Overstated assessment; medium-high confidence.** “Where one of those was missing, as in Albany or in Chicago, it failed.”

   Chicago produced a housing agreement whose implementation proved deeply disappointing, but also organizing gains and some success against employment discrimination through Operation Breadbasket. Albany secured few immediate gains, while local organizing continued after King departed. “Failed” needs a specified objective and timescale; neither case establishes the preceding universal rule. Sources: [King Institute, Chicago Campaign](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/chicago-campaign), [Albany Movement](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/albany-movement).

10. **Factual error in the scene; high confidence.** “A few hours after that speech, the leader of the movement in Mississippi was shot dead in his own driveway, in front of his children.”

    Medgar Evers’s children were inside when the shot was fired; they took cover and subsequently came outside. He died at the hospital. “Shot dead … in front of his children” gives the reader a different scene. Source: [National Park Service’s account](https://www.nps.gov/memy/learn/historyculture/assassination.htm).

11. **Participation conflated with arrests; high confidence.** “On 2 May more than a thousand children, some as young as six, walked out of a church in ranks, singing, and were arrested.”

    More than a thousand students participated, but **hundreds**, rather than the entire thousand-plus, were arrested that day. The sentence makes the participation total the arrest total. Source: [King Institute, Birmingham Campaign](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/birmingham-campaign).

12. **Incorrect distribution timing; high confidence.** “Her students delivered them before dawn.”

    Robinson’s memoir describes distribution by herself and two students on Friday morning, including delivery to a ministers’ meeting. The leaflets were reproduced overnight; distribution was not completed before dawn by her students. Source: [Jo Ann Robinson’s memoir, “The Boycott Begins”](https://www.nationalhumanitiescenter.org/pds/maai3/protest/text5/robinsonbusboycott.pdf).

13. **Undated statistic; high confidence that it needs a year.** “The typical white family in America still owns about eight times as much as the typical Black one.”

    “Still” turns a dated estimate into a present-day claim. In the Federal Reserve’s **2022** survey, median family wealth was approximately $285,000 for white families and $44,900 for Black families—about **6.3 times**, not eight. Specify the survey year and that the measure is median net worth. Source: [Federal Reserve analysis](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/greater-wealth-greater-uncertainty-changes-in-racial-inequality-in-the-survey-of-consumer-finances-20231018.html).

14. **Reader confusion and inaccurate institutional description.** “All this time the head of the national police had been treating King as an enemy of the state.”

    The United States has no single “national police” commanding the local forces encountered throughout the chapter. Identify the FBI as the federal investigative agency and its director as J. Edgar Hoover. Otherwise the following reference to “the bureau” has no clear antecedent, and readers may mistake Hoover for the superior of Birmingham’s or Selma’s police.

15. **Missing narrative connection.** “In November 1956 the Supreme Court ruled that segregated buses were illegal.”

    Readers have followed Parks’s arrest and the boycott, but no lawsuit has been introduced. Explain that a separate challenge brought by Black women reached the courts and supplied the legal victory. Without that bridge, the Supreme Court appears to intervene spontaneously, and the relationship between organizing and litigation disappears.

16. **Missing context for a pivotal appearance.** “She told her story on national television.”

    Explain why Hamer had a national audience: she was testifying at the Democratic convention’s credentials hearing, challenging Mississippi’s all-white delegation. Without the occasion and objective, the passage becomes an isolated account of suffering rather than an example of political organizing.

17. **Unexplained historical term.** “Younger activists dropped non-violence and raised the slogan of Black Power.”

    The reader is never told what Black Power meant. Its placement makes it sound synonymous with adopting violence, although the slogan also concerned political control, independent institutions and racial pride. Explain the relevant demand and specify which activists rejected nonviolence; the present collective wording flattens substantial differences.

18. **Unsupported absolutes forbidden by `style.md`.** “And in the new contest with the Soviet Union, every lynching was reported at length by Moscow radio to the peoples of Africa and Asia.” “It was on every television screen in America that night.”

    Both claims require literal universality that the narrative neither needs nor establishes. Describe Soviet exploitation of American racial violence and the wide television circulation of Birmingham’s images without “every.”
