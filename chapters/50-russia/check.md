1. **Factual error; high confidence.** “For the first time since riders had appeared on the steppe, there was no independent power on it.”

   The destruction of the Zunghar empire did not end independent government across the Eurasian steppe. Kazakh rulers retained substantial autonomy despite nominal submission to Russia or the Qing; Russian conquest and administrative incorporation continued into the nineteenth century. The defensible claim is that the last great steppe empire capable of challenging the Qing had disappeared. This also requires correcting the opening’s claim that Russia and China “divided the grasslands of Asia between them.” [Source: *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Central Asia.”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/central-asia-vii/)

2. **Factual error; high confidence.** “Most of it is still the border between Russia and China, or between each of them and Mongolia.”

   The chapter has described Nerchinsk, but that treaty’s Amur boundary was substantially overturned in 1858–60, when Russia acquired territory north of the Amur and east of the Ussuri. The Mongolian frontier also requires the later settlement at Kiakhta in 1727. “Shifted here and there” understates major territorial changes and leaves the reader with the wrong map. [Sources: Harvard’s Nerchinsk exhibit](https://russiaglobal.omeka.fas.harvard.edu/exhibits/show/trajecmain/item/18), [Dartmouth’s account of the Amur acquisitions](https://collections.dartmouth.edu/archive/text/arctica/diplomatic/EA10-04-diplomatic.html).

3. **Factual error; very high confidence.** “He had farmers growing grain in the oases, smiths casting cannon, and Swedish prisoners of war, bought from the Russians, teaching his men to make muskets.”

   The Swedish specialists belong to Galdan’s successors. Johan Gustaf Renat was captured by Russia in 1709 and by the Zunghars in 1716; Galdan died in 1697. Renat helped develop cannon production under Tsewang Rabdan and Galdan Tseren. “Bought from the Russians” also misdescribes his capture. [Source: Göran Bäärnhielm’s study of Renat’s maps.](https://goran.baarnhielm.net/Renat/index.html)

4. **Factual overgeneralisation and internal contradiction; high confidence.** “They had no towns and no rulers above the level of a clan chief, and their weapons were bows and spears.”

   This describes all Siberia immediately after giving its total population. Yet the chapter has already introduced Sibir’s khan, capital and tributary peoples. Siberia contained different political and economic societies; the description must be confined to particular forest communities. The opening’s “nobody in between had guns or a government that could stop them” similarly turns Russian military advantages into an absence of organised opposition. [Source: *The Encyclopedia of Empire*, “Sibir’, Khanate of.”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781118455074.wbeoe190)

5. **Factual error; high confidence.** “Wheat does not ripen in the northern forest, and every sack of flour had to be hauled from Russia.”

   Northern outposts depended on imported provisions, but those provisions did not all come from European Russia. Grain farming developed within Siberia, including around Yeniseysk in the 1620s and Krasnoyarsk in the 1630s. The preceding “One thing Siberia could not give them was bread” confuses the northern hunting frontier with the whole region. [Source: John F. Richards, *The World Hunt*, “The Hunt for Furs in Siberia.”](https://paperzz.com/doc/8806638/the-hunt-for-furs-in-siberia---university-of-california-p...)

6. **Misleading explanation; high confidence.** “What had changed was that a rich state with a paid army could now carry its food and its guns to wherever the horsemen were, and keep them there.”

   The 1696 campaign does not demonstrate that unrestricted capability. Qing forces themselves approached exhaustion of their supplies; the emperor’s column withdrew, while the western column intercepted Galdan. Logistics mattered enormously, but remained a severe constraint. The sentence also makes the next sixty years of Zunghar survival harder to understand. [Source: Peter Perdue, “Military Mobilization in Seventeenth and Eighteenth-Century China, Russia, and Mongolia.”](https://www.battle-of-qurman.com.cn/literature/Perdue-ModernAsianStudies-1996.pdf)

7. **Factual distortion; high confidence.** “The Russians were so few, in such a vast country, that they could not possibly trap the fur themselves.”

   Russian commercial hunters did trap substantial quantities themselves, working in organised parties. Tribute extraction existed alongside private hunting and trade. This sentence wrongly makes indigenous tribute the necessary substitute for Russian trapping, rather than one major part of the business. [Source: Oleg Bychkov, “Russian Hunters in Eastern Siberia in the Seventeenth Century,” hosted by Fort Ross Conservancy.](https://www.fortross.org/lib/50/russian-hunters-in-eastern-siberia-in-the-17th-centurylifestyle-and-economy.pdf)

8. **Factual error and internal contradiction; high confidence.** “Sable had never been important to him, since reindeer and elk fed and clothed his family.”

   Indigenous participation in fur exchange and tribute preceded Russian conquest. The chapter itself says Sibir already collected fur from forest peoples. The change was the identity, demands and coercive reach of the collectors—not the sudden discovery that sable mattered. A particular hunter might previously have hunted little sable; the generic claim about forest peoples is untenable. [Source: Richards, “The Hunt for Furs in Siberia.”](https://paperzz.com/doc/8806638/the-hunt-for-furs-in-siberia---university-of-california-p...)

9. **Factual error; high confidence.** “For three hundred years the Russians had not been free to look.”

   Russian exploration, trade and military activity towards and beyond the Urals preceded Yermak. Muscovite expeditions reached northwestern Siberia before the sixteenth-century conquests of the Volga khanates, and northern commercial routes also bypassed them. Mongol domination constrained expansion; it did not prevent three centuries of looking east. [Source: Bychkov, “Russian Hunters in Eastern Siberia,” opening historical survey.](https://www.fortross.org/lib/50/russian-hunters-in-eastern-siberia-in-the-17th-centurylifestyle-and-economy.pdf)

10. **Contradiction with chapter 22; very high confidence.** “It was the first treaty that China had signed with a European state, and it treated the two rulers as equals, which was something the Chinese court granted to nobody else.”

    The problematic clause is “granted to nobody else.” Chapter 22 explicitly describes the Han–Xiongnu treaty: “The two rulers would count as brothers, as the kings of Egypt and Hatti had once done.” Restrict the claim to the particular diplomatic significance of Nerchinsk; do not make equality unprecedented throughout Chinese history. This also breaks the style rule against exception-vulnerable absolutes.

11. **Contradiction with chapters 22 and 37; very high confidence.** “Horsemen could retreat into the grassland faster than a farming army could follow, and a farming army that did follow soon starved, because the steppe grows nothing that soldiers can eat.”

    Chapter 22 already says: “In the largest campaign, two Han armies crossed the Gobi and broke the Xiongnu on their own ground.” Chapter 37 explains how livestock fed steppe armies, and chapter 22 mentions Xiongnu millet cultivation. The genuine difficulty was obtaining and transporting sufficient provisions, especially when an enemy removed its herds. The sentence converts that difficulty into an invariably fatal outcome.

12. **Treaty’s scope overstated; high confidence.** “Now there was a line, and a government on the far side of it that had agreed to send fugitives back.”

    Following the sentence about grasslands extending to the Caspian, this implies a closed frontier across Central Asia. Nerchinsk delimited a northeastern frontier, leaving even part of that region unresolved. Its extradition provision concerned subsequent fugitives between the two empires; earlier fugitives could remain. It did not eliminate every independent people’s route of retreat. [Source: Treaty of Nerchinsk, articles I–IV.](https://worldjpn.net/documents/texts/pw/16890827.T1E.html)

13. **Doubtful economic comparison; medium confidence.** “In Moscow in the 1600s, a single top-quality sable skin could sell for more than a craftsman earned in a year, and a man who came home from the forest with a bundle of forty was set up for life.”

    The lifetime-security claim needs evidence of its own. An exceptional skin’s Moscow retail price cannot be multiplied by forty ordinary skins and treated as a hunter’s net earnings. Recorded prices varied enormously by quality: one study gives forty-skin bundles ranging from fourteen to several hundred rubles. Keep a documented price comparison tied to a date, grade and market; the current inference is unsupported. [Source: “Gold-Boiling Mangazeya,” historical price table.](https://sciup.org/gold-boiling-mangazeya-a-legendary-city-of-the-russian-arctic-148331097?lang=ru)

14. **Factual wording error; high confidence.** “In the 1550s the first Russian ruler to call himself tsar, Ivan the Terrible, brought cannon down the Volga and took the khans' two capitals.”

    Ivan IV was the first Russian ruler formally **crowned** tsar, in 1547; Ivan III had already used the title. “First to call himself” is the wrong distinction. [Sources: Library of Congress country study](https://www.marines.mil/Portals/1/Publications/Soviet%20Union%20Study_1.pdf?ver=2012-10-11-163259-017), [Kremlin Museums’ coronation account](https://www.kreml.ru/en-Us/exhibitions/virtual-exhibitions.coronations-of-tsars-in-the-moscow-kremlin/venchanie-na-tsarstvo-ivana-groznogo/).

15. **Doubtful number; medium-high confidence.** “In 1639 a party of twenty Cossacks came down a mountain stream to a grey sea and built a hut on the shore.”

    Russian institutional accounts give Moskvitin approximately thirty men, or thirty-one including himself. Some English reference accounts do give twenty, so this is a discrepancy rather than a wholly invented number. “About thirty” or “a small party” is better supported. [Sources: Presidential Library](https://www.prlib.ru/section/2101584), [Russian Academy of Sciences’ Far Eastern Geological Institute](https://www.fegi.ru/primorye/history/geograph.htm).

16. **Internal contradiction; high confidence.** “The Manchus were neither a steppe people nor a farming one.”

    They have just been introduced as “hunters, farmers and horsemen.” The intended distinction seems to be that their society combined agriculture, hunting and military horsemanship. Denying the farming component obscures the very combination offered to explain their success.

17. **Reader orientation.** “They were on the second of the great rivers by 1619 and the third by 1632.”

    None of the three rivers has been named. A general reader cannot attach these milestones to a mental map. Identify the Ob, Yenisey and Lena in west-to-east order before referring to “second” and “third.” These names would earn their place by explaining the route.

18. **Reader orientation and missing connection.** “Tibet had already accepted a Qing garrison and a Qing official at Lhasa.”

    Tibet last appeared as the place where Galdan trained as a monk. Its sudden appearance as a Qing dependency leaves a major causal step unexplained, while “accepted” makes the process sound uncomplicatedly voluntary. A brief bridge should explain the Zunghar intervention and subsequent Qing military intervention. [Source: Perdue, “The Expansion of the Qing Dynasty of China and the Zunghar Mongol State.”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61799/chapter/546312989?login=false)

19. **Reader orientation.** “A branch of the western Mongols had migrated west long before, in the 1600s, and settled on the Volga under Russian rule.”

    The people carrying the ending are never identified as the Torghuts/Kalmyks. With Zunghars, eastern Mongols and western Mongols already in play, the reader can easily mistake them for returning Zunghar refugees. One identifying name and a clear statement of their relationship to the Zunghars would prevent that confusion.

20. **Style: unresolved source uncertainty in the narrative.** “Three years later the Tatars caught his camp asleep on a river bank, and Yermak drowned trying to swim to a boat, pulled under, by the usual story, by the weight of a coat of mail that the tsar had sent him as a gift.”

    “By the usual story” advertises unresolved doubt about a decorative detail. That conflicts with style.md’s instruction to settle minor uncertainty before writing or omit the detail. It is not a hedging stack; it is an uncertain anecdote left embedded in an otherwise factual account.
