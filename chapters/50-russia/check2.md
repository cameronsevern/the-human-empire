## Factual flags

1. **Major — Latin was not the only shared language.**
   
   > “Latin was thus the one language that both camps had in common.”
   
   Both sides had access to Mongolian interpretation; the Russians attempted to use it during the negotiations. Latin became the principal negotiating language through the Jesuits, not because no other shared language existed. The preceding unchanged sentence—“nobody on either side spoke the other's language”—also oversimplifies the available interpreters.
   
   **Correct fact:** The delegations chose Latin as their principal intermediary language despite having Mongolian available. Peter Perdue also records that the Qing had Russian translators whom they did not use. [Peter Perdue, *China Marches West*, p. 167](https://www.battle-of-qurman.com.cn/literature/Perdue-2005.pdf); [Ning Chia, “Language Translation and the Treaty of Nerchinsk”](https://journals.univie.ac.at/index.php/jeacs/article/download/10065/10272/32795).

2. **Major — the new bridge restores an overstatement that the earlier revision had narrowed.**
   
   > “A people that fled from one empire would find the other waiting.”
   
   In context, this makes Nerchinsk sound like a general closure of escape routes for steppe peoples. The preceding “in the east at least” had limited that claim; this new sentence broadens it again. It also clashes with the chapter’s later account of Zunghars fleeing to Russia and Torghuts receiving assistance from Qing officials.
   
   **Correct fact:** Nerchinsk established a northeastern boundary and required the return of subsequent fugitives between the two empires, while expressly allowing earlier fugitives to remain. It did not establish an impermeable frontier across the steppe or guarantee the return of every migrating people. This is a regression introduced by the new bridge, rather than a repetition of the already-corrected geographical wording. [Treaty of Nerchinsk, articles I–IV](https://worldjpn.net/documents/texts/pw/16890827.T1E.html).

3. **Minor — Russia had substantial exports besides fur.**
   
   > “Their country had no gold or silver mines at that time, and little else that foreigners wished to buy.”
   
   The problem is the second clause. Foreign demand also supported significant trade in leather and hides, hemp, flax, potash, tallow, wax, and other goods. Fur’s importance does not establish the near-absence of other desirable exports.
   
   **Correct fact:** Fur was a valuable export and source of state revenue within a broader export economy. A contemporary account from 1689 explicitly describes foreigners buying numerous Russian commodities. [Foy de la Neuville, *A Curious and New Account of Muscovy in the Year 1689*](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10078222/3/SSEES0007.pdf); [J. T. Kotilaine, *Russia’s Foreign Trade and Economic Expansion in the Seventeenth Century*](https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/4203/1/211%20.%20J._T._Kotilane.pdf).

4. **Minor — the Qing presence in Lhasa was not continuous from the initial intervention.**
   
   > “Tibet, which the Zunghars had invaded a generation earlier, had been occupied by a Qing army in reply, and a Qing official and garrison had sat in Lhasa ever since.”
   
   “Ever since” incorrectly compresses distinct stages into an uninterrupted arrangement. The intervention was in 1720; the garrison was withdrawn in 1723, and the permanent resident-commissioner arrangement developed later.
   
   **Correct fact:** Qing troops expelled the Zunghars in 1720, withdrew in 1723, and returned following the later Tibetan civil war; resident commissioners were appointed in 1727. The earlier check’s missing causal connection has been supplied, but this separate chronological error remains. [“China Consolidates Control over Tibet,” EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/china-consolidates-control-over-tibet); [Jeungah Yu, “On the Eve of Dispatching Ambans”](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/shigaku/113/12/113_KJ00003653188/_article/-char/en).

## Clarity flags

1. **Unclear antecedent**
   
   > “He also meant to enlarge it.”
   
   The immediately preceding sentence names “grain, guns and trade.” The intended antecedent, Galdan’s state, lies farther back. A reader has to reconstruct what “it” means.

2. **A moving wall**
   
   > “The wall did more than keep raiders out, because it did not stay where it was.”
   
   This initially suggests that the existing wall moved. The following sentence explains the actual process: new defensive lines were built farther south. The metaphor briefly obscures the mechanism.

3. **Repeated explanation**
   
   > “Latin was thus the one language that both camps had in common.”
   
   > “The negotiations were therefore conducted in Latin, with the Jesuits walking back and forth between the tents.”
   
   Besides the factual problem, consecutive “thus” and “therefore” sentences explain the same connection twice.

4. **Padding**
   
   > “He could not live on wages that did not come.”
   
   This repeats the immediately preceding statement that his pay arrived years late or never. The next sentence already explains how he supported himself.
   
   > “Its trouble there was an old one.”
   
   “For centuries” at the start of the following sentence supplies the same information more concretely.
   
   > “The same thing happened at one fort after another.”
   
   “The same thing” could mean unpaid wages, extortion, depletion, or moving onward. It adds repetition without identifying the process.

5. **Exception-vulnerable absolutes**
   
   > “It was a sign of submission that could be seen from across a street, and nobody could hide whether he had given it.”
   
   The added “nobody could hide” is unnecessary: conspicuousness does not establish impossibility of concealment.
   
   > “Every Chinese dynasty had faced this situation, and the difficulty was always the same.”
   
   “Every” and “always” turn a recurring strategic difficulty into a universal claim.
   
   > “For centuries the Tatars of the Crimea had ridden north every summer to seize Russian villagers and sell them in the slave markets of the Ottoman empire, and they had carried off people by the hundred thousand.”
   
   “Every summer” asserts an uninterrupted annual schedule. The argument needs recurrent raiding, not that absolute.
   
   > “They decided to do what herding peoples had always done, and move.”
   
   “Always” makes migration sound like the universal response of pastoral societies.

6. **Announcement-style signposting**
   
   > “One more people was to test the new order.”
   
   This announces the next example before introducing it. It is a clearer breach of the no-signposting rule than the bridges that directly explain causes or consequences.

I found no chapter self-reference, prohibited cross-reference, or hedging stack. Many new transitions do satisfy the style sheet’s instruction to give readers more room; they should not be cut merely for being explanatory.
