# 25. Why did the Roman Empire become Christian?

*Judea and the cities of the Roman Empire, from about 2,000 years ago to the year 337*

Because for three hundred years the Christian churches gave ordinary people in Roman cities a family of strangers that fed its poor, nursed its sick and buried its dead, together with a promise of life after death that was open to anyone. Then an emperor took their side.

In 1961, Italian archaeologists were digging in the ruins of a theatre at Caesarea, on the coast of what is now Israel. One of the steps turned out to be an older block of limestone that later builders had reused, with part of an inscription still on its face. It was a dedication, in Latin, of a building in honour of the emperor Tiberius, who came after Augustus. The second and third lines gave the name and rank of the official who had put it up: Pontius Pilatus, prefect of Judea.

It is the one physical trace of a man whose name is spoken aloud every week in churches all over the world. Pilate was a middle-ranking Roman officer who governed a small and troublesome province for about ten years. He is remembered because, one spring, he ordered the execution of a Jewish preacher from the northern hills.

The province was troublesome for a reason that the Romans never quite grasped. Rome had taken control of the country about ninety years before Pilate arrived, and it treated the Jews as it treated every other people in the empire. They could keep their customs and their god. The Romans were happy to respect any number of gods, since they had dozens of their own, and all they asked was that subject peoples should respect Rome's gods in return and offer sacrifices for the emperor.

The Jews could not do that. Their scrolls taught that there was one God, that all the others were nothing, and that he had chosen their people and given them a law to live by. He had one house on earth, the Temple in Jerusalem, a huge complex of courts and colonnades that the king before the Roman governors had rebuilt in gleaming white stone. The Romans, who were practical about such things, excused the Jews from worshipping the emperor and let them sacrifice to their own God on the emperor's behalf.

Even so, many Jews found it unbearable that foreigners who worshipped idols should rule God's land and tax God's people. The scrolls spoke of a king whom God would send to set things right. The Hebrew word for him was *messiah*, "the anointed one", because kings were anointed with oil. In the years of Roman rule, one man after another appeared in the countryside, gathered a following and announced that the time had come. The governors dealt with all of them in the same way.

## What can be known

Jesus came from Nazareth, a village in Galilee, the hill country in the north. He was a craftsman, probably a builder or carpenter, and he spoke Aramaic, the old office language of the Persian empire, which had become the everyday speech of the region. When he was about thirty, he was baptised in the river Jordan by a preacher named John, who was calling on people to repent before God's judgment arrived. Soon afterwards Jesus began to preach on his own account.

He was born in the last years of Augustus. About five hundred years later, a monk in Rome tried to work out the exact year, so that years could be numbered from it. His arithmetic was off by a few years, but his numbering stuck, and it is the one most of the world now uses. From here on the years in this story can carry their numbers. Jesus began to preach in about the year 28.

Nearly everything known about what he said comes from four short books called gospels, a word that means "good news". They were written in Greek, between forty and seventy years after his death, by believers who wanted to bring others to belief. A historian has to read them with that in mind. Nevertheless, they agree on a great deal, and two writers from outside the movement, a Jewish historian and a Roman one, confirm the outline. Historians of every persuasion accept that Jesus lived, that John baptised him, that he gathered followers in Galilee, and that he was put to death in Jerusalem on Pilate's orders.

His message was that God's kingdom was close at hand, and that it would turn the world upside down. The poor and the hungry would be first in it, and the rich would find it very hard to enter. He told people to love their enemies, to forgive debts, and to give without expecting a return. He ate with tax collectors and prostitutes, which scandalised the respectable. He was known across Galilee as a healer.

After a year or two, he went up to Jerusalem for Passover, the spring festival at which Jews remembered their escape from slavery in Egypt. The city was packed with pilgrims, and the governor came up from the coast each year with extra troops, because a festival of liberation in an occupied country was an anxious time. In the outer court of the Temple, Jesus overturned the tables of the men who changed money for the pilgrims. A few days later the Temple authorities had him arrested at night and handed him to Pilate.

Pilate had him crucified. The condemned man was nailed or tied to a wooden cross and left to hang until he died, which could take days. It was the death that Rome kept for slaves and rebels, the same death that six thousand of Spartacus's followers had died along the road to Capua, and it was meant to be seen. According to the gospels, the notice fixed to the cross gave the charge as "King of the Jews". To the governor, Jesus was one more would-be messiah.

That should have been the end of it, as it had been for the others. Instead, within days, his followers were saying that they had seen him alive, and that God had raised him from the dead. Whatever a reader makes of that claim, the conviction of those first followers is the fact from which everything else follows. They began to call him by the Greek word for "anointed", which is *Christos*.

## The tentmaker

For the first few years, everyone who believed this was a Jew. The followers in Jerusalem went on worshipping in the Temple and keeping the law. They were one more group within Judaism, which had several.

The man who changed that never met Jesus. Paul was a Jew from Tarsus, a Greek-speaking city in the south of Turkey. He had studied the law in Jerusalem, and at first he hunted the new movement as a dangerous error. Then, on the road to Damascus, he had a vision of the risen Jesus, and for the remaining thirty years of his life he worked for the cause he had tried to destroy.

Paul took the message out of Judea and into the cities of the eastern Mediterranean, and the empire made that easy. Augustus had left behind a world at peace, with paved roads, seas cleared of pirates, and one language, Greek, that would serve in any town from Syria to Rome. In most of those towns there was a community of Jews with a meeting house, where a visiting teacher could speak. Paul travelled something like fifteen thousand kilometres, supporting himself by his trade, which was making tents. In each city he gathered a small group, stayed some months, and moved on.

Afterwards he kept in touch by letter. Seven letters that are certainly his survive, written in the fifties to groups in cities such as Corinth and Rome. They are the oldest Christian documents in existence, older than the gospels, and they are full of the problems of small new communities: quarrels, lawsuits, money, and who may eat what.

One problem mattered more than all the others. In the Greek cities, many of the people drawn to Paul's message were not Jews. Did they have to become Jews in order to follow a Jewish messiah? For a man, that meant circumcision, a serious operation for an adult. For everyone it meant the food laws, which made it impossible to eat an ordinary meal at a neighbour's house.

Some of the leaders in Jerusalem said that of course they must. Paul said that they need not, and he argued the point to their faces. What made a person right with God, he insisted, was trust in Christ, and the law was no longer the gate. In Christ, he wrote to one of his groups, there is neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor free, neither male nor female.

Paul won the argument, and it is hard to think of a more important one in the history of religion. A movement that required circumcision would have stayed a branch of Judaism. Paul's version could be joined by anybody, in any city, for the price of a bath in water and a change of life.

The groups he founded called themselves by a word that Greeks used for the gathering of a city's citizens, *ekklesia*, "the assembly". It is translated as "church". Outsiders gave their members a nickname, the Christians.

Paul's own end shows the Roman world from another side. Arrested after a riot in Jerusalem, he told the officer who was about to have him flogged that he was a Roman citizen, and the rods were put away. He then used a citizen's right to have his case heard by the emperor, and was shipped to Rome. The emperor was Nero. According to an early tradition, Paul was beheaded there in the sixties, about the time of the great fire of Rome. Nero blamed the fire on the Christians of the city, and had numbers of them torn apart by dogs or set alight as torches in his gardens.

## A city without its Temple

In the year 66, Judea rose in revolt. The rebels took Jerusalem and wiped out a Roman legion sent to retake it.

Rome answered with four legions and its best general, who became emperor halfway through the campaign and left his son Titus to finish the work. Titus laid siege to Jerusalem in the spring of 70. Inside the walls, rival rebel groups fought one another and burned each other's grain stores while the people starved. In the summer the Romans broke into the Temple courts, and the Temple burned. The city was levelled. Tens of thousands of prisoners were sold as slaves or sent to die in the arenas.

In Rome, an arch put up in honour of Titus still stands at the edge of the Forum. Carved on the inside of it, Roman soldiers carry the treasures of the Temple in the victory parade. The largest is a great lampstand with seven branches.

For Jews it was the second time that their Temple had fallen, and the question was the one their ancestors had faced in Babylon. The religion's daily worship had consisted of sacrifice, and sacrifice could be offered in one place only. That place was now rubble.

A later Jewish tradition tells how the answer was found. During the siege, an elderly teacher had his students carry him out of Jerusalem in a coffin, past the rebel guards. He went to the Roman commander and asked for one thing: a small town on the coast called Yavneh, and leave to teach there. It was granted.

Whether or not it happened exactly so, the story is true to what followed. At Yavneh and then in Galilee, teachers of the law rebuilt Judaism around the things that could be carried. Prayer took the place of sacrifice, and the meeting house, the synagogue, took the place of the Temple. The teacher took the place of the priest, and he was addressed as *rabbi*, "my master". Their rulings on how to live by the law in every circumstance of life were handed down by word of mouth and, around the year 200, written out in a book. Judaism became, even more than before, a religion of study.

It needed to be portable. In 132 the Jews of Judea revolted a second time, under a leader whom some of the rabbis hailed as the messiah. The emperor of the day crushed the rising after three years, at a cost in lives greater than the first. He rebuilt Jerusalem as a Roman town with a temple of Jupiter, forbade Jews to enter it, and renamed the province after their ancient enemies the Philistines. The Latin name was Palaestina.

By then the followers of Jesus and the followers of the rabbis had been drawing apart for two generations. The Christians had not joined the revolts. Most of them were no longer Jews by birth, and they read the fall of the Temple as God's verdict. Each side came to see the other as having betrayed the faith they shared.

One consequence was written into the gospels themselves. They were composed in these years, by people who had to live under Roman rule, and they tend to shift the blame for the death of Jesus away from the Roman governor and onto the Jewish crowd. In one of them the crowd cries out that his blood should be on them and on their children. That sentence was to be quoted, for many centuries, by Christians who wished to harm Jews.

## A family of strangers

The cities in which the churches grew were hard places to be alone.

A Roman city was crowded beyond anything in the modern West. Most people lived in single rooms in tall, badly built apartment blocks, without water or drains. Fires and collapses were routine. Disease was constant, and a baby born in such a place could expect, on average, to live perhaps twenty-five years. Many of the people in the streets were newcomers, including freed slaves, traders and labourers from other provinces, who had left their families behind or never had any. Someone who fell sick without relatives could die in a doorway. A person without a family to pay for the funeral might be thrown into a common pit.

A church was a household for people who lacked one. Its members called one another brother and sister. They met each week before dawn, sang, heard readings, and shared a meal. They also paid into a common chest.

A Christian writer in North Africa, about the year 200, explained to pagan readers what the chest was for. Nobody was forced to give, he said, but each put in a little once a month. The money went to feed and bury the poor, to support orphans, old slaves and shipwrecked sailors, and to help members who had been sent to the mines or to prison. He adds that the pagans looked at all this and said: see how they love one another.

The scale could be large. In the year 251 the bishop of Rome mentioned in a letter that his church was supporting more than fifteen hundred widows and people in distress. No pagan temple did anything of the kind, and neither did the government, whose free grain was a privilege of citizens in the capital.

Epidemics showed the difference most sharply. Twice in a hundred years, new diseases swept the empire and killed a large share of the people in the cities. When the second struck Alexandria, the bishop there described what he saw. The pagans, he wrote, pushed away those who were starting to sicken, fled from their own relatives, and threw the dying into the roads. The Christians stayed and nursed the sick, and many of them caught the disease and died of it.

The bishop was praising his own flock, but the practice he describes had an effect that he could not have known. Most victims of such a disease do not need medicine, which in any case did not exist. They need water, food and warmth while they are too weak to fetch them. Simple nursing can save a large share of them. A group that nursed its sick came out of an epidemic with more survivors, and with pagan neighbours who owed their lives to it.

Women joined in especially large numbers. The pagan world took it for granted that a father could refuse to raise a newborn, and unwanted babies, girls far more often than boys, were left outdoors to die or to be picked up by slave dealers. A letter survives from a labourer in Egypt to his pregnant wife, written about the time Jesus was born. If it is a boy, he tells her, keep it. If it is a girl, throw it out. The churches forbade the practice outright. They also honoured widows and did not press them to remarry, and women appear as hosts and organisers in Paul's letters from the start.

Slaves joined as well, and sat at the same table as free people. The churches did not oppose slavery. All the same, a slave who was a brother in the assembly was something new.

The strongest evidence for all this comes from an enemy. In the 360s, one of the last pagan emperors tried to revive the old religion, and he wrote to a pagan high priest to complain about the competition. It is disgraceful, he said, that the impious Galileans support not only their own poor but ours as well, while everyone can see that our people get no help from us.

Finally, the churches offered a promise. The old gods looked after cities and harvests and had little to say about what happened to an ordinary person after death. The Christians said that death was not the end, that the dead would rise as Jesus had, and that this held for a slave woman as much as for a senator.

None of this needed crowds converted in a day. Historians who have tried to count estimate that there were perhaps a few thousand Christians in the year 100 and several million by 300, about a tenth of the empire. That sounds like an explosion. In fact it is what happens when a group grows by about three and a half per cent a year, each member bringing in a friend, a spouse or a child every couple of decades. Such a group doubles roughly every twenty years. After two and a half centuries of doubling, a movement that could have met in one house fills cities.

## An emperor's notebook

The educated classes of the empire had a faith of their own, and it was a philosophy.

It had been founded in Athens, a generation after Alexander, by teachers who met in a painted porch on the marketplace. The Greek for porch is *stoa*, and they were called Stoics. They taught that the universe is governed by reason, and that a spark of the same reason lives in every human being, which makes all people citizens of a single great city, whatever their rank. Things outside our control, such as wealth, illness and death, are not worth our distress. What lies within our control is to do our duty and to bear what comes.

It was a creed well suited to men who ran an empire, and Roman senators took it up with enthusiasm. Its two most famous teachers under the emperors stood at opposite ends of society. One was a slave from Turkey, lame in one leg, who was freed and taught philosophy to the sons of the rich. The other read that slave's lectures as a young man and went on to be emperor. Marcus Aurelius ruled from 161 to 180, through years of war and the first of the great epidemics, and at night in his tent on the Danube frontier he wrote notes to himself in Greek. They are reminders to keep his temper, to do his work, and to remember that he will soon be dead and forgotten.

The notebook is one of the most admired books to survive from the ancient world, and it shows what the Stoics lacked. They had no assembly and no common chest, and nobody to bring the sick man water. Their philosophy offered no life after death, and it was not meant for people who could not read. It taught an emperor how to endure. It did not tell a widow in a tenement where her next meal would come from.

## Certificates

Rome tolerated nearly every religion, and its hostility to this one needs explaining.

The Romans believed that the safety of the empire depended on the goodwill of the gods, and that goodwill was kept up by sacrifice. They did not much mind what a person believed. They minded very much that he took part. The Jews were excused as an ancient people following the ways of their ancestors. The Christians had no such excuse. They were Greeks, Syrians and Romans who had abandoned the gods of their own cities and who refused, even when ordered, to burn a pinch of incense before a statue of the emperor. To their neighbours they looked like atheists, and when a flood or a plague came, they were easy to blame.

For two centuries, nonetheless, the government had no settled policy. About the year 112, the governor of a province in northern Turkey wrote to the emperor to ask what he should do with people accused of being Christians. He had questioned some under torture and had found nothing worse than what he called a perverse superstition. They met before dawn, sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, and swore an oath not to steal or commit adultery. The emperor's reply was brief. Christians were not to be hunted out. If one was accused and convicted, he must be punished, but anyone who would sacrifice to the gods was to be pardoned, and unsigned accusations were to be ignored.

In other words, being a Christian was a crime that the state preferred not to notice. Persecution was local and occasional, set off by a mob or a zealous governor. The victims were few, and the churches treasured their stories. They called those who died *martyrs*, the Greek word for witnesses.

That changed in the year 250, when the empire was in desperate trouble on its frontiers. A soldier emperor decided that the gods must be appeased, and he ordered every inhabitant of the empire to sacrifice before a local commission and to obtain a signed certificate. More than forty of the certificates have been found in the sands of Egypt, on scraps of papyrus. Each states that the bearer has always sacrificed to the gods and has now done so in the presence of the officials, and each is countersigned.

Many Christians complied, or bribed someone for a certificate. Others refused and were jailed or killed. The emperor died in battle a year later and the order lapsed.

The last and worst attempt began in 303. Churches were pulled down, scriptures were burned, and clergy were tortured, sent to the mines or executed. It went on for eight years in the eastern provinces, and several thousand people died.

It failed, and by then failure was certain, because of what the churches had become. Each city's Christians were led by a single official called a bishop, from the Greek for "overseer". The bishops wrote to one another, met in councils, and recognised one another's decisions from Spain to Syria. They controlled funds, buildings and staff. Outside the army, there was no other organisation of the kind in the empire. A government could kill bishops, but it had no means of replacing what they did.

## The sign in the sky

In 312 a general named Constantine marched on Rome to take it from a rival emperor. Constantine had been proclaimed emperor by his troops six years earlier, in the far north of Britain, and he held the western provinces. Like most soldiers, he worshipped the Sun.

The two armies met at a bridge over the Tiber just north of the city. Before the battle, Constantine had a new sign painted on his soldiers' shields, the first two letters of the name of Christ in Greek. A Christian writer of the time says that he was told to do so in a dream. Constantine himself, years later, said that he and his army had seen a cross of light in the sky above the sun. He won the battle, his rival drowned in the river, and he had no doubt about which god had given him the victory.

The next year, he and the emperor of the east agreed that everyone in the empire, Christians included, should be free to worship as they chose, and that confiscated church property should be returned. Constantine went much further in the years that followed. He excused Christian clergy from the costly public duties that fell on men of property. He allowed churches to receive inheritances, gave bishops the power to judge lawsuits, and made Sunday a day of rest. He paid for enormous churches in Rome and Jerusalem. The old religion was not banned, but ambitious men could see where the emperor's favour lay.

Why he did it has been argued over ever since. Christians were still a small minority, and hardly any of his soldiers or senators were among them, so he was not following a crowd. The simplest reading is that he believed. What he gained, whether or not he calculated it, was an alliance with the one body that reached into every city of his empire.

He soon found out what the alliance involved. The bishops were split over a question raised by a priest in Alexandria: was Christ fully God, equal to the Father, or was he God's first and greatest creation? To an old soldier this seemed a quarrel about words, and he wrote to the two sides to say so. When that did no good, he summoned the bishops of the whole empire to a council, in 325, at a town called Nicaea, near his new eastern capital. They travelled by the government's own post horses, at public expense. About three hundred came.

The council ruled that Christ was of one substance with the Father, and the words it agreed are still recited in churches. The priest and two bishops who would not sign were sent into exile by the emperor's order. From then on, a disagreement about doctrine was also a matter for the state.

When the business was finished, Constantine gave a banquet for the bishops in the palace. One of the guests described it. Many of the men present had been through the persecution that ended fourteen years earlier, and some carried its marks, a blinded eye or a crippled leg from the mines. Now they walked in through the gate between two lines of the emperor's bodyguard, who stood with their swords drawn. The guest wrote that it seemed less like real life than like a dream.
