# Chapter 25 — Why did the Roman Empire become Christian?

**Line under the title:** Judea and the cities of the Roman Empire, from about 2,000 years ago to the year 337.
**Length:** 5,000 to 5,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room.
**Dates:** This is the chapter where the book changes from "years ago" to numbered years. The change is explained once, at the birth of Jesus, and plain years are used from then on.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Christianity began as a movement inside Judaism around a teacher whom the Roman governor executed as a rebel. It became a religion that anyone could join when Paul argued that non-Jews need not take on Jewish law. It then grew for three centuries without any help from the state, at a steady rate that needed no miracles of mass conversion, because a church was a family of strangers in cities where most people had none: it fed widows, nursed the sick in epidemics, buried the dead, sat slave and free at one table, and promised life after death. The empire's occasional attempts to stamp it out made it famous. By the time an emperor took its side it was the best-organised body in the empire outside the army. In the same years the Jews lost their Temple and rebuilt their religion around teachers and study, and the two faiths drew apart with a bitterness that lasted.

## The story

1. **Opening: a stone at Caesarea.** Answer first. The inscription of Pontius Pilate. Judea under Rome: one God, one Temple, and a governor. Hopes of a king sent by God.
2. **What can be known.** Jesus of Galilee. The sources and their dates. The message, the Temple, the cross. How our years came to be counted from him. The followers who said he had risen.
3. **The tentmaker.** Paul, the roads and the sea lanes, small groups in Greek cities. The quarrel over the law. "Neither Jew nor Greek."
4. **A city without its Temple.** The Jewish revolt, the year 70, the arch in Rome. The teachers at Yavneh. A second revolt. Two religions draw apart.
5. **A family of strangers.** Life in a Roman city. The common fund. The epidemic. Women, slaves, the exposed infant. The enemy's testimony: Julian. Steady growth and compound interest.
6. **An emperor's notebook.** What the educated believed: the Stoics. A slave and an emperor teach the same philosophy. What it did not offer.
7. **Certificates.** Why Rome objected: the refusal to sacrifice. Pliny's letter. The certificates of 250. The Great Persecution. The bishops and their network.
8. **The sign in the sky.** Constantine at the bridge. Toleration, money, Sunday. The council at Nicaea: bishops with scars walk past the guards to dinner.
