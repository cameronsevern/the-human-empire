## Changed facts

No existing number, date, personal name, or direct quotation was changed or removed. Most additions restate existing material, but the following introduce or strengthen substantive claims. “Old: none” means the assertion was absent, rather than differently worded.

1. **The beginning of the Armenian persecution**
   - **Old:** “On the night of 24 April 1915, the police in Constantinople arrested about two hundred and fifty Armenian writers, clergymen, teachers and members of parliament.”
   - **New:** “The first blow fell in the capital.” The original sentence follows unchanged.
   - This adds a claim about which action came first.

2. **An exclusive cause of the genocide**
   - **Old:** “The ruling officers decided that the Armenians of the empire, as a people, were a danger behind the lines, and that the solution was to remove them.”
   - **New:** “The leap turned the acts of some into the guilt of a whole people, and everything that followed came from it.”
   - The existing explanation becomes an assertion that everything resulted from this single inference.

3. **The scope of Balfour’s assurance**
   - **Old:** The quoted undertaking specifies “the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities in Palestine”.
   - **New:** “It offered one people a home, and it assured the people already living there that nothing would harm their rights.”
   - The added paraphrase drops the qualification identifying which rights were protected.

4. **The reason for Weizmann’s access**
   - **Old:** “The movement's leader in Britain was a chemist who had solved a critical problem in the making of explosives for the navy, and he had the ear of the cabinet.”
   - **New:** “The movement's leader in Britain was a chemist who had solved a critical problem in the making of explosives for the navy. As a result, he had the ear of the cabinet.”
   - Association becomes explicit causation.

5. **The railway’s military effect**
   - **Old:** “His sons led a force of desert tribesmen north along the railway that linked Arabia to Syria, blowing up trains and pinning down Turkish garrisons.”
   - **New:** “Wherever the line was cut, the Turkish garrisons along it were stranded, so the raiders blew up trains and pinned those garrisons down.”
   - Adds an automatic consequence for every railway interruption.

6. **The relationship between Sykes–Picot and the mandates**
   - **Old:** “France received Syria and Lebanon. Britain received Iraq and Palestine.”
   - **New:** “In practice, the division looked much like the secret map.” The original sentences follow.
   - Adds an explicit geographical comparison.

7. **Lebanon’s reliability as a French ally**
   - **Old:** “France then cut the coastal mountains and the lands around them away from Syria to make a separate state, Lebanon, in which its allies the local Christians would be the largest group.”
   - **New:** “A border drawn that way gave France a small state that it could count on.”
   - Converts the intended political advantage into an assured result.

8. **The rulers’ titles**
   - **Old:** “British advisers, bases and aircraft would remain in both.”
   - **New:** “In both new states, the kings would reign while British advisers, bases and aircraft remained.”
   - Introduces a king in Transjordan in the 1921 setting.

9. **An exclusive Turkish achievement**
   - **Old:** “It fixed, with small later changes, the borders that Turkey has today.”
   - **New:** “Here, unlike in the Arab lands, the frontier was settled by the people who had fought for it.”
   - Adds a categorical contrast absent from the previous version.

10. **The rationale for population exchange**
    - **Old:** “To prevent future conflict, Greece and Turkey would exchange their minorities, compulsorily.”
    - **New:** “Minorities were seen as a cause of war, so the way to prevent future conflict was to remove them.”
    - Makes the attributed reasoning explicit; the compulsory exchange remains unchanged.

11. **The explanation for the Palestine conflict**
    - **Old:** “The two halves pulled in opposite directions from the first day.”
    - **New:** “The two halves pulled in opposite directions from the first day, because each side read the words ‘national home’ in its own way.”
    - Adds a particular causal explanation.

12. **Palestine’s availability to refugees**
    - **Old:** None.
    - **New:** “Palestine was one of the few places left open.”
    - Adds a claim about access, beyond the existing population figures.

13. **The policy’s name**
    - **Old:** “A statement of policy announced that Jewish immigration to Palestine would be limited to seventy-five thousand people over the next five years, after which there would be no more without Arab consent.”
    - **New:** “A statement of policy, known as the White Paper, announced that Jewish immigration to Palestine would be limited to seventy-five thousand people over the next five years, after which there would be no more without Arab consent.”
    - Correctly adds the name to the body; it already appeared in the caption.

## Factual flags

1. **Minor — newly introduced: Transjordan did not have a king in 1921.**

   > “In both new states, the kings would reign while British advisers, bases and aircraft remained.”

   Faisal became king of Iraq, but Abdullah ruled Transjordan as **emir**. He became king in 1946. The new wording also conflicts with “the emirate of Transjordan” immediately before it. [Royal Hashemite Court biography](https://rhc.jo/en/king-abdullah-bin-al-hussein-1882-1951)

2. **Major — newly introduced: the Balfour paraphrase broadens the protected rights.**

   > “It offered one people a home, and it assured the people already living there that nothing would harm their rights.”

   The declaration protected the existing non-Jewish communities’ **civil and religious rights**, without promising their political or national rights. That distinction matters directly to the chapter’s explanation of the competing promises. The preceding quotation preserves it; the added explanation loses it. [Declaration reproduced in the British administration’s report](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-204267/)

3. **Minor — newly introduced: April 24 was not the first action against Armenians.**

   > “The first blow fell in the capital.”

   April 24 is the conventional commemorative starting date, but persecution and massacres preceded the Constantinople arrests. Ottoman forces massacred populations considered disloyal during their February–March 1915 retreat. The arrests were a decisive attack on Armenian leadership, rather than the first blow anywhere. [US Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/world-war-i-and-the-armenian-genocide?parent=en%2F52260)

4. **Major — newly introduced regression: the Arab–Turkish contrast contradicts the chapter’s Saudi example.**

   > “Here, unlike in the Arab lands, the frontier was settled by the people who had fought for it.”

   The chapter laterally excludes Arab territorial settlements achieved through fighting, although it has already described Ibn Saud conquering Mecca and building his state. The defensible contrast is with the **British and French mandate territories**, not all Arab lands. This reintroduces the substance of earlier check item 4, despite the opening sentence having been corrected. The chapter’s own Ibn Saud paragraph supplies the counterexample.

5. **Minor — existing: the triumvirate did not consist of three army officers.**

   > “In 1908 a movement of young army officers had forced the sultan to restore a parliament, and in 1913 three of them seized power outright.”

   Talat, one of the three leaders meant here, was a civilian politician who had worked in the postal and telegraph service; Enver and Cemal were officers. The earlier chronology correction is retained, but “three of them” introduces a separate identification error. [Talat biography, TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/talat-pasa)

6. **Minor — existing: exclusive lending rights overstate Sykes–Picot.**

   > “Further inland there would be an Arab state in name, in which only France could supply advisers and lend money.”

   The agreement gave France **priority** in enterprise and local loans in area A. Exclusivity applied to supplying advisers and foreign officials, at the Arab government’s request. Priority in lending is not a monopoly. [Sykes–Picot agreement, opening provision](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/sykes.asp)

7. **Minor — existing, made more explicit: Wilson’s declaration is placed at the wrong event.**

   > “There, the American president had declared that peoples should no longer be handed about from one ruler to another like property.”

   This identifiable declaration was delivered to **Congress on 11 February 1918**, before the Paris peace conference. Its relevance to Paris is sound; “There” mislocates its delivery. [Original address, US State Department](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918Supp01v01/d59)

## Clarity flags

- **Empty signposts accumulate.**  
  > “The arrangement itself was simple.”  
  > “Its argument ran in steps.”  
  > “Its reasoning was blunt.”  
  > “Its reason was strategic.”

  These announce the character of the explanation instead of supplying it. They fall under the style sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences.

- **Several additions repeat their neighbours without helping the reader.**  
  > “The two places he named lay far apart.”  
  > “Their home in Arabia went to someone else.”  
  > “The numbers on the two sides were very unequal.”

  The next sentences respectively give the distance, identify the conqueror, and provide the unequal totals. These are padding rather than necessary orientation.

- **The genocide explanation acquires an unnecessary absolute.**  
  > “The leap turned the acts of some into the guilt of a whole people, and everything that followed came from it.”

  “Everything” turns the chosen explanation into an exclusive account of all subsequent events. The first half makes the point clearly without that additional burden.

- **The railway explanation also acquires an absolute.**  
  > “Wherever the line was cut, the Turkish garrisons along it were stranded, so the raiders blew up trains and pinned those garrisons down.”

  “Wherever” implies that every cut stranded the garrisons concerned, regardless of location, duration, repair or alternative supply. Explaining disruption of supplies would preserve the mechanism.

- **The refugee passage suggests a cleaner open-to-closed transition than occurred.**  
  > “Palestine was one of the few places left open.”

  “Open” can mean an available destination or unrestricted admission. The latter reading is misleading: Britain regulated immigration before 1939 and imposed a political ceiling in 1937. Palestine remained a destination, but access was restricted. [British administration’s 1938 report](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-198684/)

- **The violence paragraph needs chronological orientation.**  
  > “Anger turned to violence.”

  This follows the account reaching 1939, then introduces riots in 1920, 1921 and 1929. The added transition sounds like forward movement and strengthens the misleading impression that these riots followed the refugee influx after Hitler’s accession.

- **An unexplained term remains central to the Iraq paragraph.**  
  > “It would be a kingdom, made by joining three former Ottoman provinces, each with a different population: one in the north with many Kurds, one in the centre dominated by Sunni Arabs, and one in the south whose people were mostly Shia.”

  “Sunni” and “Shia” need a brief explanation as Islam’s two main branches. The style sheet requires historical terms to be explained on first appearance.

- **An existing absolute lacks a clear meaning.**  
  > “Syria, Lebanon, Iraq and Jordan are still on the map in very nearly the shapes that they were given in those years, and every one of their borders has since been fought over.”

  Does “fought over” mean disputed diplomatically, crossed by armies, or subjected to war over its location? “Every one” makes the ambiguity consequential.

No hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit chapter cross-references found.

## Overall

The revision mostly preserves the historical narrative, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. New transitions introduce an incorrect royal title, broaden Balfour’s assurance, and revive the previously corrected Turkish-exception claim. Several others add repetitive signposting. The fixed findings from the earlier check have otherwise been left alone. No files were edited.
