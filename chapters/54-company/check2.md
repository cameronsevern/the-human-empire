## Changed facts

No names, dates, numerical figures, or quotations were removed or replaced. However, several additions introduce factual claims or strengthen existing ones. Below, “addition” means there was no corresponding old sentence; the old quotation supplies its context. Restatements that preserve the original meaning are excluded.

1. **Sequence of Roe’s reception**
   - Old: “Roe spent nearly three years trailing after the court as it moved about northern India, waiting weeks for an audience, and paying bribes to officials.”
   - New: “After that, Roe was left to wait.” This precedes the same account, now beginning “He spent…”
   - Change: explicitly places his waiting after the coach episode.

2. **Duration of investors’ commitments**
   - Old: “Merchants had always handled this by forming a partnership for each voyage and dividing everything up when the ship returned.”
   - New additions: “In that way, nobody's money was tied up for longer than one voyage.” “Nobody builds a fort with money that has to be handed back in two years.”
   - Change: turns the description of a financing arrangement into universal claims about repayment and construction finance.

3. **Permanent capital at subscription**
   - Old: “The maid could not ask for her hundred guilders back.”
   - New: “The maid's position, once she had paid, was a new one. The company now had her money for good, and she could not ask for her hundred guilders back.”
   - Change: places permanent commitment at the moment she subscribed. See factual flag 1.

4. **Shareholder numbers and investment risk**
   - Old: “Within a few years, dealers were gathering every day on a bridge in Amsterdam, and later in a purpose-built courtyard, to buy and sell these shares.”
   - New addition: “Since thousands of people held shares, there was soon a regular trade in them.”
   - Old: “A person could own a small piece of a very risky business, could own pieces of several, and could get out at any time without the business having to stop.”
   - New addition: “The risk that had frightened a single merchant was now spread thinly over a great many people, and none of them was locked in.”
   - Change: adds a company-wide shareholder count, a causal explanation for trading, and a stronger claim about individual risk and liquidity.

5. **Nutmeg monopoly and surviving islanders**
   - Old: “In 1600 every nutmeg in the world grew on the Banda Islands, ten specks of land in eastern Indonesia with about fifteen thousand people.”
   - New addition: “Whoever controlled those islands would be the only seller of nutmeg on Earth and could name the price.”
   - Old: “The company divided the land into plantations and brought in slaves to pick the nutmegs.”
   - New: “With the islanders gone, the company had the nutmeg to itself. It divided the land into plantations and brought in slaves to pick the nutmegs.”
   - Change: adds exclusive control over sales and implies the indigenous population was gone. See factual flag 2.

6. **A single previous political master**
   - Old: “It became divided, into a dozen states at war with one another, and each of them was in the market for allies.”
   - New addition: “A foreigner with soldiers to offer now had many possible customers where before there had been one master.”
   - Change: strengthens imperial predominance into a claim of previously exclusive authority.

7. **Who could become an effective infantryman**
   - Old: “The drill was the important part, and there was nothing European about the men who could learn it.”
   - New addition: “Anyone who was trained for those months could stand in that line.”
   - Change: turns a claim about ethnicity into a universal claim about training sufficiency.

8. **Bengal’s comparative importance**
   - Old: “Its cloth was the finest in India, and Calcutta, the English company's main post, stood on its river.”
   - New addition: “The Company therefore had more at stake in Bengal than anywhere else.”
   - Change: adds an explicit ranking of the Company’s interests.

9. **Plassey’s outcome**
   - Old: “An agreement was drawn up in secret: the general would hold his troops back, the English would fight, and the general would become ruler and pay the Company and its officers handsomely.”
   - New addition: “The battle that followed was therefore decided before it began.”
   - Change: upgrades an agreement intended to secure victory into a claim that victory was already settled.

    - New addition: “It still ruled through an Indian prince, and it still lived by trade.”
    - Change: introduces a sharper division between commercial income before 1765 and territorial income afterwards. See factual flag 3.

11. **Financing cloth purchases before 1765**
    - Old: “The first consequence was that the Company no longer needed to send silver to Bengal.”
    - New addition: “Until then, it had paid for Bengal's cloth with coin brought from England.”

    - New addition: “They had no reason to care what state it would be in after they left.”

## Factual flags

1. **Major — the new wording reintroduces permanent capital at subscription.**

   > “The maid's position, once she had paid, was a new one. The company now had her money for good, and she could not ask for her hundred guilders back.”

   The preceding paragraph correctly says the initial commitment was ten years. “Once she had paid” now contradicts that correction: the original arrangement allowed investors to withdraw after the ten-year accounting. Permanent commitment developed subsequently. This is a regression introduced by the revision, not a repetition of the earlier flag against the corrected paragraph. [Dari-Mattiacci and colleagues, *The Emergence of the Corporate Form*](https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/documents/pdf/cbl/VOC_050_GDM.pdf)

2. **Minor — the new transition erases survivors the preceding sentence explicitly preserves.**

   > “With the islanders gone, the company had the nutmeg to itself.”

   Immediately before this, about a thousand islanders remain. The historical account also includes survivors, refugees, and continued resistance. The correct claim is that the Company destroyed and displaced most of the population and imposed control over production—not that the islanders were gone. [Westfries Museum, “1621: violence and depopulation on and around Banda”](https://pala.westfriesmuseum.nl/1621-2/1621-article/?lang=en)

3. **Major — territorial revenue began before 1765.**

   > “It still ruled through an Indian prince, and it still lived by trade.”

   In this before-and-after explanation, “still lived by trade” misleadingly postpones territorial financing until the diwani. The Company obtained the Twenty-Four Parganas in 1757 and revenues from Burdwan, Midnapur, and Chittagong in 1760. Trade continued, but territorial revenues already supported its military establishment. The 1765 grant greatly expanded and formalised that revenue base. [*The Making of British India*, including the 1760 treaty and its revenue provisions](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d1/The_making_of_British_India%2C_1756-1858_-_described_in_a_series_of_dispatches%2C_treaties%2C_statutes%2C_and_other_documents_%28IA_cu31924024059663%29.pdf)

4. **Minor — the added bullion sentence creates a false 1765 cutoff.**

   > “Until then, it had paid for Bengal's cloth with coin brought from England.”

   “Until then” makes imported English bullion the continuing financing method right up to the grant. The shift towards local resources had already begun: Bowen’s records show no direct Company silver shipments to Bengal in 1760–65. The correct sequence is a transition beginning before 1765 and enlarged by the diwani. This concerns the new sentence, not the earlier correction from “India” to “Bengal.” [H. V. Bowen, “Bullion for trade, war, and debt-relief,” especially Table 1 and pp. 456–57](https://www.academia.edu/7450603/Bullion_for_trade_war_and_debt_relief_British_movements_of_silver_to_around_and_from_Asia_1760_1833_2010_)

5. **Major — the narrative conflates successive nawabs.**

   > “The new ruler soon fell out with his masters, and in 1764 an alliance of northern princes, with the Mughal emperor himself among them, tried to drive the Company out of Bengal.”

   “The new ruler” refers back to the general installed after Plassey: Mir Jafar. But the Bengal ruler associated with the alliance defeated at Buxar was his successor, Mir Qasim, installed in 1760. Mir Jafar had been restored by 1763. The missing succession makes two different men read as one. [Banglapedia, “Mir Qasim”](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Mir_Qasim)

6. **Major — Roe’s commercial results are reduced to one port.**

   > “For all that effort, he went home with leave for the English to keep a trading house in one port.”

   His results were narrower than the comprehensive imperial settlement he wanted, but not merely permission for one trading house in one port. The British Library’s factory records describe wider trading permissions and factories at Ahmadabad, Baroda, and Broach; the documentary account of the 1618 settlement also describes customs, trading, and jurisdictional concessions. [British Library, India Office factory records](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-000173250); [*The English Factories in India*, introduction to the 1618 settlement](https://www.griet.ac.in/images/rare/The%20English%20Factories%20in%20India.pdf)

## Clarity flags

1. **Explicit signposting has returned.**

   > “It was there to make the trade pay, and one small group of islands shows what that meant.”

   The first clause supplies useful meaning; the second announces the illustrative example. It recreates the earlier check’s signpost problem in new wording.

   > “It is easy to mistake what this collapse meant.”

   This announces a correction instead of stating it.

2. **Some new setup sentences add little orientation.**

   > “What they invented was an answer to a problem about money.”

   > “The maid's position, once she had paid, was a new one.”

   Both are vague previews. The following sentences supply their entire meaning. The style sheet welcomes explanatory space, but these do less work than the useful addition explaining that a share sale changes the owner while leaving the Company’s money intact.

3. **The training explanation repeats its central point.**

   > “The weapon mattered less, however, than what the men were taught to do with it.”

   > “The drill was the important part, and there was nothing European about the men who could learn it.”

   > “Anyone who was trained for those months could stand in that line.”

   These repeatedly establish the importance and teachability of drill. The last also introduces an exception-breaking “Anyone.”

4. **Several absolutes break the style sheet.**

   > “Nobody builds a fort with money that has to be handed back in two years.”

   Short-term finance can fund long-lived assets through repayment or refinancing; the problem is unreliable financing, not impossibility.

   > “The lesson was not lost on anyone.”

   > “After that, every Indian prince wanted such troops on his side…”

   These make universal claims where the argument only requires widespread adoption.

   > “They had no reason to care what state it would be in after they left.”

   Short-term incentives do not establish the absence of every possible reason for concern.

5. **Liquidity and diversification are blurred.**

   > “The risk that had frightened a single merchant was now spread thinly over a great many people, and none of them was locked in.”

   Sharing ownership distributes the capital requirement; it does not necessarily make each investor’s personal exposure small. Selling also requires a buyer and may crystallise a loss. The surrounding explanation should keep spreading investments and being able to sell them distinct.

6. **The tenant analogy contradicts its immediate example unless qualified by time.**

   > “Like any tenant, it kept on the right side of the landlord.”

   The next paragraph describes the Company declaring war on that landlord. “Like any tenant” is also an unnecessary universal.

7. **Two added statements pull the Plassey explanation in different directions.**

   > “The battle that followed was therefore decided before it began.”

   > “On paper he had no chance.”

   These can coexist if “on paper” means the raw troop totals, but the reader has just learned that most opposing troops would not fight. The unexplained switch between nominal numbers and actual fighting strength makes the second sentence feel artificially suspenseful.

8. **The sepoys’ geographic scope slips.**

   > “To the sepoys, who had already seen their homeland annexed and missionaries in their camps…”

   Earlier, Awadh is the homeland of “a large part” of the soldiers. The new wording makes that experience collective. Similarly, the high-caste northern recruitment description principally concerns the Bengal Army, while “the sepoys” can sound like all three Company armies.

9. **An unclear image remains.**

   > “The British retook Delhi street by street, and their revenge was savage: whole villages were hanged, and captured sepoys were tied across the mouths of cannon.”

   “Whole villages were hanged” leaves the reader unsure whether it means every inhabitant, the adult men, or groups executed in multiple villages. The scale needs a literal description.

No hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions, or chapter cross-references found.

## Overall

The revision improves several explanations, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. The most consequential additions concern permanent capital and the transition to territorial revenue. It also adds avoidable absolutes and signposting. Two substantial existing problems remain: the conflated nawabs and Roe’s supposedly single-port concession.
