Read the requested files; no files changed. Findings below are ranked by seriousness. I found a continuity problem with chapter 48, but no clear contradiction with chapters 47 or 53.

1. **Factual error — British military support disappears.**
   > “No army had been sent from England to bring this about.”

   British regular troops participated: the 39th Foot was deployed to India in 1754 and fought at Plassey. The Company’s reliance on Indian soldiers is sound, but this absolute wrongly excludes British Army assistance. **Confidence: high.** [National Army Museum](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/39th-dorsetshire-regiment-foot)

2. **Factual error — permanent capital is backdated to 1602.**
   > “The money would stay in the company for good.”

   The VOC’s original arrangements provided for an accounting and an opportunity to withdraw capital after ten years. Liquidation was subsequently postponed; indefinite continuation emerged through the changes culminating in 1623. Permanence was an institutional development, not the original bargain described here. **Confidence: high.** [Gelderblom and colleagues, “Learning how to manage risk by hedging”](https://academic.oup.com/ereh/article/24/2/332/5423652)

   > “In return each investor got a share: a right to a slice of the profits every year, in proportion to what he had paid.”

   “Every year” is also wrong for the founding arrangement: distributions began in 1610 and were irregular, sometimes paid in spices. **Confidence: high.** [Same study](https://academic.oup.com/ereh/article/24/2/332/5423652)

3. **Misleading financial claim — revenue funding becomes financial self-sufficiency.**
   > “The conquest of India paid for itself as it went along.”

   Indian revenues financed expansion, but conquest also accumulated substantial debt and required outside assistance. The chapter itself subsequently describes a government bailout. The defensible claim is that conquered territories supplied revenue and borrowing capacity for further conquest—not that expansion covered its costs as it proceeded. **Confidence: high.** [Parliament’s account of the 1773 rescue](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/parliament-and-empire/parliament-and-the-american-colonies-before-1765/parliament-and-the-east-india-company/); [1806 parliamentary discussion of Company debt](https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/1806-07-10/debates/8abf8d8b-05a2-40ad-95d6-5304c816c33f/IndiaBudget)

4. **Continuity with chapter 48 / reader comprehension — what is new about the drill?**
   > “The second was a new way of fighting, with foot soldiers drilled to fire muskets together on command.”

   Chapter 48 already tells readers that “musketeers had to be drilled and paid all year round,” and shows firearms defeating larger armies at Panipat and Chaldiran. This is not necessarily a historical contradiction, but the present explanation makes an established practice sound newly introduced. Explain the particular change—flintlocks, bayonets and coordinated infantry formations—and distinguish it from the earlier gunpowder armies. The later description supplies some of this, but never explicitly resolves the apparent repetition.

5. **Factual overstatement / exception-breaking absolute — silver shipments did not simply end.**
   > “The first was that the Company stopped sending silver to India.”

   Company silver exports to Bengal had already fallen away before 1765; shipments to other Indian settlements continued or resumed, and Bengal later received shipments again. Tax revenues substantially displaced imported bullion as a way of financing purchases, but “stopped sending silver to India” is too categorical. **Confidence: high.** H. V. Bowen’s shipment table documents the regional differences and subsequent imports. [“Bullion for trade, war, and debt-relief”](https://www.academia.edu/7450603/Bullion_for_trade_war_and_debt_relief_British_movements_of_silver_to_around_and_from_Asia_1760_1833_2010_)

6. **Factual overstatement — a century of religious non-interference has a major counterexample.**
   > “The Company, for its part, had been careful for a century not to interfere with their religion.”

   The 1806 Vellore rising followed military dress regulations affecting caste marks, beards and headgear, which soldiers understood as interference with religion. The subsequent sentence, locating the lapse in the 1850s, therefore gives a false chronology. **Confidence: high.** [Account of the Vellore mutiny](https://victorianweb.org/history/empire/india/vellore.html)

7. **Factual error — Amsterdam subscriptions are confused with company-wide capital.**
   > “About eighteen hundred people signed up in Amsterdam alone, and they raised more than six million guilders, ten times what the English company had started with two years before.”

   Amsterdam’s register contains **1,143 subscribers**, contributing about **3.7 million guilders**. Roughly **6.4 million** was the total across the VOC’s chambers. The sentence combines the wrong subscriber count with the wrong geographical scope for the capital. **Confidence: high.** [Amsterdam City Archives](https://www.amsterdam.nl/stadsarchief/stukken/geld/onbekend-voc-aandeel/); [Amsterdam Museum](https://hart.amsterdam/image/2015/8/12/hollanders_gouden_eeuw_audiotour_nl_as_rec_2014_10_29_clean.pdf); [corporate-history account of total capital](https://www.inview.nl/document/id730cc81aa0ca088620378e05eb5fe766/the-one-tier-board-in-the-changing-and-converging-world-of-corporate-governance-a-comparative-study-of-boards-in-the-uk-the-us-and-the-netherlands-instituut-voor-ondernemingsrecht-nr-85-4-1-5-evolution-of-corporate-governance?ctx=WKNL_CSL_1474&tab=tekst)

8. **Factual error — Bombay did not originate as a Mughal-authorised tenancy.**
   > “With the emperor's leave it rented plots on the coast and built fortified warehouses on them, which grew into the towns of Madras, Bombay and Calcutta.”

   Bombay passed from Portugal to the English Crown through Catherine of Braganza’s marriage settlement, then to the Company in 1668 for an annual rent. Madras was obtained from a southern Indian authority, not through the same Mughal arrangement. These settlements cannot all illustrate the stated mechanism. **Confidence: high.** [Bombay chronology, Tata Institute of Fundamental Research](https://www.theory.tifr.res.in/bombay/history/c17.html); [Burton Stein, *A History of India*](https://delhihighcourt.nic.in/files/2025-05/Ebook/a_history_of_india_second_edition.pdf)

9. **Factual error — cloth names are not all Indian words.**
   > “The English language still carries the cargo lists: calico, chintz, muslin, gingham, bandanna, dungaree and pyjamas are all Indian words.”

   *Gingham* derives through Dutch from Malay; *muslin* derives through French and Italian from the name Mosul, in modern Iraq. Indian manufacture and Indian linguistic origin are different claims. **Confidence: high.** [Gingham etymology](https://www.etymonline.com/word/gingham); [Oxford’s muslin etymology](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/us/definition/english/muslin)

10. **Doubtful attribution of motive — Clive’s suicide attempt becomes boredom.**
    > “Its commander was Robert Clive, who had come out to India as a junior clerk, had twice tried to shoot himself out of boredom, and had found that he had a talent for war.”

    Accounts describe depression, isolation and a pistol failing twice. “Out of boredom” asserts a psychological explanation the evidence does not establish; two failed trigger pulls also need not mean two separate attempts. Retain the supported circumstances or omit the aside. **Confidence: high that the motive needs correction; medium on the precise reconstruction of the incident.** [English Heritage](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/london-statues-and-monuments/robert-clive)

11. **Doubtful statistic — the shipping risk lacks a defined population.**
    > “One ship in five did not come back.”

    Which ships, whose voyages and which years? Research finds substantially lower loss rates for Dutch Cape-route voyages, while some Portuguese periods were much worse. Ships retained in Asia also “did not come back” without being lost. The sentence needs a specified dataset or a non-numerical statement of risk. **Confidence: high that the unqualified figure is unsafe; medium on the intended figure’s origin.** [Study comparing Portuguese and Dutch shipping losses](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/social-science-history/article/turning-points-in-leadership-ship-size-in-the-portuguese-and-dutch-merchant-empires/05AA8EAB13D75D6DE5BCBDD02B8739A2)

12. **Reader comprehension — the decisive bridge to tax collection is omitted.**
    > “In 1765, after another victory, Clive met the Mughal emperor, who by then was a wanderer without a capital.”

    “Another victory” conceals why the emperor was in a position to make this concession. At Buxar in 1764, the Company defeated an alliance that included the emperor himself. One sentence explaining that defeat would connect military coercion to the grant; otherwise the reader sees a nearly inexplicable gift of sovereignty. **Confidence: high.** [Battle of Buxar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Buxar)

13. **Chronological ambiguity — India appears to become the fallback after 1667.**
    > “Shut out of the spices, the English company fell back on its second choice, which was India.”

    Immediately after the Run–New York settlement, this reads as the next event. Yet the Company established its Surat factory in 1613, and the chapter itself opened with Roe’s earlier embassy. Indian textiles initially also supported the spice trade through exchange in Southeast Asia. Make clear that the narrative is stepping back and describing a gradual shift. **Confidence: high.** [Om Prakash’s account of early Company trade](https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/GEHN/GEHNConferences/conf2/Conf2-OPrakash.pdf); [Stein, *A History of India*](https://delhihighcourt.nic.in/files/2025-05/Ebook/a_history_of_india_second_edition.pdf)

14. **Reader comprehension — a consequential annexation remains unidentified.**
    > “The Company had annexed the home kingdom of many of its soldiers.”

    The reader needs a place, a time and the connection to the grievance. Identify Awadh, annexed in 1856, and briefly explain that these soldiers’ families lived there. “Home kingdom” otherwise introduces an unseen country immediately before the rebellion. **Confidence: high.** [National Army Museum’s account of Company armies and recruitment](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/armies-east-india-company)

15. **Style — sentences announce the explanation instead of giving it.**
    > “Nutmeg shows how they used it.”

    This announces the illustrative example and falls under the prohibition on signpost sentences. The following sentence already begins the example.

    > “The consequences followed quickly.”

    This similarly announces the consequences that the next paragraphs enumerate. Neither sentence supplies necessary historical information or orientation.
