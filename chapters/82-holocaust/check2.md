## Factual flags

1. **Major — the added sentence misstates the distinction between the earlier and later shootings.**

   > “Once the women and children were included, the aim was no longer to kill enemies. It was to destroy whole communities.”

   Jewish men were already being murdered because they were Jews, rather than because they were actual combatants or individually established enemies. Conversely, Nazi ideology continued to classify entire Jewish communities as enemies when women and children were included. The documented change was the widening of systematic murder from predominantly men to entire communities—not the abandonment of an earlier, different purpose of killing enemies. [USHMM, Einsatzgruppen and other SS and Police Units](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/einsatzgruppen-and-other-ss-and-police-units-in-the-soviet-union).

2. **Minor — the new education claim overgeneralises.**

   > “These were educated men, trained to run a modern state.”

   Following the doctorate count, this implies that the participants shared an educational preparation for government. Their backgrounds differed substantially. Eichmann, for example, completed neither secondary school nor his vocational mechanics course; his administrative expertise developed through his Nazi career. The doctorate count supports a claim about many participants, not this uniform account of their training. [Yad Vashem, Adolf Eichmann](https://collections.yadvashem.org/en/about/o213).

3. **Major — the battalion’s total is attributed too narrowly and placed within too short a period.**

   > “Over the following year that one battalion shot thirty-eight thousand people.”

   Browning’s figure is **at least 38,000 shooting deaths in which the battalion participated**, including joint operations with other units. It includes the November 1943 massacres at Majdanek and Poniatowa, roughly sixteen months after the July 1942 starting point. It is not a tally of people independently shot by this battalion during the following twelve months. [Christopher Browning, *Ordinary Men*, p. 142](https://www.fisheaters.com/srpdf/OrdinaryMen.pdf).

4. **Minor — the new warehouse conclusion is too comprehensive.**

   > “Whatever could be sold had been kept.”

   In its immediate setting—the property discovered at liberation—this suggests that saleable loot had all been retained. Much had already been transported away, and the SS burned the Kanada II storage complex on 23 January 1945. Soviet troops found what remained in surviving stores. The earlier sentence about storerooms the SS had not burned can describe those surviving buildings; the new blanket conclusion cannot describe the property as a whole. [Auschwitz-Birkenau Memorial, evacuation and liquidation](https://lekcja.auschwitz.org/en_11_wyzwolenie/sco_2.html); [liberation](https://www.auschwitz.org/en/history/liberation/).

5. **Minor — the map’s stated date conflicts with its described deportation routes.**

   > “Europe in 1942, roughly: the land ruled by Germany, its allies and the countries they occupied, the area of the mass shootings behind the eastern front, and the main deportation routes, simplified; the enlargement shows the Warsaw ghetto and the six killing centres in occupied Poland.”

   The accompanying alternative text describes routes from Greece and Budapest to Auschwitz. The major deportations from Greece began in 1943; those from Hungary occurred in 1944. “Roughly” does not resolve that chronological mismatch. A 1942 territorial background can carry later deportation routes, but the caption must distinguish their dates. [USHMM, deportations to killing centres](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/deportations-to-killing-centers); [Hungarian deportations](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/timeline-event/holocaust/1942-1945/deportation-of-hungarian-jews).

## Clarity flags

- **Ambiguous chronological transition:**
  > “The head of the security forces watched an execution that summer and came away worried about the nerves of his men.”

  The preceding narrative has reached July 1942 and then “the following year.” “That summer” therefore points readers toward the wrong year; the incident belongs to 1941.

- **An absolute that outruns the underlying fact:**
  > “With that index in hand, the occupiers knew where every registered Jew lived.”

  An index supplies registered addresses, not guaranteed current whereabouts. The later account of hiding makes the distinction particularly relevant. [Joods Monument explains that these addresses record a particular moment in 1941 or 1942](https://www.joodsmonument.nl/en/page/741/application-of-the-data).

- **Unclear scope:**
  > “Refusing, in other words, cost almost nothing.”

  The evidence just given concerns official punishment. “Cost” could also mean ostracism, humiliation or career consequences. Specify the kind of consequence being discussed.

- **Repetition with little additional explanation:**
  > “The list, in other words, reached far beyond the lands that Germany held.”

  The preceding sentences already explicitly identify unconquered countries and give England and Ireland as examples.

  > “The victims, in effect, paid for their own deportation.”

  This immediately repeats “The fares were paid out of the money taken from the passengers.”

- **Repeated setup:**
  > “Driving out people in those numbers was a different matter from anything tried in Germany.”
  >
  > “The first measures went further than anything yet tried in Germany.”

  Adjacent sentences repeat both the comparison and nearly the same wording.

- **A setup sentence duplicates the next sentence:**
  > “One man also thought about how all this would be remembered.”

  Ringelblum’s decision to leave a record states that idea immediately afterwards. This is dispensable setup rather than necessary orientation.

- **Vague standalone qualification:**
  > “The comparison is not simple, because the conditions were very different.”

  “Conditions were very different” does not explain which conditions. The following paragraph supplies the actual distinction; the added clause contributes little.

- **Padding with an unclear causal implication:**
  > “A crime without a name was hard to write into a law.”

  This does not explain the political or legal obstacles, and it follows the sentence saying a name already existed. Lemkin had proposed international criminal provisions against group destruction in 1933, before coining “genocide.” [Getty, Lemkin’s earlier proposals](https://www.getty.edu/publications/occasional-papers-2/2/).
