1. **[chapters/20-ashoka/chapter.md:125](chapters/20-ashoka/chapter.md:125)**  
   > “A king speaking from a rock face was not a new sight in the north-west of the empire.”  
   > “Those provinces had belonged to the Persian kings before Alexander, and Darius had told his own story on a cliff.”

   **Misleading geography.** Darius’s inscription at Behistun is in western Iran, outside Ashoka’s empire. Former Persian rule over northwestern India and Afghanistan does not establish that inhabitants there had already seen such cliff inscriptions. Chapter 13 explicitly locates Behistun “in the west of Iran.” The defensible comparison is with a Persian precedent elsewhere, not an established local sight. [Source: UNESCO, Bisotun](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1222/).

2. **[chapters/18-democracy/chapter.md:153](chapters/18-democracy/chapter.md:153)**  
   > “In one respect Athens asked more of its subjects than the Great King asked of his.”  
   > “The Persians took their tribute and left each people to govern itself.”

   **The contrast overstates Persian autonomy.** Persian rulers supported and installed local tyrants; Mardonius subsequently replaced Ionian tyrannies with democracies. They intervened in local government as well as collecting tribute. Chapter 13 itself describes satraps judging disputes and Greek strongmen maintained in power by Persia. Local administration continued under imperial supervision; peoples were not universally left to govern themselves. This also violates the prohibition on exception-breaking absolutes. [Source: Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Ionian Revolt”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ionian-revolt/).

3. **[chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:66](chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:66)**  
   > “Men now fought from the backs of their horses.”  
   > “A rider needs no vehicle, no wheelwright and no second man to drive, and so the chariot, which had ruined the treasuries of the bronze kings, began its long retirement into parades and royal hunts.”

   **Misleading chronology and an unsupported causal claim.** The placement makes mounted combat sound new under the eighth-century reforms, although Assyrian mounted fighters are depicted in the ninth century. Early mounted archers also sometimes depended on a second rider controlling their reins. [Source: “The First Cavalries in the Ancient Near East”](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2ss7863x).

   More seriously, chapter 11 says chariots were expensive and the palace system vulnerable; it does **not** establish that chariots “ruined the treasuries.” Its account describes interacting harvest failures, warfare and disrupted trade. “Cost the bronze kings dearly” would preserve the supported point. [Source: Eric Cline, *1177 B.C.*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691208015/1177-bc).

4. **[chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:163](chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:163)**  
   > “Sumerian was the language of the first cities of the plain, and by his day nobody had spoken it for well over a thousand years.”

   **“Nobody had spoken it” is wrong.** Sumerian had ceased to be an everyday native language, but remained a learned and liturgical language. Say that it had ceased to be a mother tongue, rather than that nobody spoke it. The earlier chapters’ distinction between Akkadian and the older southern language does not support this stronger claim. It also breaks the explicit rule against absolutes. [Source: University of Cambridge, “Mesopotamian Languages”](https://www.arch.cam.ac.uk/about-us/mesopotamia/mesopotamia-history/mesopotamia-languages).

5. **[chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:9](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:9)**  
   > “Nothing about this was unusual.”  
   > “Great kingdoms had been swallowing small ones for a very long time, and everybody understood what such a defeat meant.”  
   > “When the house was ash and the people were gone, there was not much left to say for him.”

   **The new opening presents one interpretation of defeat as universal.** Defeat could signify a god’s anger with his own people rather than his impotence. Chapter 8 already describes divine punishment as the explanation for Akkad’s destruction; chapter 14’s own Mesha passage supplies another explicit example. The opening therefore undermines the distinction the chapter later explains. “Nothing” and “everybody” also breach the style guide’s rule on absolutes. [Source: the Mesha inscription](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/anet/320-the-stela-of-mesha/).

6. **[chapters/16-caste/chapter.md:19](chapters/16-caste/chapter.md:19)**  
   > “Zoroaster composed his hymns there in a sister language, and his priests kept them in the same way, by heart.”

   **“There” supplies an unwarrantedly definite location.** Its antecedent is the Iranian plateau reached by cousins who “turned west.” Chapter 13 more carefully places Zoroaster “somewhere in the east of the Iranian world.” His precise homeland is uncertain; proposed locations include Central Asia as well as eastern Iran. The language and oral-transmission comparison is sound, but the geographic connection needs correction. [Source: Encyclopaedia Iranica, historical review of Zoroastrianism](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroastrianism-i-historical-review/).

7. **[chapters/13-persia/chapter.md:185](chapters/13-persia/chapter.md:185)**  
   > “The satraps, the roads and the storehouses were waiting for him, and he took them over as he found them.”

   **The new ending overstates continuity and ends in summary.** Alexander retained much of the administrative framework, but replaced officials and altered responsibilities. Chapter 19 explicitly describes Macedonians controlling troops and revenue alongside a retained Persian governor, followed by replacements of other governors. “As he found them” suppresses those changes. [Source: “Alexander the Great and an Experiment in Government”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-classical-journal/article/abs/alexander-the-great-and-an-experiment-in-government2/616CAE88EC878B00CC37A298D416DFC6).

   Editorially, the final sentence now restates “Their system outlived them” and “he did not have to invent a government,” rather than supplying the concrete landing required by *style.md*.

8. **[chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:85](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:85)**  
   > “The Persian kings, with as many peoples to rule, had left each of them its own laws and customs and asked for tribute.”

   **Misleading comparison and excessive universality.** Immediately after “Seven kingdoms,” “as many peoples” reads as an equal count. Chapter 13 describes approximately twenty provinces and twenty-three peoples represented at Persepolis. If the intended meaning is comparable diversity, say that. “Each” also turns substantial preservation of local institutions into an exceptionless policy; Persian intervention in Ionian constitutions disproves that stronger formulation. [Source: Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Ionian Revolt”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ionian-revolt/).

9. **[chapters/15-money/chapter.md:165](chapters/15-money/chapter.md:165)**  
   > “Sargon of Akkad had boasted of the five thousand men who ate bread before him every day, and the storekeepers of the Persian kings were still measuring out flour by the litre.”

   **Accurate callbacks placed inside a misleading chronology.** The paragraph introduces these as examples of provisioning “Before coins.” But chapter 13’s Persian ration records belong to Darius’s reign, when Persian coinage already existed; chapter 15 itself has explained Persian minting at Sardis. The Persian example demonstrates that rations continued **alongside** coinage, not a stage before it. Chapter 8’s 5,400 men can reasonably be rounded to five thousand under the style guide. [Source: Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Daric”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/daric/).

10. **[chapters/17-thinkers/chapter.md:43](chapters/17-thinkers/chapter.md:43)**  
    > “On the Ganges, in the same years, the Brahmins were teaching the opposite.”

    **Too broad: inherited rank is not the opposite of moral cultivation.** Chapter 16 describes birth-based social duties, but also study, conduct and spiritual knowledge. The new sentence makes Brahmin teaching sound uniformly opposed to judging character independently of known parentage. The *Chandogya Upanishad* supplies a counterexample: Gautama accepts Satyakama, whose paternal lineage is unknown, after hearing his truthful explanation. Specify hereditary social rank instead of claiming an unqualified philosophical opposite. [Source: *Chandogya Upanishad* 4.4](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/chandogya-upanishad-shankara-bhashya/d/doc1145267.html).

11. **[chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:85](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:85)**  
    > “The tablets were written about five years after his surrender.”

    **The date is established for one tablet, not all four.** Only one of the four Jehoiachin ration tablets preserves its date: Nebuchadnezzar’s thirteenth year, 592/591 BCE. Say “One dated tablet was written…” The chapter’s own lookups file explicitly makes this distinction. [Source: Center for Online Judaic Studies](https://cojs.org/babylonian_ration_list-_king_jehoiakhin_in_exile-_592-1_bce/).

12. **[chapters/22-wall/chapter.md:48](chapters/22-wall/chapter.md:48)**  
    > “The first tax collectors of the plain of Iraq had succeeded because a heap of grain cannot be hidden and a farmer cannot leave his field.”

    **The callback drops the earlier chapter’s necessary qualifications.** Chapter 6 explains that grain is readily visible and measurable, and that leaving irrigated land is costly. It expressly says some families left to fish or herd, and describes records of runaway workers. Farmers could leave; they could not take their irrigated fields with them. This repeats chapter 6’s compressed opening while contradicting its fuller explanation, and breaks the rule against absolutes.

13. **[chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:191](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:191)**  
    > “Ashoka, forty years earlier and on the far side of the mountains, had filled his rocks with regret for a conquest.”

    **Overstates the callback and interrupts the paragraph’s argument.** Chapter 20 identifies a particular edict expressing remorse, distinguishes the broader programme of moral instruction, and notes that the remorse edict was omitted in Kalinga. “Filled his rocks” makes regret sound like the general content. The insertion also interrupts the explanation of what the Han changed, between Qin’s justification for rule and the Han alternative. A narrower Ashoka comparison belongs with the discussion of Qin’s inscriptions.

14. **[chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:151](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:151)**  
    > “It was an old story on the plain where the exiles now lived, and a telling of it lay among the tablets in the library at Nineveh.”

    **Correct callback, awkward insertion.** Chapter 12 does describe the Nineveh flood tablet. But this sentence separates “the two versions” of Genesis from “In one, Noah…” and “In the other…”. Introducing a third telling in between makes those references momentarily ambiguous and can suggest that the Nineveh text features Noah. Move the comparison after the two biblical versions have been explained.

15. **[chapters/16-caste/chapter.md:152](chapters/16-caste/chapter.md:152)**  
    > “The priests of Marduk had done the same service for Cyrus.”

    **Correct comparison, damaged reference in the following sentence.** Chapter 13 supports the legitimising role. However, the next sentence—“The king in return gave the priest cattle, land and protection”—now appears to refer to Cyrus and a priest of Marduk, instead of the Indian king and Brahmin introduced before the insertion. Complete the Indian exchange before introducing Cyrus.

16. **[chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:78](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:78)**  
    > “What became of him afterwards is known from an unexpected source.”

    **Explicit signpost.** This announces a forthcoming surprise instead of presenting the evidence, contrary to *style.md*’s “No signpost sentences” rule. The excavation sentence immediately following it can introduce the evidence directly.
