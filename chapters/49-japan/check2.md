## Factual flags

1. **Major — the land war did not end quickly.**

   > “As a result, the fighting on land was over quickly.”

   Seoul fell quickly; the land fighting did not. Korean resistance and Chinese intervention produced further substantial campaigns, including the recapture of Pyongyang in 1593, followed by renewed invasion and land warfare in 1597–1598. The sentence also contradicts the chapter’s subsequent account of ambushes, Chinese intervention and Japanese withdrawal.

   **Correct fact:** The initial Japanese advance was rapid, and Seoul fell within weeks. The war continued until 1598. [Jinju National Museum, Imjin War Hall](https://jinju.museum.go.kr/eng/html/sub02/020101.html).

2. **Major — firearms did not introduce a wholly new possibility of recruiting ordinary infantry.**

   > “Until then, skill at arms had taken years to acquire.”

   In context, this contrasts all earlier military service with quickly trained peasant gunners. It mistakes the prolonged training of mounted archers and elite warriors for the training required of every soldier. Large forces of foot soldiers equipped with pikes already existed before the Portuguese firearms arrived.

   **Correct fact:** Firearms provided another effective role for ordinary infantry; they did not replace a system in which every combatant needed years of elite weapons training. Thomas Conlan describes armies of thousands, largely composed of pike-wielding infantry, in his account of Japan before 1550. [Conlan, “Japan, 1200–1550”](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/japan-1200-1550/).

3. **Minor — “second measure” creates a misleading chronology.**

   > “His second measure was quieter, and it reached just as far.”

   Coming immediately after the 1588 sword hunt, this presents the land surveys as a subsequent reform. Hideyoshi’s survey programme began in 1582 and continued as he acquired territory.

   **Correct fact:** The surveys and the sword hunt were related measures, but the surveys began earlier. “Another measure” would preserve the connection without reversing the apparent sequence. [EBSCO, “Taikō Kenchi Survey”](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/taiko-kenchi-survey).

4. **Minor — the restrictions did not neatly target Japanese people first and foreign missionaries afterwards.**

   > “The first of them were aimed at the Japanese themselves.”

   > “The foreigners who had brought the religion came next.”

   The 1635 edict combined restrictions on Japanese overseas movement with orders concerning Christianity and the imprisonment of Western missionaries. The chapter itself also places the nationwide Christian prohibition in 1614. The Portuguese expulsion came later, but action against foreign missionaries was already underway.

   **Correct fact:** Restrictions on Japanese movement and on Christian activity overlapped; the Portuguese were finally excluded in 1639. [The 1635 edict, translated at Columbia University](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/main_pop/ps/ps_japan-tokugawa-edicts-foreigners.htm).

5. **Major — the surviving two-century firearms claim remains false.**

   > “For two hundred years, however, no Japanese army fired one in battle.”

   Matsumae forces used guns against the Ainu in Shakushain’s war in 1669. An account drawing on Matsumae and Tsugaru records describes approximately 200 gunners firing during the fighting at Kunnui.

   **Correct fact:** Prolonged peace greatly reduced battlefield use, but Japanese domain forces still used firearms in some conflicts. This is a remaining error in the firearms paragraph, distinct from the earlier “forgotten gun” wording and the territorial peace sentence that have already been corrected. [“Shakushain’s Revolt of 1669,” especially p. 63](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/tgkt/1/0/1_KJ00004265913/_pdf).

## Clarity flags

1. **New signposts announce explanations.**

   > “The two halves of that century belong together.”

   > “The Tokugawa solved it with a rule about where a lord had to live.”

   The first announces a connection that the next sentence supplies. The second announces a solution immediately before stating the residence requirement. Both fall under the style sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences.

2. **The added explanations sometimes repeat a point already fully made.**

   > “The first, in other words, did the hard and violent work, the second shaped what he left, and the third collected the reward.”

   This translates the rice-cake saying immediately after giving it, leaving the metaphor little work to do.

   > “The rule that held his family also kept him short of money.”

   This repeats the preceding explanation of two households, processions and depleted income.

   > “So many readers wanted something to read.”

   This is particularly thin padding between the literacy figures and the publishing sentence. Unlike a useful transition, it adds neither orientation nor explanation.

3. **“Those same men” blurs generations.**

   > “The gun helped a few men to end Japan's wars, and those same men then decided what, and who, would be allowed into the country.”

   Readers can reasonably understand this to mean that the three unifiers personally established the later restrictions. The chapter subsequently carries that process into 1639, under their successors. The connection between unification and controlled foreign relations works; the identity of the decision-makers needs greater precision.

4. **Several new absolutes are more sweeping than their explanations require.**

   > “A charge by those horsemen was the thing that every other commander dreaded.”

   “Every other commander” is an unnecessary universal.

   > “A farmer without weapons could not rebel, and he could not join an army and rise through it.”

   This turns an obstacle into an impossibility: rebels can acquire weapons, and armies can supply recruits.

   > “Yi had also built a few vessels of a special design, which took away the Japanese method altogether.”

   “Altogether” also leaves the scope unclear: protection against boarding these particular vessels is different from eliminating boarding throughout the battle.

5. **Some older absolutes remain stylistically brittle.**

   > “Japan had an emperor, who lived in the old capital of Kyoto and was honoured by everyone and obeyed by no one.”

   > “For the first time the ruler of Japan knew what the country was worth, down to the last valley.”

   These make universal claims about obedience and complete information where the underlying points are weakened imperial authority and greatly improved fiscal knowledge. Neither needs its absolute to remain confident.

6. **“Those books” supplies the wrong immediate referent.**

   > “The kingdom had recently produced one of the cleverest inventions in the history of writing, and it had come from a complaint about those books.”

   The previous paragraph concerns scholarly government. Sejong’s complaint in the following sentences concerns ordinary people’s inability to express themselves in writing and understand legal charges. “Those books” makes the transition sound like a complaint about scholarly books specifically.

7. **The land-survey summary obscures the process.**

   > “A lord's standing now rested on a number that the ruler had written down.”

   The preceding sentences describe surveyors assessing productive capacity. This new sentence makes the result sound like a number personally assigned by Hideyoshi. It also repeats the preceding statement about rank without explaining anything further.

8. **The war’s verdict conflicts with the benefit immediately described.**

   > “The war had achieved nothing for Japan and had wrecked Korea.”

   The next paragraphs describe captives contributing to Japan’s porcelain industry. “Nothing” therefore jars with the chapter’s own account. Failure to achieve the invasion’s military and political aims is the clearer point.

9. **The subtitle understates the chronological reach.**

   > “Japan and Korea, from 1543 to about 1700”

   The final section substantially discusses eighteenth- and nineteenth-century developments, including 1720, 1801 and literacy “by the 1800s”; the persecution narrative reaches 1865. These are more than a single brief forward glance.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions or prohibited chapter cross-references. The confident interpretation of divided loyalty and controlled trade is not itself a problem.

## Overall

The revision improves paragraph spacing and supplies useful explanations, but it is not entirely fact-neutral. The new claim that land fighting ended quickly needs correction, several connectors introduce misleading chronology or absolutes, and the older two-century firearms claim remains wrong. Some added transitions also repeat explanations rather than helping the reader forward.
