## Factual flags

1. **Major — mandatory minimums had exceptions.**

   > “A judge could no longer give less than the minimum, whatever the circumstances of the case.”

   The same 1986 legislation enacted a substantial-assistance exception. On the government’s motion, a court could sentence below the statutory minimum when a defendant helped investigate or prosecute someone else. A further “safety valve” followed in 1994. The correct distinction is that judges generally lost discretion to go below the minimum, not that exceptions ceased to exist. [US Sentencing Commission, 2017 overview](https://www.ussc.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/research-and-publications/research-publications/2017/20170711_Mand-Min.pdf)

2. **Major — rural concentration is confused with a rural majority.**

   > “Most of the dead in the first wave were white and rural.”

   The rural-majority claim is wrong. Rural communities suffered especially rapid increases and severe local effects, but most deaths occurred in metropolitan areas. Research examining the first wave also finds higher opioid-overdose rates in the most urban counties, alongside faster acceleration in rural counties. The defensible fact is that the early epidemic disproportionately affected white Americans and devastated many rural communities—not that most victims were rural residents. [Original geographic analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9335141/), [CDC metropolitan/nonmetropolitan death counts](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/ss/ss6619a1.htm)

3. **Minor — the chapter contradicts its own “two drugs” claim.**

   > “It is also one of the two that are sold openly in shops.”

   Later, the chapter describes legal cannabis sales in Canada and numerous American states. Alcohol and tobacco are therefore not the only two drugs sold openly in shops, even within the chapter’s own examples. The correct fact is that tobacco is legally sold in shops despite its high dependence risk. **Source:** the chapter’s “Dissuasion” section.

4. **Minor — heroin existed before Bayer marketed it.**

   > “Heroin, too, began life as a product of a reputable firm.”

   Heroin was first synthesized in 1874; Bayer introduced it commercially in 1898. The addition conflates invention with commercial introduction. Its pharmaceutical marketing began with Bayer, but the substance did not. [DEA Museum history](https://museum.dea.gov/exhibits/online-exhibits/cannabis-coca-and-poppy-natures-addictive-plants/opium-poppy)

5. **Minor — territorial waters were not the outer limit of enforcement.**

   > “Beyond that line American law did not reach, so the ships could wait there openly while the boats ran the cases ashore.”

   This is too broad across the Prohibition period. Customs enforcement extended beyond territorial waters, and the 1924 British-American treaty expressly permitted boarding and seizure outside the three-mile boundary, within a distance determined by an hour’s sailing. Offshore anchorage initially offered protection, but crossing the territorial boundary did not categorically put ships beyond American enforcement. [Supreme Court’s account in *Cook v. United States*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/288/102)

6. **Minor — the Mexico transition gives the wrong sequence.**

   > “The next bulge appeared nearer the customers. The trade's centre moved north to Mexico, through which the drugs had to pass.”

   Positioned after Escobar’s 1993 death and the Colombian campaign, “next” makes Mexico’s rise sound subsequent to those events. Major rerouting through Mexico was already happening in the 1980s; a 1993 GAO report estimated that as much as two-thirds of US-bound cocaine transited Mexico in 1990. Nor did all cocaine have to pass through Mexico: Caribbean routes continued. [Contemporary GAO report](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-152.pdf)

7. **Minor — Capone’s photograph is attached to the wrong event.**

   > “Al Capone in the police photographs taken when he was sent to prison, 17 June 1931.”

   June 1931 belongs to his initial plea proceedings, not his eventual imprisonment following the tax trial. He subsequently withdrew that plea, was convicted later in 1931, and entered Atlanta penitentiary after his appeals failed. The photograph’s date should not be described as the date he was sent to prison. [FBI chronology](https://www.fbi.gov/history/cases-and-criminals/al-capone)

8. **Minor — Portugal’s commission membership is inaccurately fixed.**

   > “In practice, a person caught with up to ten days' supply of any drug, including heroin, is sent to a panel of a social worker, a lawyer and a doctor, which may recommend treatment or impose a small fine, and most often does nothing.”

   The original system required a legal expert and two members drawn from relevant health or social professions. It did not require precisely one doctor and one social worker; psychologists and other qualified professionals could serve. The correct description is a three-person commission with legal and health/social expertise. [Research on the Portuguese system](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/add.16099)

## Clarity flags

- **Explicit signposting:**  
  > “The reasoning runs in steps.”

  This announces the explanation instead of giving it, directly breaking the prohibition on signpost sentences. The following sentences already supply the steps.

- **Repetition without additional explanation:**  
  > “Most people who try these substances do not end up like the rat. Only a minority do…”

  The second sentence repeats the first before reaching its useful point: that dependence rates differ by drug.

- **Repeated explanation:**  
  > “A woman married to a heavy drinker had almost no way to protect herself from what he spent.”

  This immediately repeats the preceding explanation about marriage and access to a husband’s wages.

- **Repeated distinction:**  
  > “The line was drawn, in other words, between the person who sells and the person who uses.”

  The preceding two sentences have already stated precisely that distinction. This adds little orientation.

- **A comparison loses its reference point:**  
  > “Fentanyl is made in laboratories. It is fifty times stronger, and a few grains of it can kill.”

  Stronger than what? The sentence break makes an existing ambiguity more noticeable. Identify heroin as the comparator; “grains” can also mean either visible particles or a formal unit of weight.

- **The balloon metaphor becomes obscure:**  
  > “The trade keeps moving because the demand that pulls it has not moved.”

  “Moved” means different things for trade and demand. Readers may interpret this as a claim that demand’s geographical distribution never changed, rather than that demand persisted despite enforcement.

- **Unnecessary exception-breaking absolutes:**  
  > “Opium dissolved in alcohol was sold by every grocer and chemist in Britain and America…”  
  > “…soon had branches in every town.”  
  > “Their leader, Pablo Escobar, offered every official he met a choice between silver and lead…”

  “Every” makes each sentence depend on an implausible universal claim. Widespread availability, a national organization, and systematic intimidation make the intended points without those absolutes.

- **An unnamed institution obscures continuity:**  
  > “The men who finished the job founded a league that cared about nothing else.”

  The reader must infer that this is a different organization from the women’s temperance union. Naming the Anti-Saloon League would clarify the actors and fits the style sheet’s allowance for necessary names.

I found no hedging stacks, explicit chapter-self-reference, or body cross-references. Ordinary transitions and confident interpretations are not problems in themselves.
