Read style.md first, then chapters 08–14 in full and in order. No files edited. Items below are ordered by release importance.

1. **The definition of empire creates a false distinction.** [08-empire/chapter.md:16](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:16)

   > “A kingdom is one people under one king. Egypt was a kingdom, however large.”

   Kingdoms can contain several peoples, and kingdoms can also be empires. The distinction cannot establish Akkad’s priority. [Source: *The Encyclopedia of Empire*, definition](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/book/10.1002/9781118455074/homepage/custom_copy.htm).

   **Replace:** “Akkad brought formerly independent cities and their surrounding lands under a lasting central power, making it an early model of empire.”

2. **The opening turns the development of monotheism into a deduction that does not follow.** [14-one-god/chapter.md:5](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:5)

   > “Its exiles decided that their god had not been beaten, that he had sent the defeat himself, and that a god who could do that must be the god of the whole world.”

   The chapter’s own Moab example shows that explaining defeat as divine punishment did not require monotheism. The missing step is the rejection of other gods, which arrives much later in the narrative.

   **Replace:** “Among its exiles, prophets turned defeat into evidence of their god’s power; one went further and declared that no other god existed.”

3. **The account of Hammurabi reverses its own conclusion about legal authority.** [09-law/chapter.md:145](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:145)

   > “It is a small step from there to holding the rule up against the king himself.”

   Line 122 says judges were not bound by the collection. Lines 141–147 then treat it as enforceable legislation and a near precursor of constitutional restraint. Publishing royal judgments and establishing remedies against the king are substantially different developments. [Source: *The Legal Authority of the Laws of Hammurabi*](https://academic.oup.com/book/31901/chapter-abstract/267578408).

   **Replace:** “By setting his judgments in stone, Hammurabi gave his subjects a lasting statement of the justice he claimed to provide.”

   Recast the surrounding passage accordingly; replacing this sentence alone will not resolve the contradiction.

4. **The wheel vocabulary is made to date the entire Indo-European family.** [10-wheel/chapter.md:129](chapters/10-wheel/chapter.md:129)

   > “So the speakers of that parent language had wheels, axles and yokes, and they were still speaking one tongue after the wagon was invented.”

   This collapses different stages of the language family. The earlier split involving Anatolian languages matters; recent research distinguishes that older common ancestor from the later steppe expansion. [Source: Lazaridis and colleagues, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08531-5).

   **Replace:** “The shared wagon words link many branches of the family to communities that remained in contact after wheeled transport appeared.”

   Also replace the following “That puts them less than 5,500 years ago” with: “They help date that later chapter in the family’s history.”

5. **The Bronze Age collapse receives a settled explanation immediately after being declared unsettled.** [11-bronze/chapter.md:156](chapters/11-bronze/chapter.md:156)

   > “Whatever the order, the palaces fell together because they had come to depend on each other.”

   Interdependence is an explanatory model, not an established common cause of every collapse. Their different trajectories also undermine “fell together.” Research on Hittite drought explicitly distinguishes regional experiences and multiple causes. [Source: Cornell’s account of the research](https://archaeology.cornell.edu/news/rare-drought-coincided-hittite-empire-collapse).

   **Replace:** “Their dependence on distant supplies and allies gave local disasters a way to spread.”

6. **The fall of Assyria is assigned to the wrong Babylonian reign.** [14-one-god/chapter.md:71](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:71)

   > “About 2,635 years ago, Assyria collapsed, and Babylon took over most of its empire under a king named Nebuchadnezzar.”

   Nabopolassar ruled Babylon when Nineveh fell in 612 BCE; Nebuchadnezzar succeeded him in 605. Chapter 12 has already described the earlier conquest. [Source: *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, Babylonian history](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/babylonia/babylonia-i/).

   **Replace:** “Babylon helped destroy Assyria, and under its next king, Nebuchadnezzar, it secured control of the lands west to the Mediterranean.”

7. **Sargon’s name becomes proof of an invented psychological biography.** [08-empire/chapter.md:12](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:12)

   > “The only person who needs to call himself True King is one who is not, and who knows that people are saying so.”

   This breaches the rule against brittle absolutes. A claim of legitimacy does not establish illegitimacy, the circumstances of naming, or what the bearer privately believed.

   **Replace:** “The name made a claim that mattered to any ruler: his power was rightful.”

   The preceding assertion at line 7 that this was definitely a name adopted on seizing power also needs removal or explicit attribution.

8. **The inscription does not establish the world’s first standing army.** [08-empire/chapter.md:60](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:60)

   > “They were fed from his stores all year round, and they could be sent anywhere at any season. It is the earliest standing army on record.”

   The inscription reports feeding 5,400 men daily. It does not establish all their occupations, year-round deployment, or priority over every earlier force. [Source: the inscription in CDLI](https://cdli.earth/artifacts/461937).

   **Replace:** “His boast was about the number of men he could support every day, a measure of the resources gathered around his court.”

   The preceding account of every earlier army being seasonal also needs narrowing.

9. **Enheduanna’s disputed authorship is treated as eyewitness testimony.** [08-empire/chapter.md:85](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:85)

   > “In one hymn she describes a rebellion at Ur.”

   Most manuscripts are roughly five centuries later; whether they preserve her composition or later writing in her name remains disputed. This matters because the chapter uses the poem to reconstruct her experience. [Source: the Enheduana translation project’s authorship discussion](https://enheduana.org/authorship/).

   **Replace:** “One hymn speaks in Enheduanna’s voice, describing a rebellion at Ur and her expulsion from the temple.”

   At [line 83](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:83), also replace:

   > “Enheduanna is the first author in the history of the world whom we can name.”

   **With:** “Enheduanna is the earliest named figure to whom a surviving body of literary works is attributed.”

10. **The Uluburun tin claim misstates the study and conceals a material dispute.** [11-bronze/chapter.md:35](chapters/11-bronze/chapter.md:35)

    > “One team traced about a third of it to a valley in what is now Tajikistan, more than three thousand kilometres from the place where the ship went down.”

    The proposed third involved sources in both Uzbekistan and Tajikistan, not one Tajik valley. The attribution has also been directly challenged. [Original study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9710885/); [published rebuttal](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1211478/full).

    **Replace:** “The ship carried a tonne of tin, enough to show the scale on which this scarce metal travelled.”

    Cut the preceding sentence about testing unless the disagreement becomes part of the story.

11. **Tax districts are presented as a settled map of Persian provinces.** [13-persia/chapter.md:101](chapters/13-persia/chapter.md:101)

    > “The empire was divided into about twenty provinces.”

    This imports Herodotus’s twenty tribute districts into a more complicated administrative hierarchy. They should not simply be equated with satrapies. [Source: *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, Achaemenid satrapies](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/achaemenid-satrapies/).

    **Replace:** “The empire was divided into provinces governed by royal appointees, with smaller districts beneath them.”

12. **The Persian kings’ religious ancestry is asserted before the uncertainty is acknowledged.** [13-persia/chapter.md:89](chapters/13-persia/chapter.md:89)

    > “The religion of the Persian kings grew from that teaching.”

    Whether their religion derived directly from Zoroaster’s teaching is precisely the disputed question. Shared reverence for Ahuramazda does not settle it. [Source: *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, Achaemenid religion](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/achaemenid-religion/).

    **Replace:** “Darius’s inscriptions share with Zoroaster’s hymns a reverence for Ahuramazda and a sharp opposition between truth and the Lie.”

13. **Assyrian chariots are retired from warfare too early.** [12-assyria/chapter.md:66](chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:66)

    > “A rider needs no vehicle and no wheelwright, and so the chariot, which had cost the bronze kings so dearly, began its long retirement into parades and royal hunts.”

    Cavalry displaced some functions, but Assyrian chariots retained combat roles. The sentence substitutes a simple replacement story for a change in military specialization. [Source: the Assyrian Empire Builders project](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/aebp/Technicalterms/index.html).

    **Replace:** “Cavalry took over much of the scouting and pursuit, while chariots remained part of the battle line.”

14. **Stopping tin imports does not stop all new bronze objects.** [11-bronze/chapter.md:152](chapters/11-bronze/chapter.md:152)

    > “Without tin there is no new bronze.”

    Existing bronze could be remelted and recast; recycling increased during the transition to the Iron Age. The omission makes the proposed collapse mechanism artificially automatic. [Source: archaeological analysis from Beth-Shemesh](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X16300414).

    **Replace:** “Without fresh tin, smiths had to rely more heavily on melting down old bronze.”

15. **Judah’s loss of sovereignty is confused with the removal of its population from the land.** [14-one-god/chapter.md:99](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:99)

    > “Judah had now lost its king, its land and its temple.”

    Many inhabitants remained in Judah. The sentence reinforces the surrounding tendency to make the deported elite stand for the whole population. [Source: Oded Lipschits on Judah during the exile](https://courses.edx.org/assets/courseware/v1/55d514007e18ffb5760f9bcede43a2e9/asset-v1%3AIsraelX%2BBIBARC101%2B3T2019%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Lipschits_Shedding_new_light_on_the_dark_years_Chapter_2_in_Kelle_et_al._eds_Interpreting_Exile_2011.pdf).

    **Replace:** “Judah had lost its monarchy and its temple, and many of the families that had governed it were now in exile.”

16. **The ending makes the rebuilt temple dispensable too abruptly.** [14-one-god/chapter.md:187](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:187)

    > “A religion of this kind does not need a particular hill. It can be practised wherever there is a copy and somebody who can read it aloud.”

    The chapter has just described rebuilding the temple and continuing sacrifices. Reading the law makes important practices portable; it does not make the whole religion independent of Jerusalem.

    **Replace:** “Reading and obeying the law gave people far from Jerusalem a way to share in a faith whose temple they might never see.”

17. **The opening makes a draft animal a requirement for the wheel itself.** [10-wheel/chapter.md:5](chapters/10-wheel/chapter.md:5)

    > “It needs an axle that fits it exactly, carpentry fine enough to make that fit, and a strong animal to pull the load.”

    The chapter itself supplies the counterexample at line 75: the wheelbarrow. The argument concerns the usefulness of early heavy carts, not a mechanical requirement of wheels.

    **Replace:** “A useful farm cart needed a well-made axle, strong carpentry and enough pulling power to move its own weight as well as its load.”

18. **The steppe becomes uninhabitable until the wagon arrives.** [10-wheel/chapter.md:79](chapters/10-wheel/chapter.md:79)

    > “On the grasslands it did far more, because it made them a place where people could live.”

    Line 86 immediately describes people already living there. The intended distinction is between river-based settlement and greater mobility across the open grassland.

    **Replace:** “On the grasslands it let herding families range much further from their settlements beside the rivers.”

    At [line 90](chapters/10-wheel/chapter.md:90), replace:

    > “On foot, that cannot be done. With a wagon, it can.”

    **With:** “A wagon let a family carry far more water and equipment between camps.”

19. **The Akkadian collapse is forced into an unsupported loot-dependent model.** [08-empire/chapter.md:140](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:140)

    > “Once it stopped growing, the cost remained and the loot did not. Then any shock at all, whether a weak king or a dry decade, left the centre unable to pay the men who held it together.”

    This bypasses the regular extraction from conquered farmers already described at line 62. It also turns “any shock” into an inevitable collapse, contrary to the preceding discussion of several pressures acting together.

    **Replace:** “Rebellion threatened the harvests and payments that supported the king’s men, while defending the remaining provinces kept those men expensive.”

20. **A universal religious settlement is contradicted by the chapter’s own evidence.** [14-one-god/chapter.md:126](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:126)

    > “The clearest statements, however, come from the exile, and it took several generations more to become the settled faith of the whole people.”

    “The whole people” closes a process that the Elephantine evidence at line 174 explicitly leaves uneven.

    **Replace:** “The clearest statements come from the exile, but the garrison on the Nile would still honour other gods more than a century later.”

21. **The king list is described as ending where it actually resumes.** [08-empire/chapter.md:118](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:118)

    > “Then, after Naram-Sin’s son, the orderly list breaks off, and in its place the clerk has written a question.”

    The question interrupts the sequence; the list subsequently gives two more reigns. That distinction also helps reconcile the collapse of imperial power with the survival of kings at Akkad. [Source: Oxford’s translation of the Sumerian King List](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section2/tr211.htm).

    **Replace:** “After Naram-Sin’s son, the list interrupts its orderly succession with a question before naming rival claimants and two later kings.”

22. **Selected archaeological agreement becomes a guarantee of the entire royal picture.** [12-assyria/chapter.md:96](chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:96)

    > “In other words, wherever the ground can be checked against the carving in the palace, the two agree.”

    The passage establishes three correspondences, not exhaustive verification. “Wherever” gives the propaganda more independent confirmation than the chapter has demonstrated.

    **Replace:** “The ramp, the arrowheads and the destruction layer confirm the main sequence shown in the carving: siege, assault and the fall of the city.”

23. **The comparison of metal quantities is misleading.** [12-assyria/chapter.md:64](chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:64)

    > “The ship that sank off the Turkish coast in the age of bronze had carried one tonne of tin.”

    This compares a finished weapon metal with the smaller ingredient in an alloy. Chapter 11 gives eleven tonnes of copper and tin aboard the ship. Neither comparison alone measures the empires’ total access to metal.

    **Replace:** “For scale, the ship that sank off Turkey had carried eleven tonnes of copper and tin together.”

24. **The furnace illustration needs its later date stated.** [12-assyria/chapter.md:35](chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:35)

    > “A full-size museum model of an ancient iron furnace, cut open.”

    In this position it reads as a model of the Assyrian equipment being described. The image record identifies a furnace type from the first to fifth centuries CE. [Source: photograph’s description](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Smeltingfurnace.jpg).

    **Replace:** “A full-size model of a furnace from the first five centuries CE, illustrating the bloomery method used long before.”

25. **The opening portrait is identified more narrowly than the evidence permits.** [08-empire/chapter.md:10](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:10)

    > “It shows either Sargon or his grandson.”

    These are proposed identifications, not an established exhaustive choice. [Source: ANU’s record of the Nineveh head](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/entities/anuarchivesitem/77ddbabd-5e50-4815-92f0-7e7192daabc9).

    **Replace:** “The ruler’s identity is unknown.”

26. **Assyria and Persia are reduced to opposing methods that the narrative itself shows them sharing.** [13-persia/chapter.md:183](chapters/13-persia/chapter.md:183)

    > “The Persians had found that it costs less to be obeyed than to be feared, and that most people will put up with a distant king if he leaves their gods and their landlords alone.”

    Fear was one means of obtaining obedience, not its alternative. Chapter 12 describes Assyrian accommodation of compliant kings; chapter 13 describes Persian exemplary torture. The cost comparison is also unmeasured.

    **Replace:** “Leaving local rulers and priests in place gave the Persian kings people who could collect tribute and enforce orders on their behalf.”

27. **The deportation of Israel is substantially retold rather than used to advance the new story.** [14-one-god/chapter.md:35](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:35)

    > “More than twenty-seven thousand were marched off to distant provinces, and strangers were brought in to farm their land. For about a century, Assyrian records still mention people with names built on the name of Yahweh.”

    Chapter 12, lines 141–145, has already supplied the number, resettlement and disappearance from identifiable records. Here the useful new consequence is the movement of northern traditions into Judah.

    **Replace:** “Assyria deported thousands from the northern kingdom, while refugees who escaped south brought their stories and traditions to Judah.”

    Then streamline the overlapping sentence at line 37.

28. **The alphabet’s advantages are explained three times in succession.** [13-persia/chapter.md:136](chapters/13-persia/chapter.md:136)

    > “A clerk did not need years of schooling in the hundreds of wedge-shaped signs.”

    And [14-one-god/chapter.md:45](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:45):

    > “Twenty-two letters can be learned in a few weeks, where the wedge-shaped signs of Babylon took years.”

    Chapter 12, line 147, has already established the twenty-two-letter alphabet, ink and contrast with cuneiform. The later versions repeat the explanation and increasingly confuse learning signs with acquiring scribal competence.

    **Replace in chapter 13:** “Aramaic also let officials send their messages on light sheets of leather or papyrus.”

    **Replace in chapter 14:** “In Judah, alphabetic writing spread beyond royal monuments into the records and messages of everyday administration.”

29. **A signpost also introduces a chronology contradiction.** [08-empire/chapter.md:89](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:89)

    > “That brings us to the great weakness of what Sargon had made, and it showed itself the moment he died.”

    “That brings us” narrates the chapter’s movement. The next paragraph says risings had already occurred during Sargon’s old age.

    **Replace:** “The cities rebelled in Sargon’s old age, and his death gave them another opening.”

30. **The narrator announces an answer instead of giving it.** [08-empire/chapter.md:70](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:70)

    > “Sargon understood that they were not enough, and his answer to that was the cleverest thing he did.”

    This is precisely the prohibited “an answer is coming” construction, with an unearned superlative attached.

    **Replace:** “Sargon also placed his family inside the religious institutions of the cities he conquered.”

31. **“Easy to state” has become a repeated narrator’s tic.** [09-law/chapter.md:74](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:74)

    > “A conqueror in that position has a problem that is easy to state.”

    Compare chapter 08, line 140: “Underneath all three causes lay a weakness in the thing itself, and it is easy to state.” Both delay the information while advertising its simplicity.

    **Replace the chapter 09 sentence:** “Hammurabi now ruled cities accustomed to their own kings and their own judgments.”

    **Replace the chapter 08 sentence:** “Holding conquered cities required a steady flow of food and labour.”

32. **The account of the wheel’s spread stacks uncertainty around an unnecessary origin claim.** [10-wheel/chapter.md:51](chapters/10-wheel/chapter.md:51)

    > “Most likely it was invented once, or very nearly, and then it spread from farm to farm with remarkable speed.”

    “Most likely,” “once, or very nearly,” and the imagined farm-to-farm route weaken a point that can be made directly from the distribution already described.

    **Replace:** “Within a few centuries, wheeled transport appears in evidence across a vast stretch of Europe and the Middle East.”

    Remove the following sentence if this replacement makes it redundant.

33. **The Sea Peoples reconstruction contains a hedge stack and then an absolute.** [11-bronze/chapter.md:146](chapters/11-bronze/chapter.md:146)

    > “The peoples whom the Egyptians fought were probably not a single nation of pirates. More likely they were a mixture of the displaced: farmers whose crops had failed, soldiers whose palaces could no longer pay them, sailors who had turned to plunder.”

    The occupational biographies are conjectural. The following claim that homeward-bound raiders do not bring families is another exception-breaking absolute.

    **Replace the passage:** “The Egyptian carvings show families travelling in ox carts alongside the fighting men, so the movement included people carrying their households with them.”

34. **A crowded paragraph loses the ruler who performs its next action.** [12-assyria/chapter.md:66](chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:66)

    > “He also began to abolish the more troublesome of the tribute-paying kingdoms altogether, and to turn them into provinces under Assyrian governors, which was the answer that Sargon of Akkad had found to the same problem sixteen centuries earlier.”

    “He” reaches back across the cavalry discussion to an unnamed king. Army reform, chariot development, annexation and the Akkadian comparison are compressed into one paragraph.

    **Replace, beginning a new paragraph:** “The same king also tightened his control over rebellious subjects. He replaced their rulers with Assyrian governors, turning tribute-paying kingdoms into provinces.”

    The Sargon comparison can follow as a separate sentence if retained.

35. **An ambiguous pronoun spoils the tooth example.** [09-law/chapter.md:91](chapters/09-law/chapter.md:91)

    > “He can afford to knock out the tooth of a poorer neighbour of his own rank, or a dozen of them.”

    “Them” could mean teeth or neighbours; the intervening qualifications also bury the point.

    **Replace:** “A wealthy man can afford to pay for teeth he knocks out.”

36. **Chapter 08’s ending summarizes instead of landing.** [08-empire/chapter.md:156](chapters/08-empire/chapter.md:156)

    > “That is what the first empire left behind. The kingdom itself was gone in a few generations. The wish to rule the four quarters of the world was not, and it has not gone away since.”

    The previous paragraph already ends on the captured victory stone. This adds an announced summary and a generalized modern moral, contrary to the ending rule.

    **Replace:** “A thousand years after Akkad fell, another conqueror still wanted Naram-Sin’s victory stone for himself.”

    Alternatively, end on the existing sentence immediately before this paragraph: “He wanted to own it.”
