## Factual flags

1. **Major — the harbour was threatened, not lost.**

   > “With the harbour lost and the wall crumbling, everything depended on help from the west.”

   Ottoman ships entering the Golden Horn did not give Mehmed possession of the whole harbour. Christian ships continued operating there. Barbaro describes their harbour organisation on 10 May and a Christian vessel passing through the defensive boom on 23 May.

   **Correct fact:** The portage exposed the harbour wall and divided the defenders’ resources; the harbour remained contested. [Nicolo Barbaro’s eyewitness diary, entries for 10 and 23 May](https://deremilitari.org/2016/08/the-siege-of-constantinople-in-1453-according-to-nicolo-barbaro/).

2. **Minor — the new settlement sentence misrepresents the sequence.**

   > “He did not wait for settlers to come of their own accord.”

   In context, this presents compulsory settlement as the immediate alternative to trying voluntary immigration. Mehmed encouraged voluntary settlement as well; its inadequate results helped prompt compulsory transfers.

   **Correct fact:** Repopulation combined inducements to voluntary migrants with forced resettlement. The coercion is real, but the new sentence suppresses an actual part of the policy. [Tuomas Laitila’s study of Mehmed’s policy, pp. 54–55](https://journal.fi/ABzF/article/download/153197/abf04-04laitila/369768); [Joint History Project, *The Ottoman Empire*, p. 46](https://jointhistory.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/workbook1_eng.pdf).

3. **Minor — the replacement architectural superlative is also wrong for 1453.**

   > “Its church of Holy Wisdom, Hagia Sophia, was the largest church in the world.”

   Later:

   > “He ordered a man to climb to the pulpit and proclaim that there was no god but God, and the largest church in the world became a mosque.”

   The earlier flag about dome width has been fixed, but “largest church” introduces a different problem. Cluny III had surpassed Hagia Sophia centuries before the conquest. The second sentence unambiguously assigns the superlative to 1453.

   **Correct fact:** Hagia Sophia had been the world’s largest church for centuries, but no longer held that distinction in 1453. [Smarthistory’s architectural account of Cluny Abbey](https://smarthistory.org/cluny-abbey/).

4. **Major — the earlier artillery-exclusivity problem remains.**

   > “To keep them in numbers took far more money than any baron or town had.”

   The wording has changed since `check.md`, but the factual claim has not been fixed. Florence manufactured and fielded substantial artillery. The new sentence about weapons belonging to “kings and sultans” reinforces the same misleading exclusion.

   **Correct fact:** Maintaining artillery required substantial revenue and organisation, which some city-states also possessed. The interpretation that artillery favoured well-financed states can stand without excluding towns categorically. [Fabrizio Ansani, “Craftsmen, Artillery, and War Production in Renaissance Florence”](https://www.academia.edu/17054789/Craftsmen_artillery_and_war_production_in_Renaissance_Florence_Vulcan_The_international_journal_of_the_social_history_of_military_technology_4_2016_).

5. **Minor — the exact fourfold salary comparison misreads the source.**

   > “The sultan gave him four times what the emperor had promised, along with a supply of bronze, and told him to start.”

   Doukas says that a quarter of Mehmed’s remuneration would have been enough to keep Orban in Constantinople. That hypothetical comparison does not establish that Mehmed paid exactly four times Constantine’s promised salary.

   **Correct fact:** Mehmed paid Orban generously and supplied bronze; the exact ratio to the emperor’s promise is not established by this passage. [Gábor Ágoston, “Ottoman Artillery and European Military Technology,” note 69, quoting Doukas](https://www.reenactor.ru/ARH/PDF/Agoston_01.pdf).

## Clarity flags

- **Signpost sentences:**  
  > “The record of this defence speaks for itself.”  
  > “The reason lies in what the wall had been designed to resist.”

  Both announce the evidence or explanation immediately following them. These fall under the style sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences.

- **An absolute replaces a defensible emphasis:**  
  > “There were a dozen such bands, and at first nothing marked this one out except its place on the map.”

  “Nothing … except” excludes every other distinction. Geography can carry the chosen interpretation without this unnecessary universal claim.

- **Another unnecessary absolute:**  
  > “The sultan’s great fleet had failed in front of his whole army.”

  “Whole army” demands that every contingent witnessed the battle. The preceding sentence already establishes its public character.

- **Sea supply is made an unconditional guarantee:**  
  > “A city under siege can hold out for as long as food and reinforcements can reach it…”  
  > “Guns at the land wall would not be enough, then, as long as ships could still come in.”

  Supplies improve endurance; they do not prevent a successful assault. The new sentence strengthens an existing overstatement into a rule that the chapter’s eventual storming of the city makes particularly awkward.

- **Local contradiction about the chain:**  
  > “As long as it held, the harbour was safe…”  
  > “The chain still held, and yet it no longer kept the harbour safe.”

  The intended distinction is clear after reflection: the chain blocked the entrance but could be bypassed. The earlier sentence makes safety conditional only on the chain remaining intact, so the later sentence contradicts its literal wording.

- **Repeated physical explanation:**  
  > “This rough barricade turned out to be better against cannon than stone was, because earth swallows a ball without shattering.”  
  > “Stone cracks and falls, whereas a heap of earth and wool simply takes the ball in.”

  These explain the same contrast twice without adding a step to the mechanism.

- **Padding immediately before the substance:**  
  > “The arrangement was a bargain with two sides.”  
  > “The besiegers, for their part, had troubles of their own.”

  The following sentences supply the two sides and the troubles. These additions contribute little orientation.

- **Repeated conclusion about the name:**  
  > “The conquest, then, did not invent the word.”

  The opening already says the name predates the conquest, and the revised paragraph has just repeated that fact. This is a third statement of the same conclusion.

- **An unexplained technical term:**  
  > “The Ottomans had a word for this kind of removal…”

  The sentence raises an expectation that the word will be supplied, then never supplies it. Either the terminology matters enough to explain or this setup is unnecessary.

- **Potentially confusing ignition vocabulary:**  
  > “…one of them put a burning match to a small hole at the back.”

  A general reader may picture a modern matchstick. Here “match” means a burning slow-match cord; the term needs the brief explanation required by the style sheet.
