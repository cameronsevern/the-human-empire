## Factual flags

1. **Minor — “The oldest war that we can look at closely took place about ten thousand years ago.”**  
   This contradicts the chapter’s later account of Jebel Sahaba. That site supplies closely studied evidence of repeated intergroup violence at least **13,400 years old**, considerably earlier than Nataruk. “One of the oldest” would resolve the problem. [Jebel Sahaba reassessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8159958/).

2. **Minor — “Measured against their numbers, it is twice the toll that war took on Germany or Russia in the century of the two world wars.”**  
   The comparison concerns an **annual death rate**, not the cumulative toll over the periods described. The underlying estimates are about 330 deaths per 100,000 people per year for the Murngin, versus 160 for Germany and 150 for Russia during **1900–1990**. Specify “about twice the annual rate”; otherwise twenty years appears to be compared directly with a century. [Data and source references](https://ourworldindata.org/ethnographic-and-archaeological-evidence-on-violent-deaths).

3. **Minor — “It held sixty-one people.”**  
   Sixty-one is the number examined in the reassessment, not the cemetery’s known total. The researchers identify **at least sixty-four individuals**, including three whose remains were unavailable. [Reassessment, Materials and methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8159958/).

4. **Minor — “About two thirds of them had been wounded by weapons.” / “Sixteen of the dead carry old wounds that had healed and fresh ones that had not.”**  
   These figures conflate **all bone lesions** with traumatic injuries. Forty-one of sixty-one individuals—67.2%—had lesions; thirty-eight—62.3%—had identifiable trauma, not all specifically demonstrated weapon wounds. Sixteen had both healed and unhealed *lesions*, but only ten had both healed and unhealed injuries classified as trauma. The repeated-violence interpretation remains supported; the numerical descriptions need correcting. [Reassessment, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8159958/).

5. **Minor — “Neither of these peoples farmed, and neither had a king.”**  
   The farming claim remains incorrect for the Northwest Coast example, despite the earlier wording change. Peoples there cultivated edible roots in maintained gardens, including transplanting, weeding and selective harvesting. Their economies relied heavily on fishing, but that does not mean they lacked cultivation. [Douglas Deur’s research on Northwest Coast gardens](https://repository.lsu.edu/gradschool_disstheses/7259/).

6. **Major — “So war does not grow from farming, or kings, or some poison in the blood. It grows from a pile of food that cannot be moved, and a crowd of neighbours who know where it is.”**  
   As an explanation of war’s origin, this contradicts the chapter’s own accounts of mobile raiders fighting over revenge, women and sorcery. The supported distinction is that stored food and defensible resources can make warfare more rewarding and escape harder—not that warfare requires them. The chapter’s earlier examples establish this internally; comparative research also records lethal conflict arising from several different motives. [Fry and Söderberg’s study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23869015/).

## Clarity flags

1. **“Africa, Australia and the Nile, from before our species to about 10,000 years ago.”**  
   The subtitle does not describe the chapter’s actual range: substantial sections concern twentieth-century observations, and North America supplies a major example.

2. **“The answer comes in two halves, and the first half is flattering.” / “What they did next is the telling part.” / “The second half is less comfortable, and it is hiding in the story just told.”**  
   These explicitly announce answers and revelations, breaking the style sheet’s **“No signpost sentences”** rule. The repeated “half of the answer” framing also makes the section feel constructed around an argument rather than narrated.

3. **“That is a puzzle, since our nearest relatives are nothing like it.”**  
   “That” and “it” have unclear referents. The intended puzzle seems to be how humans became comparatively tolerant despite their relationship to more physically aggressive apes.

4. **“People who believe that the world is full of wills seldom believe in accidents.”**  
   “Full of wills” is opaque for a general reader. Explain that events are understood as the actions of spirits or other intentional beings.

5. **“All of them depend on one condition.” / “Take that condition away, and the limits go with it.”**  
   The preceding four restraints include marriage connections and voluntary participation. The text explains why settlement changes mobility and possessions, but not why it removes those social restraints. That missing link makes the transition difficult to follow.

6. **“Yet when a scholar counted the killings that they remembered, he found that they had murdered one another more often than people do in the United States.”**  
   The comparison leaves both periods unstated and switches from historical recollections to a present-tense national rate. Briefly identify the historical period and the US comparison year.

7. **“So the typical act of war, for most of human history, was not a battle.”**  
   This closely repeats the opening’s “for most of our history a war was a night raid … not a battle.” The following “murder committed by a committee” already delivers the point memorably; the repeated formulation adds little.

## Overall

The revision resolves many earlier problems and reads fluently. The main remaining substantive issue is the closing explanation, which contradicts the chapter’s broader account of why people fight.
