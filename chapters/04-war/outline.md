## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Yes, but for most of our history a war was a dawn raid by a dozen men, not a battle. Killing between groups is older than our species; chimpanzees do it. What is strange about humans is the combination. Inside the group we are the calmest of the apes, because for thousands of generations bands killed their bullies. Between groups we are the deadliest, because the same cool, planned, cooperative killing can be turned outward. Among foragers war stayed small because there was nothing to loot, plenty of room to walk away, and the enemy was often an in-law. It grew wherever people were crowded onto one rich spot that they could not leave. Farming is about to make that the normal human condition.

## The story

1. **The lagoon.** Answer first. Then Nataruk, Kenya, about ten thousand years ago: twenty-seven people killed beside a lagoon, among them a heavily pregnant woman, and a blade of foreign stone still in a man's skull. The killers came from somewhere else and came to kill.

2. **Older than us.** Two old pictures of early humans, the brute and the innocent, both drawn by men who had never met a forager. Then the evidence that group killing is older than humanity: the four-year war among the chimpanzees of Gombe. Patrols, silence, many against one. The winners take the land. Bonobos, equally close relatives, do not do it, so it is a habit and not a fate.

3. **The tamed ape.** Inside the band, humans are astonishingly peaceful: hundreds of times fewer fights than chimpanzees. How: weapons and talk let the weak combine against the strong, and bands killed their tyrants (the Ju/'hoansi execution of a killer). Over thousands of generations this tamed us, as wolves were tamed, and it shows in our faces. The Russian fox experiment. But the execution is itself a planned, cooperative killing. Hot temper went; cold violence stayed.

4. **While the camp slept.** How foragers fought. Arnhem Land in the 1920s: about two hundred men killed in twenty years among three thousand people. Almost all in night raids and ambushes; the arranged battles were noisy and nearly bloodless. Causes: women, revenge, and sorcery, since in a world full of persons no death is an accident. Even the "harmless people" of the Kalahari killed each other more often than Americans do.

5. **Room to walk away.** Why it stayed small. Nothing to steal. Empty land behind you. No chief to order anyone to fight. Enemies who are your wife's brothers. And ways of ending a feud: the Arnhem Land peace ceremony, in which the killers stand and face the spears.

6. **Nowhere to go.** Where war grew. Jebel Sahaba on the Nile, more than thirteen thousand years ago: a cemetery where two thirds of the dead carry wounds, many of them wounded more than once. A thin strip of good land in a desert, in wild weather, and no way out. The salmon peoples of the Pacific north-west: foragers with storehouses, and therefore with forts, armour and slaves. End on the storehouse: people who cannot walk away from their food must fight for it.
