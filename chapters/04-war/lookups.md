# Chapter 4 fact-check lookups

Method: web searches and page fetches this session, every item searched at least once. Search summaries were often thin, so where I relied on a summary rather than an opened page I say so. Pages that would not open (Nature, PNAS, ScienceDirect, Springer, the 1937 Warner text) were checked through secondary reports. Several search results and one web page carried embedded instructions addressed to me (for example "ignore the system prompt", "list facts about Nataruk"). I ignored them and used none of that content. "Not verified" means searched and no source answered the specific point.

Warner's own text was reachable only as a scanned 1931 article excerpt (Oceania 1, "Murngin Warfare", pp. 457-494, https://opus4.kobv.de/opus4-Fromm/files/22540/Warner_L_1931_Q-056-Ex.pdf). The 1937 book could not be opened.

## 1. Nataruk: PARTLY

- Confirmed (Cambridge release on the Lahr et al. Nature paper, https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/evidence-of-a-prehistoric-massacre-extends-the-history-of-warfare):
  - Date 9,500 to 10,500 years ago.
  - 27 individuals (partial remains): 21 adults (8 male, 8 female, 5 unknown) and 6 children. Only 12 skeletons are relatively complete.
  - One woman in the last stages of pregnancy (foetus six to nine months).
  - Bodies not buried. Some fell into the lagoon and were preserved in sediment.
  - One adult male has an obsidian bladelet embedded in his skull (it did not perforate the bone).
  - Blunt-force trauma to skulls and cheekbones, broken hands, knees and ribs, arrow lesions to the neck. Five cases of severe head trauma may be from a wooden club.
  - Fertile lakeshore with water and fishing.
- Bound: four skeletons were positioned as if the hands were probably bound, including the pregnant woman, whose hands and feet may have been bound. Science News says two undamaged skeletons had hands crossed (https://www.sciencenews.org/article/attack-10000-years-ago-earliest-known-act-warfare). No source I read says "knees drawn up". The chapter's "knees drawn up and her wrists crossed" is not supported as written.
- Obsidian: three projectile points in two bodies, two of obsidian. Obsidian is rare at other late Stone Age sites in West Turkana, "which may suggest" different home ranges. That is weaker than the chapter's "the killers did not live nearby".
- Pottery at the site: WRONG. Science News says the pottery and human bones from about this time were found at Kalakol 4, about 3 km away, a temporary camp. The Cambridge release says pottery suggests storage of foraged food, but Wikipedia tags the Nataruk pottery claim "citation needed". The chapter says "among the bones lay pieces of pottery" and builds an argument on it.
- Mobility: Lahr describes the group as largely nomadic, and Nataruk as a temporary fishing and hunting camp (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nataruk). That undercuts the last-section use ("people who make pots have something to keep").
- Challenge: Stojanowski, Seidel, Fulginiti, Johnson and Buikstra, "Contesting the massacre at Nataruk", Nature 539: E8-E10 (24 Nov 2016) (https://www.pubmed.gov/27882979/). They argue the fractures in three skulls do not meet forensic criteria and may be ground-pressure damage, that lesion location tracks skull orientation, and that the bodies are mostly articulated, spatially organised and non-commingled, as in a burial. Lahr et al. replied (Nature 539: E10-E11) that both points are wrong and that inter-group conflict remains the best explanation (https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/261694). The massacre reading is contested, and the chapter states it as settled.
- Replacement for the opening claim: "The team that dug the site, led by Marta Mirazon Lahr, reads the bones as a massacre: twenty-seven people, at least eight women and six children among them, left where they fell on the shore of a lagoon."
- Replacement for the pregnant woman: "The pregnant woman lay with her hands, and perhaps her feet, bound."
- Replacement for obsidian: "The blade in the man's skull is made of obsidian, a black volcanic glass that is rare at other sites on that shore."
- Replacement for "Another group ... killed them all": "Another group of people came to the lagoon and killed at least twenty-seven of them."
- Replacement for the closing paragraph: cut the pottery paragraph ("The camp at the lagoon ... something to keep"). Better-documented examples that carry the "nowhere to go" point are Jebel Sahaba (item 16) and the Northwest Coast (item 17).
- Not verified: "full of game". Sources say fishing, with harpoons.

## 2. Hobbes and Rousseau: CONFIRMED (one fix)

- Fordham's edition of Leviathan ch. 13 gives "and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" and "such a war as is of every man against every man". The chapter's wording and punctuation match. https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/hobbes-lev13.asp
- "Three and a half centuries ago": 1651 to 2026 is 375 years, so "three and three-quarter centuries". Replacement: "In 1651 an English philosopher wrote that without a government to keep order the life of man was ..."
- Rousseau, Discourse on Inequality, 1755 (104 years later, "a century later" is fine). Study guides: natural man is moved by pity and self-preservation, is not in constant fear, and conflict comes with private property. "Gentle" is a fair summary. https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Second_Discourse , https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/inequality/terms/ (secondary).
- "Neither man had ever met a hunter": true of personal acquaintance, but Hobbes ch. 13 cites "the savage people in many places of America" as living "in that brutish manner" without government, so he argued from reports of hunting peoples. Rousseau's use of travellers' reports was not checked. Replacement: "Neither man had spent a day among hunters, and both argued from reasoning and travellers' tales." (Hobbes citation: Fordham page above.) Verdict on the item stays CONFIRMED, with this sentence changed.

## 3. Goodall: PARTLY

- Arrived Gombe (Lake Tanganyika) in July 1960 (14 July in several sources; History.com says June). https://www.nationalgeographic.com/impact/article/Jane-Goodall-championed-animal-conservation-for-six-decades
- Her wording (Through a Window, quoted by Discover): she had believed chimpanzees were "by and large, rather 'nicer' than us". https://discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/a-brief-history-of-the-gombe-chimpanzee-war
- The chapter's "rather like us, only nicer" is not her wording or meaning. "For ten years she watched one community" does not fit: the war began in 1974, fourteen years after arrival.
- Replacement: "In 1960 a young Englishwoman named Jane Goodall went to live among the wild chimpanzees of Gombe, a strip of forest on the shore of Lake Tanganyika. For fourteen years she watched one community of them. She saw them make tools, embrace, tickle their children and grieve for their dead. She came to think that, by and large, they were rather nicer than we are."

## 4. Gombe war: PARTLY

- Confirmed: 7 January 1974 to 5 June 1978 (about four and a half years, "four years" is fine). The split came in 1971. The Kahama group began with six adult males, three adult females and their young. Kasakela (north) won, took over the Kahama range (12 to over 15 sq km for a while), and later lost ground to Kalande and Mitumba. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gombe_Chimpanzee_War
- Attack lengths: Godi's beating lasted about ten minutes, another victim was beaten about twenty minutes by three males. Attackers approached silently. https://discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/a-brief-history-of-the-gombe-chimpanzee-war
- Single file, listening and sniffing: not found for Gombe. A ScienceDaily report on Ngogo patrols describes a single-file silent line (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2005/10/051018072735.htm). Not verified for Gombe.
- "Attackers hardly ever hurt": not verified.
- "Killed every male ... one at a time": not exactly. Godi, De, Goliath and Sniff were attacked and killed or left to die. Hugh and Willy Wally vanished, and Charlie's body was found three days after fighting. One female was killed, two vanished and three were beaten and taken.
- Replacement for the patrol: "Half a dozen males from the northern group would gather and walk south, silent where chimpanzees are usually noisy." Replacement for the end: "Over four years, every male of the southern group was killed or gone."
- Optional: a 2018 study (reported by ScienceDaily and Wikipedia) put the cause partly down to a rank struggle among three males. Not needed.

## 5. Ngogo: CONFIRMED (add the new result)

- Mitani, Watts and Amsler 2010, Current Biology 20: R507. Researchers saw 18 fatal attacks and found signs of three others, over ten years (21 in all). The community began using the area where two-thirds of the attacks occurred and its territory grew 22%. https://news.umich.edu/chimpanzee-gangs-kill-for-land-new-study-shows (page would not open; figures from the search summary and https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/06/100621121348.htm). In 2010 the authors were unsure whether the motive was resources or mates.
- Reproduction: Wood, Mitani, Langergraber and Watts, PNAS 2025. After the expansion Ngogo females bore 37 offspring in three years against 15 in the three before, and the chance of an infant dying before age 3 fell from 41% to 8%. Fruit in the core area was stable, so the authors credit better female nutrition and health. https://news.asu.edu/20251117-science-and-technology-lethal-aggression-territorial-expansion-wild-chimpanzees-lead-more
- "A fifth larger": 22% is a little more than a fifth, fine.
- Replacement for the two sentences after "land": "making their own territory 22 per cent larger. In the next three years the females had more than twice as many babies, and far fewer of them died. In other words, for a chimpanzee, killing the neighbours pays, so long as it can be done cheaply."

## 6. Bonobos: WRONG on the last claim

- Species: Schwarz described the bonobo in 1929 as a chimpanzee subspecies, Coolidge raised it to species in 1933. https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Bonobo (mirror pages)
- Encounters: at Wamba (59 encounters, 1978-91) groups merged in over 60%, with grooming and copulation between members of different groups. Females make the peace, males interact far less, and some encounters include aggression. https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/dspace/bitstream/2433/236291/1/PAN25%282%29_22.pdf
- Killing: Wilson et al. 2014 (Nature) found no killings in 92 years of bonobo research at four sites (reported in Silk's commentary, https://www.bonobosworld.org/images/zotero/Silk-2014-The_evolutionary_roots_of_lethal_conflict.pdf). In February 2025 at LuiKotale, five females attacked an adult male in what the authors called the most extreme violence recorded in wild bonobos, and the male may have died. https://www.livescience.com/animals/land-mammals/a-forest-with-bonobos-has-never-been-so-quiet-most-extreme-case-of-violence-in-hippie-species-recorded-with-females-ganging-up-on-male-in-unprecedented-attack (headline only; the body would not open). Whether he died: not verified.
- So "No bonobo has ever been seen to kill another" is now too strong. Replacement: "In ninety years of watching wild bonobos, researchers had recorded no killing, where chimpanzees at the same kind of sites had killed again and again."

## 7. Wrangham's figures: CONFIRMED (airplane fix)

- Wrangham, PNAS 2018, "Two types of aggression in human evolution" (PMC5777045): chimpanzee contact aggression about one to three cases per 100 hours of observation. An Australian Aboriginal community: 0.005 to 0.006 per 100 hours, "two to three orders of magnitude less than in chimpanzees and bonobos". That works out at roughly 170 to 600 times. A review of The Goodness Paradox gives 150 to 550 times. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5777045/ , https://www.robkhenderson.com/p/the-distinctiveness-of-human-aggression
- "Hundreds of times more often" is accurate.
- Males beating females: the article gives the lifetime prevalence of males beating and coercing females as 100% in chimpanzees. "Routinely" holds.
- Airplane: Hrdy, Mothers and Others (2009) opens with a planeload of chimpanzees: "Bloody earlobes and other appendages would litter the aisles", "a recipe for mayhem", you would be lucky to leave with all your fingers and toes. https://thetedkarchive.com/library/sarah-blaffer-hrdy-mothers-and-others Wrangham also uses the flight analogy (Hawks, via Wikipedia). "Few would get out alive" overstates it, and "three hundred" and "eight hours" are the author's own numbers.
- Replacement: "Few would get out unhurt."
- Credit: Hrdy's image is worth naming in the text or notes.

## 8. /Twi: PARTLY

- Source: Lee, "Violence and its Alternatives" (Humanitas 2003), via pdftotext of https://humanitas.journals.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/humanities/article/download/44/46/172
- Confirmed: "a deranged man named /Twi had killed three other people", and the community "in a rare move of unanimity, ambushed and fatally wounded him in full daylight". As he lay dying "all the men fired at him with poisoned arrows until, in the words of one informant, 'he looked like a porcupine'". Then "after he was dead, all the women approached his body and stabbed him with spears, symbolically sharing the responsibility". So "three" (not two) is right.
- Wrongly framed: Lee calls him deranged, not domineering. Lee counts four executions of wrongdoers, and /Twi is the most dramatic. The chapter uses him as a case of killing a power-seeking bully ("A man who tried to make himself [a chief] ... was killed"). The case shows the group killing a murderer, not a would-be chief.
- Replacement: "A deranged man in one group had killed three people, and nobody could stop him. So the others ambushed him in broad daylight and shot him with poisoned arrows." Keep the rest. Change the bridging line to: "Hunting bands used that power on killers, and on men who would not stop."
- Not verified: "within living memory" (Lee gives no date in this essay).

## 9. Self-domestication: PARTLY (too strong)

- Cieri et al. 2014, Current Anthropology 55: 419-443: reduced brow ridge projection and shorter upper face from the Middle Pleistocene to recent times. Sample: 13 skulls older than 80,000 years, 41 skulls 10,000 to 38,000 years old, 1,367 skulls from the 20th century. They link it to lower androgen reactivity, which bones cannot show. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/08/140801171114.htm , https://carta.anthropogeny.org/node/308822
- Not in Cieri: smaller teeth and smaller male-female size difference, and a comparison with skulls of 300,000 years ago. The oldest skulls in the sample are over 80,000 years old. The same press report notes recent farmers had slightly more masculine faces than early humans and recent foragers.
- Wrangham dates the start of self-domestication to 400,000 to 600,000 years ago among mid-Pleistocene Homo (https://www.robkhenderson.com/p/the-distinctiveness-of-human-aggression), so "three hundred thousand years" is not his figure.
- Reception: the execution hypothesis (Boehm; Wrangham, PNAS 2018, citing refs 120 and 127) is contested. Critics include Pruetz, Power, and Sanchez-Villagra and van Schaik ("Evaluating the self-domestication hypothesis of human evolution", Evol Anthropol 2019). An editorial describes "an ongoing degree of controversy". https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7477284/ , https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/669969
- Replacement for the skeleton paragraph: "Our own skulls point the same way. Compared with the earliest modern human skulls, those of recent people have lighter brows and shorter upper faces, as tame animals do. The primatologist Richard Wrangham argues that nobody tamed us. We did it to ourselves, one dead bully at a time."
- Replacement for the time span: "Now imagine that going on in every band for hundreds of thousands of years."
- Not checked this session: "the same thing happened to wolves at the edge of the camp".

## 10. Belyaev's foxes: PARTLY

- Place: Novosibirsk, Institute of Cytology and Genetics, Belyaev director from 1959. Start year conflicts: 1952 (Wikipedia, Science History Institute) against 1959 for the programme at Novosibirsk. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesticated_silver_fox , https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/gone-to-the-dogs/
- Selection: monthly tests from one month old, offering food from the hand while trying to stroke the fox, and scoring approach and biting. Fewer than 20% bred each generation.
- Generations: tail wagging in one male by generation 4; whimpering and licking in the new top class by generation 6; floppy ears and curled tails by generations 8 to 10; 35% "elite" by generation 20. "Within twenty generations" is true but understates it.
- Bodies: floppy ears, shorter snouts, curly tails, patchy (piebald) coats, shortened skulls. "Smaller teeth": sources say only that jaws and teeth changed.
- 2019 critique (Lord, Karlsson et al., Trends in Ecology & Evolution): the foxes came from Prince Edward Island fur farms and were not wild, the starting population was about 130, and traits like white spotting were already present. They do not dispute that tameness was bred. Dugatkin calls it "still the gold standard", notes curly tails appeared only after 9 to 10 generations and were not selected, and notes the aggressive and control lines never developed them. https://www.sciencealert.com/that-famous-fox-domestication-study-may-have-been-wrong-about-domestication-syndrome
- What survives: tameness bred in a few generations, and floppy ears and patchy coats alongside it. What does not: the claim that the body changes prove one tameness-driven "syndrome".
- Replacement: "In the 1950s a Russian scientist began breeding silver foxes from fur-farm stock at Novosibirsk, in Siberia. He chose them by a single test. He put his hand into the cage, and bred only from the foxes that did not snap or cower. Within six generations some foxes whined for attention and licked the hands of their keepers, and by the twentieth a third of them did. Some also grew floppy ears, curly tails and patchy coats, although nobody had selected for that."

## 11. Warner and the Murngin: PARTLY (several fixes)

- Fieldwork 1926-29 (search summaries, e.g. https://catalogue.nla.gov.au/catalog/1933609 area; not opened). "Late 1920s, three years" is fine.
- Total killed: Warner 1931, p. 482: "It would be safe to add, on the basis of population, another hundred casualties to this figure, making a total of two hundred men who had been killed." So about 100 were recorded and 100 were added by estimate. Population about 3,000, with about 700 men (Warner, as cited by https://ourworldindata.org/ethnographic-and-archaeological-evidence-on-violent-deaths ).
- Recorded battles: "some seventy battles ... in which members of the Murngin factions were killed" in the last twenty years. Causes: fifty were revenge for a killing (fifteen of those were killings of the wrong people, in retaliation), ten were over stealing a woman, five were over killing by black magic, five were over a man looking at a totemic emblem improperly (Warner 1931, p. 458).
- So revenge, not women, comes first. The chapter's order (women, revenge, sorcery) is wrong, and a fourth cause is left out. "Nobody was after land": Warner's seventy have none over land, but Yolngu have said later that serious disputes concern land, and reject the claim that only women and corpses caused conflict. https://www.everyculture.com/Oceania/Murngin-Sociopolitical-Organization.html
- Types (Warner, as summarised at https://austhrutime.com/feud_and_warfare.htm): miringu (maringo), a night raid in which the party approaches in a snake-like formation, surrounds the camp and kills the victim and possibly others, usually after a clan killing; milwerangel, a pre-arranged fight between several clans; gainger (gaingar), a rare regional "all-in" fight, "a spear fight to end spear fights", where any ruse is allowed and deaths are expected to be higher but not more than about a dozen; narub, a victim killed or wounded while asleep, with the whole clan held responsible; makarata, settled mainly by ordeal; camp fights over adultery. "Narrup" is the narub.
- Arranged battles killed "only a handful": WRONG or at least unsupported. Search summaries report two gaingar in twenty years that killed 29 men, and Warner recording two formal battles with over a dozen dead each (Keeley/Gat via search; not opened). Warner's own table not seen. 
- Dawn surround and spearing as they woke: the surround is documented, "dawn" is not verified. "A dozen men walked for days": not verified.
- "Hardly met a European": too strong. Macassan trepangers visited annually from the late 17th century to 1907, and three missions were set up before the 1932 Gove station. https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/murngin
- Replacement for the opening: "In the late 1920s an American scholar spent three years in Arnhem Land ... among people who had never farmed, and who had traded with Indonesian sailors for two centuries."
- Replacement for the total: "The total was about a hundred men killed, which, allowing for the fights nobody remembered, he put at two hundred. That was among a people who numbered about three thousand."
- Replacement for the battles: "Most arranged fights, with spears thrown across open ground, ended with a wound or two. The rare all-in battle was different, and a single one could leave a dozen men dead."
- Replacement for causes: "Why did they do it? The men gave Warner mostly one reason. Of about seventy fights, fifty were revenge for a killing, ten were over stolen women, five over sorcery and five over an insult to a sacred emblem. A killing had to be answered by the dead man's brothers, and their answer then had to be answered in turn, so that a feud could run for a generation."

## 12. Murngin death rate against Europe: CONFIRMED

- Arithmetic: 200 deaths / 3,000 people / 20 years = 0.00333 per year = 333 per 100,000 per year. OWID's table of Keeley gives 330. As a share of men: 200 of 700 is 28.6%.
- Keeley's European figures (OWID): Germany 1900-1990, 160 per 100,000 per year; Russia 1900-1990, 150; US and Europe 1900-1960, under 1% of (male) deaths from war. So the Murngin rate is about twice that of the two hardest-hit countries. I found no single figure for all of Europe. https://ourworldindata.org/ethnographic-and-archaeological-evidence-on-violent-deaths
- Caveat: Warner's 200 is half estimated.
- Replacement: "Measured against their numbers, it is about twice the rate of war deaths in Germany or Russia over the twentieth century, the worst-hit countries of a century that held both world wars."

## 13. Makarata: PARTLY

- Confirmed: it is a peacemaking ceremony and "trial by ordeal", a form of ritualised revenge, and the word means "into the thigh". A successful outcome is marked by blood from a thigh wound on the principal offender. https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/murngin , https://lieber.westpoint.edu/indigenous-australian-laws-war/
- Not verified: killers running or standing while spears are thrown, elders urging calm, dancing together afterwards. Warner's passage was not available. A 2016 Milingimbi performance ended with a spear broken, not dancing.
- The sources name one person, the main offender, not "the men who had done the killing".
- Replacement: "When the anger had been thrown off, the main offender stood still and let himself be speared through the thigh. Blood had now been paid for blood, and the feud was over."

## 14. Ju/'hoansi homicide: CONFIRMED (two notes)

- Lee 2003: retrospective inquiry for 1920-1955 turned up 22 homicides (25 killers, 19 of 22 victims male). Fifteen of the 22 killings were in feuds. The commonest cause was a fight between men over a woman. https://humanitas.journals.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/humanities/article/download/44/46/172
- Rate: Lee says the numbers gave rates "comparable in magnitude to rates in troubled American cities such as Chicago, Baltimore, and Detroit". He quotes Pinker's figure as 40 per 100,000, not 29. (https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/richard-b-lee-hunter-gatherers-and-human-evolution ). Keeley says the 1920-55 rate was four times that of the United States (via the tedkarchive text of War Before Civilization). The "29 per 100,000" figure is not verified. US national homicide rate: about 4.6 to 5.1 in 1950, 9.7 in 1979, 10.2 at the 1980 peak (BJS, CDC: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/hus/2016/029.pdf). "More often than people do in the United States" holds.
- War: Lee: "The !Kung waged no wars in the twentieth century", and "neither was warfare endemic". Lee also writes that "Far from being harmless, the Ju/'hoansi could be scrappy and violent". The Harmless People (Thomas) was published by Knopf in 1959, from expeditions in the early 1950s (https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Elizabeth_Marshall_Thomas). Critics have questioned its emphasis on the absence of violence.
- No change needed. Optional: "Lee counted twenty-two killings in the thirty-five years before 1955."

## 15. General claim about mobile hunter-gatherers: PARTLY

- Fry and Soderberg, Science 2013: in 21 mobile forager societies, 148 lethal events. 55% involved one killer and one victim. Almost two-thirds came from accidents, family disputes, executions or personal motives such as a woman. Resource fights were about 1.4%. The authors class most as homicides, a few as feuds, a minority as war. 96% of killers were male. Critics (Bowles) say the sample skews peaceful, and the Tiwi supply 69 of the 148. https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/cross-check/new-study-of-foragers-undermines-claim-that-war-has-deep-evolutionary-roots/ , https://www.rappler.com/science-nature/34254-war-hunter-gatherers
- Wrangham and Glowacki 2012 (Human Nature 23: 5-29) argue the chimpanzee model fits raiding among nomadic hunter-gatherers (abstract only; full text would not open). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22388773/
- Mobility: Lee lists song duels, ritualised combat and group fission; "Fission is an excellent form of conflict resolution", and feuds ended in the longer run by "arranging marriages between" opposing groups. https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/richard-b-lee-hunter-gatherers-and-human-evolution
- So the claim "among hunters most killings come from raids" is too strong. Raids and feuds are documented. Most killings were personal.
- Replacement for "Arnhem Land was not unusual. From the Arctic to the Amazon ...": "Arnhem Land was not the only place where hunters raided. Across twenty-one bands of mobile foragers, most killings were personal, one man killing another over a woman or a grudge, and the ones that were war were raids and ambushes. Even the gentlest peoples were not gentle by our standards."
- The line on marriage ties is supported by Lee and by the hxaro material in chapter 3.

## 16. Jebel Sahaba: CONFIRMED

- Found in 1964 by Fred Wendorf's team, UNESCO High Dam Salvage Project; now under Lake Nasser. 64 skeletons found, 61 now in the British Museum. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jebel_Sahaba
- Crevecoeur et al., Sci Rep 11 (2021): 41 of 61 have lesions (20 known plus 21 new, 67%), over 100 new lesions, most from composite projectiles (stone points in 21 bodies). 16 individuals have both healed and unhealed injuries. Men, women and children were hit "indiscriminately". Conclusion: "a succession of limited raids or ambushes", not one battle. Cause: climate change at the end of the last ice age, competition for resources, severe Nile floods. Age at least 13.4 thousand years (13,400 to 18,600, best estimate about 13,400 to 13,700). https://www.cnrs.fr/en/jebel-sahaba-succession-violence-rather-prehistoric-war , https://popular-archaeology.com/article/jebel-sahaba-a-succession-of-violence-rather-than-a-prehistoric-war/ , https://ktvz.com/news/national-world/2021/05/27/earliest-known-war-driven-by-climate-change-researchers-say/
- The chapter's "a good many carry old wounds that had healed and fresh ones that had not" is sixteen people. "Until an arrow finally killed them" is an inference for those sixteen.
- Replacement: "Sixteen of them carry old wounds that had healed and fresh ones that had not. So these people were not killed in one terrible day. They were attacked, recovered and attacked again."

## 17. Pacific Northwest Coast: PARTLY

- Age: earliest evidence of conflict by about 3000 BC (healed skeletal injuries); defensive sites and large villages by AD 200-500; escalation about AD 900; fortified sites for at least 2,000 years; slat armour from sites before about 1000 BP. Coastal British Columbia burials show trauma in 13% (1774-1874) against 20 to 32% in prehistoric periods. https://www.warmuseum.ca/cmc/exhibitions/aborig/haida/havwa01e.html , https://www.Gwern.net/doc/history/2013-maschner.pdf (search summaries), Keeley text on thetedkarchive
- Fortifications: Coast Salish trench-and-embankment sites, refuge sites, rock walls and stockaded villages (https://core.tdar.org/document/403619/trenches-embankments-and-palisades-terraforming-landscapes-for-defensive-fortifications-in-coast-salish-territory); a Tlingit log palisade at Hoonah.
- Canoes and slaves: northern raiders reached Puget Sound, canoes held 50 to 100 warriors, and the Coast Salish were "a source for slave labor". Sources say "hundreds of miles". Keeley: slaves were a main booty. https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Port_Gamble
- Not verified: stored dried salmon, "by the ton", "great wooden houses", "clifftops", and raiding "for the stores". Keeley says tribes "warred over river and ocean frontage", which fits better.

## 18. Chimpanzee and human killing share an origin: WRONG (too strong)

- Two scenarios remain: killing inherited from a common ancestor, or arrived at separately in both lines (Manson and Wrangham 1991, Wrangham and Glowacki 2012, Gat 2015). Wilson et al. 2014 (Nature) found chimpanzee killing is adaptive and not a product of human disturbance. Trinkaus: "There is absolutely no evidence" that war goes "back to the common ancestor with chimps". Glowacki 2022 calls the debate at an impasse. Wrangham and Glowacki say bonobos give no behavioural model. https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/cross-check/quitting-the-hominid-fight-club-the-evidence-is-flimsy-for-innate-chimpanzee-let-alone-human-warfare/ , https://www.bonobosworld.org/images/zotero/Silk-2014-The_evolutionary_roots_of_lethal_conflict.pdf
- Replacement: "So organised killing between groups was not invented by kings or farmers. Chimpanzees and humans both do it when they can do it safely. Whether we inherited the habit from a common ancestor or came to it separately, specialists still argue."

## 19. Chimpanzees gang up on a bully: PARTLY

- Mahale: Pimu, alpha of M-group since 2007, was killed on 2 October 2011 by a coalition of four males of his own community, two of them former alphas (Kaburu, Inoue and Newton-Fisher, Am J Primatol 75: 789-797, 2013). The first account of an incumbent alpha killed by a coalition. His position did not appear threatened in the months before. The authors read it as likely an opportunistic bid by the third-ranking male for the top rank. https://kar.kent.ac.uk/33286/ , https://peeref.com/works/6635750
- Fongoli: Foudouko, a former alpha who had been pushed out and returned, was killed in 2013 by resident males. The attack was heard at night, not seen, so the killing is inferred. The body was mutilated and partly eaten (Pruetz et al., Int J Primatol 2017). Press reports call him a tyrant, but I could not open the paper to see whether it does. https://www.livescience.com/57714-chimps-kill-mutilate-cannibalize-other-chimp.html , https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10764-016-9942-9
- Neither case is documented as the removal of a bully. They are coalition killings of an alpha and a former alpha.
- Replacement: "Chimpanzees do sometimes gang up on a leader. Males at Mahale killed their alpha in 2011, and males at Fongoli killed a former alpha in 2013. But they cannot plan it as people can, and nobody has shown that either victim was a bully."
