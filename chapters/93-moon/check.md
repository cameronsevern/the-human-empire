1. **Wrong budget attribution; high confidence.** “At its height it employed four hundred thousand people in twenty thousand companies and universities, and took more than four cents of every dollar that the American government spent.”

   The roughly 4.4% figure belongs to **NASA’s entire budget**, not Apollo alone. Attribute that share to NASA. The sentence also stacks three statistics, contrary to “one number per point.” [NASA budget clarification](https://www.nasa.gov/space-science-and-astrobiology-at-ames/interesting-fact-of-the-month-current/interesting-fact-of-the-month-2020/).

2. **Misleading causal claim; high confidence.** “Without him the programme broke up into rival design offices.”

   Rival offices were already competing while Korolev was alive. In 1964, Korolev received the lunar-landing project while Chelomei received a separate circumlunar project. His death weakened leadership of an already divided effort; it did not create the division. [NASA’s *Challenge to Apollo*](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/SP-4408pt1.pdf).

3. **Wrong fuel figure; high confidence.** “When the legs touched the dust, there was fuel left for about twenty-five seconds more.”

   Postflight analysis found approximately **45 seconds of firing time remaining**, including about 20 seconds reserved for an abort. Twenty-five seconds describes the usable landing margin after that allowance, not all remaining fuel. [NASA’s annotated landing transcript](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/a11/a11.landing.html).

4. **Doubtful ending and break of the absolute rule; high confidence.** “The letters will be there for longer than there have been human beings.”

   Lack of wind and rain does not prevent erosion by impacts and their debris. Research using lunar-orbiter images estimates that astronaut tracks disappear over tens of thousands of years, rather than millions. The particular initials have no guaranteed survival time. [NASA’s account of lunar surface turnover](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/earths-moon-hit-by-surprising-number-of-meteoroids/).

5. **Misleading account of Apollo’s legacy; high confidence about chronology.** “Rockets that could reach the Moon could put satellites in orbit by the hundred, and it is satellites that changed daily life.”

   In context, this makes useful satellites sound like a consequence of building Moon rockets. Weather, communications and reconnaissance satellites developed through distinct programmes: TIROS-1 launched in 1960 and Telstar in 1962. Chapter 92 already describes American reconnaissance satellites operating in 1960. The defensible connection is to the wider space race, rather than Apollo specifically. [NASA’s historical timeline](https://www.nasa.gov/specials/timeline/index.html).

6. **Conflict with the earlier chronology; high confidence.** “They also let each side count the other's missiles, which made the first arms agreements possible.”

   Chapter 92 has already described the **1963 nuclear-test-ban treaty**. Counting missile installations relates specifically to later strategic-arms agreements, especially SALT I in 1972, rather than “the first arms agreements” generally. Name that narrower achievement. [State Department history of SALT](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1969-1976/salt).

7. **Mislocated death toll; high confidence.** “About twenty thousand of them died there.”

   “There” points to the underground rocket factory. The approximately 20,000 deaths refer to prisoners of the **whole Mittelbau camp complex**, including people killed after transfers and during evacuation marches. The distinction matters when attributing deaths to the factory itself. [Mittelbau-Dora Memorial’s accounting](https://www.dora.de/de/geschichte/chronologie/konzentrationslager/bilanz).

8. **Incorrect chronology of segregation; high confidence.** “The paths of the early flights were checked by a group of Black women mathematicians in Virginia, who worked in a segregated office and were known as computers.”

   This places the segregated computing unit alongside the crewed spaceflights. The West Area Computing unit was disbanded in **1958**, before those flights; Johnson had moved into another research division earlier. Distinguish their earlier segregated employment from their later spaceflight work. [Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/inside_adams/2017/02/hidden-figures-no-more-african-american-women-in-space-exploration/?loclr=blogbook), [NASA’s history of Langley’s computers](https://www.nasa.gov/history/langleys-computers-1935-1970/).

9. **False absolute; high confidence.** “His name was published for the first time in his obituary.”

   Korolev published a book under his name in **1934**. What was concealed was his identity as the Soviet space programme’s Chief Designer, not his name’s existence in print. [NASA’s *Challenge to Apollo*](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/SP-4408pt1.pdf).

10. **Misleading scientific explanation; high confidence.** “The rocks that the astronauts brought back showed that the Moon is four and a half billion years old and was once part of the Earth, knocked loose by a collision with another young planet.”

    The samples support formation through a giant impact, but “was once part of the Earth, knocked loose” suggests a pre-existing chunk simply detached. The Moon formed from collision debris; the contributions of Earth and the impactor remain subjects of research. Retain the giant-impact explanation while describing formation from debris. [NASA’s explanation of Moon formation](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/formation/).

11. **Misleading sequence; high confidence.** “The army was allowed to turn to von Braun, who put a small American satellite into orbit within three months.”

    Following the Vanguard failure and the sentence about founding NASA, this implies the wrong order. The Army programme was authorised on **8 November 1957**, before Vanguard’s December failure; Explorer 1 launched in January 1958, before NASA’s establishment that summer. “Within three months” needs its starting point. [NASA launch chronology](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19800019885/downloads/19800019885.pdf), [NASA’s Explorer 1 history](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/explorer-1/about/).

12. **Chronological ambiguity; high confidence.** “In a private meeting the following year, which was recorded, he told the head of the space agency that he was not that interested in space.”

    The immediately preceding “not because they were easy” speech was delivered in **September 1962**. The recorded meeting was in **November 1962**, the same year. “The following year” works only if the reader reaches back past that sentence to May 1961. [NASA’s account of the meeting](https://www.nasa.gov/history/JFK-Webbconv/pages/backgnd.html).

13. **Unsupported precision; high confidence that the exact timing is uncertain.** “Three men were dead in less than half a minute.”

    The investigation could not establish an exact time of death. Rapid incapacitation and the end of voice transmissions do not establish that all three were dead within thirty seconds. State that they died from inhaling toxic gases before rescuers could open the hatch. [NASA’s *Apollo by the Numbers*, Apollo 1 medical findings](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/sp-4029.pdf).

14. **False absolute; high confidence.** “It weighed eighty-three kilograms, and it did nothing except circle the Earth every ninety-six minutes and send out a steady beep that any radio amateur could pick up.”

    Sputnik carried temperature and pressure transmitters, and its signals and orbital behaviour supplied scientific information about the atmosphere. “Did nothing except” erases that function. [NASA’s account of Sputnik’s instruments and objectives](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/sputnik/siddiqi.html).

15. **Overgeneralised physics; high confidence.** “Anything moving at that speed above the air will circle the Earth with its engine off.”

    Approximately 28,000 kilometres an hour is appropriate for a **low Earth orbit**, with motion directed sideways and sufficient clearance from the atmosphere. Speed alone does not guarantee orbit; direction and altitude matter. Keep the cannonball explanation but bound this concluding claim. [ESA’s low-Earth-orbit explanation](https://www.esa.int/media/low-earth-orbit).

16. **Wrong physical detail; high confidence.** “They planted a flag stiffened with wire, since there is no wind, set out instruments, and gathered twenty-one kilograms of rock.”

    The flag was held out by a **telescoping horizontal aluminium crossbar**, not stiffening wire. [Smithsonian’s Apollo flag-pole description](https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/flag-pole-apollo/nasm_A19770570000).

17. **Reader orientation.** “On 27 January 1967 the first crew was sealed into its capsule on top of an unfuelled rocket for a rehearsal.”

    “The first crew” is confusing after Gagarin and several American astronauts have already flown. Explain that this was the crew intended for the **first crewed Apollo mission**. The chapter never introduces Apollo by name, leaving the reader without a clear distinction between the earlier flights and the Moon programme.

18. **Missing technical explanation.** “Its programs were stored by threading copper wires through or around tiny magnetic rings, a wire through a ring for a one and around it for a zero.”

    A general reader has not been told what ones and zeros represent or how they become instructions. One brief explanation of binary storage would connect the weaving to the computer’s work; otherwise the vivid detail remains opaque.

19. **Missing explanation of the danger.** “The cabin was filled with pure oxygen, and it was full of wiring and nylon.”

    The reader must infer why these details matter. Explain that the pressurised oxygen atmosphere made combustible materials burn fiercely, and that wiring provided a possible ignition source. This is the kind of causal bridge required by “give the reader room.”

20. **Break of the absolute rule.** “They forecast the weather, carry television and telephone calls across oceans, tell every telephone where it is, and watch the ice caps and the forests.”

    “Every telephone” is plainly too broad: a landline is enough to break it. Say that navigation satellites help suitably equipped phones determine their position.
