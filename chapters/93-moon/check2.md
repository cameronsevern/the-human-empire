## Factual flags

1. **Minor — wrong altitude, now repeated.**
   
   > “Two thousand metres up, an alarm sounded that neither man had seen in training.”
   
   The first 1202 alarm occurred at approximately **33,000 feet, or 10,000 metres**, not 2,000 metres. Subsequent alarms occurred lower down. The new callback repeats the wrong altitude. [NASA’s Apollo 11 landing account](https://www.nasa.gov/history/50-years-ago-one-small-step-one-giant-leap/)

2. **Minor — incorrect population fraction.**
   
   > “About six hundred million people, a fifth of everyone alive, watched on television as he climbed down the ladder more than six hours later.”
   
   World population in 1969 was approximately **3.6 billion**. Six hundred million is therefore about **one sixth**, not one fifth. [World Bank population series, via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/SPPOPTOTLWLD)

3. **Minor — a new, misleading historical first.**
   
   > “The first step from paper to hardware was a small one.”
   
   Immediately before the 1926 launch, this implies that practical rocket experimentation began then. Goddard had already conducted solid-propellant experiments and demonstrated rocket thrust in a vacuum in **1915**. The 1926 milestone was the first successful **liquid-fuel rocket flight**. [NASA’s Goddard history](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/robert-goddard/)

4. **Minor — the oxygen explanation does not apply to rockets generally.**
   
   > “Unlike a jet or a propeller, it needs no air to push against or to burn with, since it carries its own oxygen.”
   
   Chemical rockets carry their required reactants, but rocket propulsion does not inherently require oxygen or combustion. Ion engines, for example, electrically accelerate xenon. The general principle is carrying and expelling reaction mass; the combustion explanation needs to be restricted to the relevant chemical rockets. [NASA’s ion-propulsion explanation](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dawn/technology/ion-propulsion/)

5. **Minor — the wrong government is credited with the disclosure.**
   
   > “She died of overheating within a few hours, which the Soviet government did not admit for forty-five years.”
   
   Forty-five years after 1957 was **2002**, after the Soviet Union had ceased to exist. The disclosure came from a Russian scientific institute, not the Soviet government. [Smithsonian’s account of the disclosure](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/air-space-magazine/laika-declassified-180967077/)

6. **Major — technical impossibility replaces a development-and-schedule constraint.**
   
   > “To set the entire spacecraft down on the Moon and lift it off again would have needed a rocket far larger than anyone could build.”
   
   NASA studied the larger **Nova** launcher for precisely this approach. The obstacle was developing the launcher and landing technology within Kennedy’s deadline, not an established inability to build such a rocket. “Larger than any available rocket” would preserve the point accurately. [NASA’s account of the mission-mode decision](https://www.nasa.gov/history/60-years-ago-nasa-decides-on-lunar-orbit-rendezvous-for-moon-landing/)

7. **Minor — the cancelled missions’ rockets did not all become exhibits.**
   
   > “Three planned missions were cancelled to save money, and their rockets were laid out on lawns as museum pieces.”
   
   This gives all three the same fate. Apollo 20 was cancelled to release a Saturn V for **Skylab**, which launched in 1973. Surplus hardware did become museum exhibits, but the sentence erases that reassignment. [NASA’s history of Apollo 20’s cancellation](https://www.nasa.gov/history/50-years-ago-nasa-cancels-apollo-20-mission/)

## Clarity flags

1. **New ambiguity at a crucial moment**
   
   > “In Washington a speech lay ready in case it did.”
   
   The nearest positive verb phrase is “fire,” so this can mean the speech was ready in case the engine **fired successfully**. The old sentence clearly specified failure.

2. **Explicit signposting**
   
   > “The reason is simple enough.”  
   > “What happened next shows how the Soviet state used talent.”  
   > “The two statements fit together better than they seem to.”
   
   These announce an explanation or lesson before giving it, contrary to the sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences.

3. **Repeated setup**
   
   > “The idea was older than the means.”  
   > “Long before anyone could build the machinery, a few people worked out what the machinery would have to do.”
   
   Both make the same point, immediately before the schoolteacher supplies the concrete example. The added sentence supplies little orientation.

4. **Explanation repeated almost immediately**
   
   > “Her team had made sure that the computer could drop its less urgent tasks if it was overloaded, and that is what it did in the last minutes of the descent.”  
   > “The alarm that sounded two thousand metres up was the computer doing exactly that: shedding its lesser jobs and carrying on with the landing.”
   
   The callback is useful, but “drop its less urgent tasks” and “shedding its lesser jobs” repeat the explanation.

5. **Redundant date and causal setup**
   
   > “The beating of 1938 played its part.”  
   > “His jaw, broken by his interrogators in 1938, had made it impossible to fit a breathing tube.”
   
   The second sentence already supplies the cause; repeating both the date and the setup adds padding.

6. **Absolutes that exceed their useful meaning**
   
   > “A rocket big enough to matter cost far more than any professor could raise.”
   
   “Big enough to matter” is undefined, and “any professor” makes an unnecessary universal funding claim. Specify the scale of military missile development.

   > “In a contest that began from nothing, a head start in big rockets would count for little.”
   
   “From nothing” obscures the existing rockets, expertise, institutions, and spacecraft programmes described throughout the chapter. The intended point is that neither side possessed the complete capability for a lunar landing.

   > “Everything that landed would also have to take off again, carrying its own fuel for the journey home.”
   
   “Everything” unnecessarily includes spent hardware and equipment. The useful distinction is that direct ascent required landing the **Earth-return vehicle and its return propellant**. The chapter’s own diagram later explicitly shows only the landing craft’s upper half departing.

7. **A new setup blurs two different durations**
   
   > “After the long journey and the anxious landing, the time on the surface was short and busy.”  
   > “Armstrong and his companion spent two hours and thirty-one minutes outside.”
   
   “Time on the surface” includes their stay inside the lander; the next sentence measures time outside. The setup should identify the moonwalk.

8. **Unexplained historical reference**
   
   > “Five days after Gagarin's flight came the disaster at the Bay of Pigs, a second humiliation in less than a week.”
   
   A reader without historical background is not told that this was the failed American-backed invasion of Cuba. Calling it a second humiliation explains its significance without explaining the event.

9. **An imaginary person becomes a literal subject**
   
   > “The schoolboy was wrong: a rocket pushes against its own exhaust, not against the air, which is exactly why it can work in a vacuum.”
   
   No schoolboy has spoken. The mistaken assertion belongs to the newspaper; the wording momentarily misdirects the reader.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions, or explicit body cross-references. I have not repeated the earlier check’s resolved flags.

## Overall

The revision preserves the chapter’s numerical and chronological content, but it is not entirely fact-neutral: several bridges introduce new claims. The main revision problems are the broadened “first” claim, overgeneralised technical explanations, repeated setup, and the ambiguous contingency-speech sentence.
