I found no clear contradiction with chapters 9–11, and no hedging stacks, explicit chapter self-mentions, or body cross-references. The issues below are ranked by seriousness. No files were edited.

1. **Unsupported claim about legal redress.** “A man in Kalinga whose brother had been jailed for nothing had no court in which he could accuse the judge.”

   The surviving evidence does not establish that categorical absence. The Separate Edicts demand action against unjust imprisonment and provide supervision of judicial officers; they do not describe every available complaint procedure. The defensible statement is that we do not know what effective redress this family had. **Confidence: high that the claim exceeds the evidence.** [Separate Rock Edict I](https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/dhammika/wheel386.html#kalinga)

2. **A disputed conquest becomes an established event.** “His son pushed the frontier far down into the south of India.”

   This attributes the southern expansion securely to Bindusara. Its attribution to him rather than Chandragupta is disputed; early evidence does not establish Bindusara’s responsibility. State the empire’s southern extent under Ashoka without assigning an otherwise undocumented campaign. **Confidence: high that qualification or replacement is needed; uncertain who made the conquests.** [*The Minor Rock-Edicts of Asoka and Some Connected Problems*, p. 17](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/18130.pdf)

3. **An absolute obscures explicitly Buddhist inscriptions.** “The rock edicts say nothing about the life of a monk or about escape from suffering.”

   This needs to distinguish the **Major Rock Edicts** from the other inscriptions. The Bairat/Bhabru Minor Rock Edict addresses the Buddhist community and recommends teachings for monks and nuns. “The rock edicts” includes that text. **Confidence: high.** [Minor Rock Edict 3](https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/dhammika/wheel386.html#minor)

4. **Official duties become an unsupported power of entry.** “The officers of dhamma, who could walk into any household, made a useful set of eyes.”

   Rock Edict V describes their work among social groups and in royal relatives’ households. It does not establish a right to enter **any** household. The surveillance interpretation may be argued, but this particular power should not be presented as documented. **Confidence: high.** [Rock Edict V](https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/dhammika/wheel386.html#rock)

5. **The construction explanation omits actual foundations.** “A pillar has no separate base.” “The bottom two or three metres were left rough and sunk into a pit, so that the shaft seems to grow straight out of the ground.”

   Excavated examples rested on substantial stone foundation slabs. Some buried portions were also polished. The description can say there was no *visible pedestal*, but the present wording teaches the wrong construction method and makes the rough surface universal. **Confidence: high.** [Harry Falk, *Aśokan Sites and Artefacts*, pp. 146–147, 198](https://dokumen.pub/aokan-sites-and-artefacts-1nbsped-3805337124.html)

6. **The quarry attribution is disputed.** “Most of the pale pillars were cut from the sandstone hills at Chunar, which stand beside the Ganges about two hundred kilometres upstream from Pataliputra.”

   Chunar is the traditional attribution, not a secure provenance for most shafts. Falk distinguishes Chunar-type stone from Pabhosā-type sandstone and assigns most shafts to the latter. Presenting a single established source also makes the following transport reconstruction look more certain than it is. **Confidence: high that the attribution is disputed; moderate on individual quarry assignments.** [Falk, pp. 142–147 and 154–157](https://dokumen.pub/aokan-sites-and-artefacts-1nbsped-3805337124.html)

7. **Alexander’s stopping point is misplaced.** “There his soldiers refused to go any further, and he turned back.”

   “There” refers to the Indus valley “in what is now Pakistan.” The refusal occurred at the **Hyphasis, now the Beas, in present-day India**, after the army had crossed several Punjab rivers. **Confidence: high.** [Arrian, *Anabasis*, Book 5, summarized in the Oxford edition’s introduction](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780191633140_A23590291/preview-9780191633140_A23590291.pdf)

8. **The pillar’s relocation date needs correction or reconciliation.** “The pillar had been brought to Delhi in the 1350s by a sultan who admired it, and he had called in learned men to tell him what it said.”

   The contemporary account translated in the Archaeological Survey’s memoir dates its erection to **30 September 1367**. Some modern summaries give 1356, so there is a source conflict, but the contemporary dated account deserves priority. **Confidence: medium-high.** [*A Memoir on Kotla Firoz Shah, Delhi*, p. 41](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/22024.pdf)

9. **The script’s mechanism is explained incorrectly.** “Each sign stands for a consonant, and a small stroke added to it shows which vowel follows.”

   In Brahmi, a basic consonant sign includes an inherent **a** vowel; added marks change that vowel. There are also independent vowel signs. A simple “ka → ki” example would explain the mechanism accurately. **Confidence: high.** [Unicode specification, Brahmi](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-14/)

10. **Two paraphrases strengthen the original into absolutes.** “The only conquest worth making, he says, is ‘conquest by Dhamma’.” “The ceremonies that women perform at births and weddings, he remarks, are trivial and worthless, and the only ceremony that bears fruit is good behaviour.”

    Rock Edict XIII calls dhamma conquest the **best** conquest. Rock Edict IX allows that other ceremonies can produce limited, worldly results. Both “only” formulations erase distinctions the text actually makes. **Confidence: high.** [Rock Edicts IX and XIII](https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/dhammika/wheel386.html#rock)

11. **A diplomatic conclusion rests on an unintroduced mission.** “Nothing in Greek writing mentions his envoys, and the Greek kings went on fighting each other exactly as before.”

    The preceding paragraph introduces foreign kings and Ashoka’s claims of influence, but never introduces the envoys. A general reader cannot tell whether an embassy has been independently established or is inferred from Ashoka’s account. Explain that distinction before referring to “his envoys”; also clarify what their supposed mission was.

12. **The decipherment explanation leaves a small but important gap.** “If that guess was right, he had the letters d and n, and the rest of the alphabet followed within weeks.”

    A reader looking at *danam* will reasonably wonder why the discovery yields two letters rather than five, and what happened to **m**. Briefly explain the syllabic signs and nasal mark here. The later, currently inaccurate account of the script arrives too late to make this discovery intelligible.

13. **Minor source criticism becomes a detour.** “How far can he be trusted?” “The other famous source has to be handled with even more care.”

    These introduce two paragraphs about assessing Megasthenes and dating the *Arthashastra*. The outline explicitly asks for this discussion, but it conflicts with `style.md`: settle minor source problems before writing, and do not argue sources in the body. The archaeological wall can carry the useful point; the handbook discussion introduces a source chiefly to explain why it cannot supply the story.

14. **Explicit signposts announce the argument.** “The first thing to know is that the emperor's sorrow was not published there.” “That, then, is what the family in Kalinga got, and it is worth setting against what they did not get.”

    These breach the prohibition on announcing a point or forthcoming comparison. State the omission in Kalinga directly, then move directly to what remained unchanged.

15. **Numbers accumulate beyond the style guide’s allowance.** “It was surrounded by a moat and by a wall of timber, which had slits for archers to shoot through, sixty-four gates and five hundred and seventy towers.” “The city, he says, was governed by thirty officials who sat in six committees of five.”

    The first follows the city’s fifteen-kilometre length; the second stacks three figures for one administrative point. Both breach “one number per point.” Keep the city’s length and the timber wall; describe the committees through one useful task rather than their arithmetic.

16. **An exception breaks the administrative inference.** “A ruler who is being obeyed does not need to tell his officers that they do not understand him.”

    Obedient officials can misunderstand instructions, and rulers can repeat or dramatize demands. Ashoka’s words show concern about implementation; they do not prove disobedience. This is an overextended inference and an exception-vulnerable absolute, rather than a problem with the narrator taking a view.
