# Chapter 20 — Has a conqueror ever been sorry?

**Line under the title:** India, from about 2,350 years ago to about 2,200 years ago.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,500 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

One did, and said so in public. The emperor Ashoka, grandson of the man who built the first empire to cover most of India, conquered the coastal country of Kalinga and then had his remorse cut into rock, with his own count of the dead. He spent the rest of his reign telling his subjects and his officers how to behave, on boulders and polished pillars from Afghanistan to the south of India. He did not give Kalinga back, disband his army or end the death penalty, and he warned the forest peoples that he could still punish. What a conquered family gained was a standard, published over the head of the local magistrate, and an emperor who fought no further war of conquest. How much of it reached them cannot be known, because almost all the evidence is the emperor's own voice. The empire broke up within fifty years of his death and the script was forgotten. The words were read again in 1837, and modern India took his lions for its emblem.

## The story

1. **The pillar nobody could read.** Answer first. A polished pillar in Delhi, covered in letters that had been unreadable for centuries. James Prinsep at the Calcutta mint works out the alphabet in 1837. The king turns out to be Ashoka.
2. **Five hundred elephants.** Alexander turns back; Chandragupta takes the Ganges kingdom and the north-west; the peace with Seleucus. The Greek ambassador's city with a wooden wall, and what was dug up in Patna. Why the ambassador and the handbook of statecraft must both be used with care.
3. **A hundred thousand killed.** Ashoka; Kalinga; the thirteenth rock edict in his own words. Set beside Hammurabi's list of captured cities.
4. **What the king meant by dhamma.** The Buddha's teaching in three sentences. Ashoka as a lay follower. The dhamma of the edicts as a short code of behaviour that every sect could accept. Two peacocks and a deer.
5. **Wells, shade trees and five Greek kings.** Roads, wells, medicine, the officers of dhamma, reports at any hour. Envoys to the Greek kings. The Greek inscription at Kandahar.
6. **Fifty tonnes of polished stone.** The edict pillar: how it was cut, moved, set up and inscribed. The sultan's cart with forty-two wheels. Reading aloud. The script.
7. **All men are my children.** Kalinga's own edicts, which leave the confession out. What did not change, what did, and how far an order travelled. Whose voice the evidence is.
8. **Four lions.** The empire falls apart; the script is forgotten; the teaching reaches Sri Lanka and beyond; the lion capital of Sarnath becomes the emblem of India.
