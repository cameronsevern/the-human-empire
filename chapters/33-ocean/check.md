1. **Factual error; internal contradiction; absolute.** “No empire controlled it, and no navy patrolled it.”

   No empire controlled the entire ocean, but regional naval enforcement certainly existed—including the Srivijayan navy described later in this chapter. Zheng He’s suppression of pirates at Palembang in 1407 also falls within the stated period. Distinguish absence of ocean-wide sovereignty from absence of naval policing. **Confidence: high.** [Translated historical accounts of Zheng He](https://edspace.american.edu/justinjacobs/wp-content/uploads/sites/986/2022/12/31-Historical-Accounts-of-Zheng-He.pdf).

2. **Faulty explanation of the central mechanism.** “A ship bound from India to China rode the south-west wind across the Bay of Bengal and then had to wait for the next season before it could go on, or go back.”

   The onward and return journeys require different explanations. The seasonal windows for India–Straits travel and Southeast Asia–China travel overlapped: approximately April–August and June–August respectively. Waiting was common, but crossing the bay did not automatically require waiting for a new season before continuing to China. This also undermines the later claim that ships arriving from either direction necessarily stopped at Malacca until the season turned. **Confidence: high on the overstatement.** [*Archaeological Recovery of the Java Sea Wreck*, p. 7](https://www.iseas.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/mathers_flecker_1997_java_sea_wreck.pdf).

3. **Factual error; absolute.** “It was the one stretch of the whole route where every vessel was bound to pass slowly and close to land.”

   Malacca was not compulsory. The Sunda Strait offered another established passage between the Indian Ocean and the Java Sea, and Srivijaya’s strategic position involved both routes. The preceding sentence acknowledges a detour, making “every vessel” internally inconsistent too. **Confidence: high.** [Hermann Kulke, “Śrīvijaya Revisited”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/befeo_0336-1519_2016_num_102_1_6231).

4. **Doubtful causal conclusion.** “His aim, it seems, had been to break the monopoly, and in that he succeeded.” “Srivijaya never recovered its grip.”

   Breaking a trade monopoly is an interpretation of the campaign, not an established royal objective. The aftermath was also more complicated than permanent collapse in 1025: Srivijaya recovered and reconstituted its kingdom, while further Chola intervention followed in 1067–68. Presenting one raid as the decisive end of its commercial power is too neat. **Confidence: high that the account needs qualification; medium on the precise extent of recovery.** [Leonard Andaya, “The Trans-Sumatra Trade and the Ethnicization of the ‘Batak’”](https://brill.com/downloadpdf/journals/bki/158/3/article-p367_1.pdf); [Kulke](https://www.persee.fr/doc/befeo_0336-1519_2016_num_102_1_6231).

5. **Factual error; absolute.** “Apart from the Chola raid, no Indian state ever sent a military force across the Bay of Bengal.”

   Even within this chapter’s period, there was another Chola expedition to Kedah in 1067–68. The useful argument is that Indian cultural influence did not generally depend on conquest; a claim about every Indian military expedition is unnecessary and false as phrased. **Confidence: high.** [Kulke](https://www.persee.fr/doc/befeo_0336-1519_2016_num_102_1_6231).

6. **Unsupported reconstruction presented as known fact.** “An Arab or Persian crew had sailed this vessel some eight thousand kilometres from the Persian Gulf to China, loaded it with enough crockery to stock every kitchen in a fair-sized city, and got a third of the way home before it sank.”

   The ship’s construction and cargo establish connections, not this complete itinerary. Its crew was probably multinational; its departure port, destination and route remain uncertain. “A third of the way home” therefore supplies precision the evidence cannot support. Keep the physical ship and cargo as the secure story. **Confidence: high.** [Smithsonian, “The Crew and Their Possessions”](https://asia-archive.si.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/01Intro.pdf); [Wilson and Flecker, “Dating the Belitung Shipwreck”](https://asia.si.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/shipwrecked-03-wilsonflecker.pdf).

7. **Factual error; absolute.** “It left no ruins because it was not that kind of state.”

   Srivijaya did leave structural remains, including brick religious buildings at Palembang. Timber construction helps explain the scarcity of surviving secular architecture; it does not justify “no ruins” or the inference that this state did not build monuments. The section heading repeats the problem. **Confidence: high.** [Pierre-Yves Manguin, “Southeast Sumatra in Protohistoric and Srivijaya Times”](https://www.academia.edu/9845940/Southeast_Sumatra_in_Protohistoric_and_Srivijaya_Times_Upstream_Downstream_Relations_and_the_Settlement_of_the_Peneplain).

8. **Factual error.** “A pilgrim climbs it by walking round each terrace in turn, past nearly three thousand carved panels that tell the Buddha's lives.”

   Borobudur has approximately 2,672 panels, but only 1,460 are narrative; 1,212 are decorative. Nor do all the narratives depict the Buddha’s lives: they include other Buddhist teachings and Sudhana’s search for enlightenment. **Confidence: high.** [UNESCO documentation](https://whc.unesco.org/document/186919); [Smarthistory, “Borobudur”](https://smarthistory.org/borobudur/).

9. **Misleading history of discovery.** “For a century, visitors saw the stone temples standing in forest and imagined a few holy precincts.”

   In conjunction with the following lidar sentence, this implies that the extensive city was unknown until laser surveys. Archaeologists had already mapped substantial settlement and water infrastructure; the comprehensive 2007 map preceded the 2012 lidar survey. Lidar revealed crucial detail beneath forest, rather than discovering the urban landscape from scratch. **Confidence: high.** [Evans et al., 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1964867/); [Evans et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732978/).

10. **Doubtful superlative.** “By the time that ship went down, the Indian Ocean was already the busiest sea in the world, and for the next seven hundred years it stayed so.”

    “Busiest” needs a measure—ships, tonnage, value or people—and comparative evidence across seven centuries. The wreck establishes substantial long-distance commerce, not a continuous global ranking. A major trading network is the defensible fact. **Confidence: high that the evidence cited by the story does not establish the ranking; no confidence in a replacement ranking.** [Smithsonian’s account of what the wreck demonstrates](https://asia.si.edu/research/publications/shipwrecked-tang-treasures-and-monsoon-winds/).

11. **Doubtful priority claim.** “He is the first Muslim ruler known in the islands.”

    Malik al-Salih’s 1297 gravestone is secure early evidence, but an earlier ruler’s inscription at Lamreh, dated 1211, has been reported and debated. “One of the earliest securely attested Muslim rulers” avoids making a disputed priority carry the ending. **Confidence: medium; the earlier evidence itself is disputed.** [Geoff Wade, “Southeast Asian Islam and Southern China in the Fourteenth Century”](https://repository.uinsaizu.ac.id/31159/1/Geoff%20Wade%20%28editor%29_%20Li%20Tana%20%28editor%29_%20-%20Anthony%20Reid%20and%20the%20study%20of%20the%20Southeast%20Asian%20past-Institute%20of%20Southeast%20Asian%20Studies%20%28ISEAS%29%20%282012%29.pdf).

12. **Reader loses the geographical connection.** “The largest state to grow out of this borrowing lay inland, in Cambodia.”

    The following section becomes a substantial account of Angkor’s rice, reservoirs and decline without explaining how this inland kingdom connects to the ocean trade that organizes the chapter. The outline promises that connection, but the body does not deliver it. Explain the connection before moving inland, or reduce the detour.

13. **Reader cannot identify the central object.** “The grandest result stands in the middle of Java.”

    The monument described over the next paragraph is never named. “Borobudur” is useful identification, not an expendable name: it lets the reader recognize the object, connect it to an illustration and distinguish it from Angkor Wat immediately afterwards.

14. **Reader loses track of which king and temple.** “In 1025 the Chola king sent a fleet across the bay.”

    The preceding temple-builder was Rajaraja I; the expedition’s ruler was his son Rajendra I. Without a succession cue, “the Chola king” sounds like the same man. Naming both is optional, but identifying the son is necessary. Likewise, “the great temple” needs a location or name when its inscription becomes evidence. **Confidence: high on the succession.** [Kulke](https://www.persee.fr/doc/befeo_0336-1519_2016_num_102_1_6231).

15. **Signpost sentence; sweeping absolute.** “Two consequences followed, and they shaped everything else.”

    This announces the explanation instead of giving it, and “everything else” exceeds the actual argument. The following sentences already explain sailing calendars and resident merchant communities. This is a direct breach of the no-signposts rule, rather than an objection to a confident voice.

16. **Breaks the numerical-selection rule.** “At its centre is an oval enclosure whose outer wall is two hundred and fifty metres round and eleven metres high, built of nearly a million granite blocks laid without any mortar, in courses so even that the wall seems to have been drawn with a ruler.”

    Three measurements serve one point: the wall’s scale and workmanship. `style.md` explicitly says not to stack three statistics where one will do. Keep the most vivid measurement and let the mortarless construction and ruler image carry the rest.
