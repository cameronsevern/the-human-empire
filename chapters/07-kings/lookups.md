# Chapter 7 fact check (web lookups)

Method note: every item below was searched on the web. Where a primary or museum page could not be fetched, that is stated. Search-engine summaries were cross-checked against fetched pages where possible.

1. Royal Cemetery of Ur. Claim: "in the 1920s", British archaeologist, "two thousand graves", "sixteen", "about four and a half thousand years old". Verdict: Confirmed. Penn Almanac: joint Penn/British Museum expedition began 1922, major royal cemetery excavation from 1926, closed 1934; "more than 2,000 burials" (also "nearly 2,000" in the same piece); sixteen royal tombs; about 4,500 years old. Wikipedia: slightly more than 2,000 burials (Woolley found 1,850, a further 260 identified later); sixteen called royal; Early Dynastic IIIA, 2600-2500 BC. Note "royal" is Woolley's label based on size, wealth and mass sacrifice. Sources: https://almanac.upenn.edu/archive/volumes/v56/n09/ur.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Cemetery_at_Ur

2. Great Death Pit (PG 1237). Claim: 74 bodies; 6 men near the entrance with weapons; 68 women; headdresses of gold leaf, necklaces of blue stone, "hands on the strings of their harps"; cups beside them; poison theory. Verdict: Confirmed except the harps detail, which could not be verified.
 - Penn Museum press release: 74 sacrificial victims; six soldiers at the entrance (one with a crushed copper helmet). Penn Almanac: 74 bodies (a Penn blog and one caption say 73). Women wore headdresses and jewellery of gold, silver, lapis lazuli, carnelian; lyres and a harp found in the pit with the women; attendants found with small cups; Woolley concluded they drank poison willingly. Woolley himself wrote the bones were too crushed to show cause of death.
 - "Some of them still had their hands on the strings": the search results say lyres/harp were found with women and one woman lay with her arm across a lyre; no source fetched confirms hands on strings in this pit. Could not verify.
 - Replacement (confident): "They wore headdresses of gold leaf and necklaces of blue stone, and beside some of them lay harps."
 - Sources: https://penn.museum/about/press-room/press-releases/two-4600-year-old-skulls-from-famous-excavations-at-royal-tombs-of-ur-iraq-traveled-to-the-hospital-of-the-university-of-pennsylvania-for-cat-scans ; https://almanac.upenn.edu/archive/volumes/v56/n09/ur.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Cemetery_at_Ur ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/abs/pg-1237-royal-cemetery-of-ur-patterns-in-death/FFC236B22E5AA31A188B8ABB44E0E27D

3. CT scans. Claim: "two of the skulls from these tombs ... holes punched in them by a pointed weapon". Verdict: Confirmed, with one wording change. Paper: Baadsgaard, Monge, Cox and Zettler, "Human sacrifice and intentional corpse preservation in the Royal Cemetery of Ur", Antiquity 85(327):27-42 (March 2011). Two skulls: one from the Great Death Pit (PG 1237), a young woman; one from a smaller death pit attached to the "King's Grave" (PG 789), a male soldier with a crushed copper helmet. Penn and the abstract say "felled with a sharp instrument"; The History Blog reports two round holes about an inch wide in the man's skull and one in the woman's, with cracks radiating from them that form only in living bone. The sources say "sharp instrument", not "pointed weapon". Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E44F1731C922EF9FF43E0B5D2DF67AC1 ; https://www.thehistoryblog.com/archives/3752 ; the Penn press release above.
 Replacement: "both turned out to have round holes made by a sharp instrument, and the cracks around them show that the wounds were made while the person was alive." (The scans were done in 2009 and published in 2011; "in recent years" is fair.)

4. Cities. Claim: governed from temples with a council of elders; "more than a dozen walled cities" about 5,000 years ago; "a day or two" apart. Verdict: Overstated / Could not verify the numbers.
 - Wikipedia (Sumer): each city-state centred on a temple of its patron god; Uruk-period cities were assisted by a council of elders. Confirmed in outline.
 - No source gave "more than a dozen" at one time or "a day or two's walk". A secondary page citing Whitehouse says there were probably never more than twenty city-states and names thirteen of 10,000-20,000 people. Distances vary greatly: Lagash and Umma are close neighbours, but Uruk and Kish (the pair in item 6) are far apart in the plain (Kish is in the north), so "a day or two's walk apart" does not hold for all pairs.
 - Replacement: "By about five thousand years ago there were a dozen or more walled cities on the plain, and neighbouring cities, such as Lagash and Umma, lay close enough that their fields met." And in the later paragraph: "With a dozen rival cities packed onto one plain, one war was hardly finished before the next was brewing."
 - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer ; https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/User:SomeGuyWhoRandomlyEdits/Early_Dynastic_IIIb (secondary)

5. lugal and egal. Claim: lugal = lu "man" + gal "big", "big man", later "king"; "big house" = palace. Verdict: Confirmed. Wiktionary and grammar sources: lugal = lu2 "man/person" + gal "big, great", the sense shifting from "great man" to "king"; e2-gal = e2 "house" + gal = "palace" (Wikipedia: É.GAL, literally "big house"). The idea that the word began as a war-leader title is the chapter's interpretation (Jacobsen's theory), not a documented fact; the chapter presents it plainly. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer ; https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/E-ana ; https://kaikki.org/dictionary/Sumerian/meaning/𒂍/𒂍𒃲/𒂍𒃲.html

6. Gilgamesh and Aga. Claim: Kish demands Uruk submit; Gilgamesh asks the elders, who advise giving in; then the young men, who choose war; written down long after. Verdict: Confirmed. ETCSL 1.8.1.1: Aga's envoys come from Kish; elders: "we should submit to the house of Kic. We should not smite it with weapons!" (lines 9-14); the able-bodied men: "You old men should not submit to the house of Kic! Should we young men not smite it with weapons?" (24-29). ETCSL does not state Aga's demand explicitly in the lines checked (other renderings: dig wells and finish the work for Kish). Oldest copies are Old Babylonian (about 1800 BC), many centuries after Aga (Early Dynastic I). Source: https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1811.htm

7. Stele of the Vultures. Claim: Eannatum, about 4,450 years old; victory over the city next door; strip of farmland; block of helmeted soldiers behind shields with spears, marching over bodies, king in front; other side the god with a net full of naked enemies, clubbing one; "something that had never been pictured before". Verdict: Confirmed except "never been pictured before" (Overstated) and "marching in step" (not in any source).
 - Wikipedia: c. 2460-2475 BC, Early Dynastic III; dispute over Gu-Edin, a strip of farmland between Lagash and Umma; Eannatum leads a phalanx with enemies trampled; Ningirsu holds a net full of naked captives and a mace. It is called "the earliest known war monument"; no source says it is the earliest depiction of a phalanx. Louvre record not retrieved (my fetch returned an unrelated object), so helmets/shields are from standard descriptions, not verified on a museum page. "Marching in step" is not attested.
 - Replacement: "Only broken pieces of the stone survive, but one of them shows an army as a block. The soldiers are packed shoulder to shoulder and several rows deep. Each man wears a helmet. In front of them is a wall of large shields, and between the shields a row of spears points forward. The block advances over the bodies of the enemy. The king walks in front."
 - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stele_of_the_Vultures ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eanatum (search result)

8. Sumerian King List. Claim: "descended from heaven". Verdict: Confirmed. ETCSL 2.1.1 opens: "After the kingship descended from heaven, the kingship was in Eridug." Earliest version (the Ur III list) dates to about 2100 BC (Steinkeller; Jacobsen argued Utu-hegal's reign); the standard Weld-Blundell Prism is about 1800 BC; chapter's "a later list" is right. Sources: https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section2/tr211.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumerian_King_List

9. Ur-Nanshe plaque. Claim: early Lagash king on a plaque with a basket of bricks on his head, carrying the first load for a new temple. Verdict: Confirmed. Louvre AO 2344, perforated limestone plaque, Early Dynastic III (about 2550-2500 BC), from Tello (Girsu): upper register shows Ur-Nanshe with a basket of bricks on his head, commemorating the building of a temple of Ningirsu; a banquet below. (My direct fetch of the Louvre record returned the wrong object, so this rests on Smarthistory and secondary pages.) "Not shown ... sitting on a throne": in the lower register he sits at the banquet holding a cup, so the sentence is true of the upper scene only. Safe wording: "He is shown carrying a basket of bricks balanced on his head, like a common labourer, bringing the first load for a new temple." Sources: https://smarthistory.org/perforated-relief-of-ur-nanshe ; https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-ncc-zeliart/?p=1550

10. Urukagina. Claim: boasted he would not hand over the widow and the orphan to the powerful. Verdict: Confirmed. CDLI: his reform text concludes with the pledge not to give the orphan and widow over to the powerful (Ukg. 4). Translations vary ("waif" for orphan). He ruled about 2350 BC, later than Eannatum. Sources: https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/urukagina ; https://www.milestonedocuments.com/documents/view/reform-edict-of-urukagina/text

11. Nile geography. Claim: about a thousand kilometres to the sea; strip a few kilometres wide; summer flood; flows north; prevailing north wind; hieroglyphs: boat without sail = north, boat with sail = south. Verdict: Confirmed, approximate. Wikipedia (Nile): about 800 km from Aswan to Cairo plus the delta to the sea, so roughly 1,000 km; flows north; Blue Nile floods July-October. Hieroglyph: Gardiner P1 (boat) determinative for sailing downstream (north), P2 (boat under sail) for sailing upstream (south); P5 sail sign for north wind. Width of the strip and "prevailing wind from the north" are standard but no fetched page stated them; low risk. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sail_(hieroglyph)

12. Unification and the Narmer Palette. Claim: about 5,100 years ago a southern king conquered the north; slab of dark stone about two-thirds of a metre tall, found in a temple in the south; smiting a kneeling enemy by the hair with a club; other side, different crown (the northern crown), two rows of bound, beheaded bodies with heads between their legs; pose copied for 3,000 years. Verdict: Confirmed, with a caution on "conquered".
 - Wikipedia: 63-64 cm tall, 42 cm wide, siltstone (greywacke in older descriptions) from Wadi Hammamat; found 1897-98 by Quibell and Green in the Main Deposit of the Temple of Horus at Nekhen (Hierakonpolis), in southern Egypt; about 3200-3000 BC; front (smiting side) has the White Crown, the king raising a mace over a kneeling captive he holds by the hair; back has the Red Crown and decapitated corpses with heads between their legs (ten near the bottom; the procession scene shows two rows of bodies by the standard bearers). Smiting images almost identical to this appear on temple pylons as late as the Roman period, so "thirty centuries" is safe.
 - Caution: scholars treat unification as a gradual process whose last stage fell near Narmer; the Palette may commemorate an achieved unity rather than a single conquest. The chapter's "a king from the south conquered the north" is the traditional reading. Safe replacement: "About five thousand one hundred years ago, the south of Egypt, under a king we call Narmer, took control of the north, and the whole valley became one kingdom."
 - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narmer_Palette ; https://www.worldhistory.org/Early_Dynastic_Period_In_Egypt/ ; https://egymonuments.gov.eg/historical-periods/early-dynastic-period/

13. The king as Horus. Claim: "living form of the sky god", "born of a human mother", joined the gods among the stars after death; maat. Verdict: Confirmed with a wording point. Pyramid Texts: the king in life was the god Horus on earth, heir of Osiris and son of Re; after death he ascends to the sky as a star among the imperishable stars or joins Re. Horus is the falcon god of kingship and the sky, so "sky god" is acceptable; "god of kingship and the sky" is more exact. "Born of a human mother": Old Kingdom texts (e.g. the Westcar Papyrus) make Re the father of the first kings of the Fifth Dynasty and the mother a human woman; no source fetched states this for Khufu's period. Safer: "He was held to be the living Horus, the falcon god of the sky and of kingship, and when he died he went up to join the other gods among the stars." Sources: https://www.worldhistory.org/article/148/the-pyramid-texts-guide-to-the-afterlife/ ; https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/9781136198670/chapters/10.4324/9781315005454-17

14. Great Pyramid. Claims: about 4,550 years ago; 2.3 million blocks, "most of them weighing more than a family car"; 146 m; nothing taller for 3,800 years; "finished within the king's own reign, which lasted a little over twenty years"; "a block every few minutes". Verdict: Confirmed except the reign length (Wrong).
 - Wikipedia: built about 2600-2560 BC (so about 4,550-4,600 years ago); estimated 2.3 million blocks; original height 146.6 m (280 cubits); average block about 2.5 tonnes (6 million tonnes total), so "most weighing more than a family car" is safe; tallest structure until Lincoln Cathedral's spire (c. 1311), about 3,800 years (Lincoln's own page: tallest from 1311 to 1548); about 250 blocks a day needed, roughly one every six minutes around the clock or every three in daylight; construction about 26 years per Wikipedia (Herodotus: 20).
 - Khufu's reign: Turin Canon 23 years; but the Merer diary and Dakhla Oasis inscription are dated to year 27 and graffiti in the pyramid to year 28 or 29, so the reign was at least 27 years; Wikipedia gives about 23 to 29. "A little over twenty years" is Wrong as a statement about the reign.
 - Replacement: "It was finished within the king's own reign, which lasted at least twenty-seven years. To set two million three hundred thousand blocks in about twenty-five years, working through every hour of daylight, the builders had to cut, haul and place a block every few minutes, and go on doing so without a break for a quarter of a century." And later: "For twenty-five years, a large share..." and "After twenty-five years of that".
 - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khufu ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Cathedral

15. Herodotus. Claim: "everybody assumed ... slaves under the whip. A Greek traveller ... was told so". Verdict: Wrong as to what Herodotus said. Histories 2.124: Cheops closed the temples, "compelled all the Egyptians to work for him", they worked "in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months"; ten years for the road, twenty for the pyramid. No mention of slaves or whips. The slave-and-whip picture comes from later films and popular tradition. Visit date: about 450 BC, roughly 2,100 years after Khufu, so "two thousand years afterwards" is fine.
 Replacement: "For a long time, everybody assumed that this was done by slaves under the whip. The Greek traveller Herodotus, who visited Egypt two thousand years afterwards, was told that Khufu had forced all the Egyptians to work for him, in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months, and the idea of the whip has been in films ever since."
 Sources: https://cts.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0016/tlg001/perseus-eng2/2.124.1-2.124.5 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza

16. Workers' town. Claim: "in the 1990s archaeologists found where the builders had actually lived"; "Just south of the pyramid"; streets, long dormitories, bakeries with bell-shaped pots, breweries, bones of cattle, sheep, goats showing regular meat, "which most Egyptian farmers did not". Verdict: Overstated on the date, the location and the reign; the contents are Confirmed.
 - AERA: excavation of the previously unknown site began in December 1988, about 400 m south of the Sphinx (Heit el-Ghurab, "Lost City of the Pyramids"); streets and alleys lined with bakeries, commissaries, kitchens, warehouses, workshops; "galleries" that Lehner interprets as barracks; bread-mould ash layers from bakeries; Egyptian government and press reports of large quantities of cattle, sheep, goat and pig bones, with Lehner saying the meat supply was rich. The 1990 discovery was the workers' cemetery (Hawass). So "in the 1990s" is Wrong for the town (1988).
 - Dating: AERA says the settlement was occupied in the 4th Dynasty with most of the visible architecture belonging to the time of Khafre and Menkaure, i.e. just after Khufu. Describing it as the town of Khufu's builders is therefore stretched; it housed the Giza workforce of the next pyramids and is the best evidence for how the Giza labour was organised.
 - Location: south-southeast of the Sphinx, not "just south of the pyramid" (Khufu's); the Sphinx lies below Khafre's pyramid.
 - "Which most Egyptian farmers did not": no source found; drop or soften.
 - Replacement: "In 1988 archaeologists began digging a town south of the Sphinx where the builders of the Giza pyramids had lived, and it changed the picture. It had streets, long galleries that look like dormitories, and bakeries with rows of bell-shaped pots in which bread was baked by the thousand loaves. There were breweries beside them. The rubbish heaps were full of the bones of cattle, sheep and goats, enough to show that the workers ate meat regularly."
 - Sources: https://www.aeraweb.org/lost-city/ ; https://egymonuments.gov.eg/en/monuments/workers-town-and-cemetery/ ; https://www.livescience.com/28937-giza-pyramid-archaeology.html ; https://archaeology.org/?p=2639

17. Workers' tombs. Claim: small mud-brick tombs beside the pyramids; skeletons with worn spines and knees; healed, well-set fractures. Verdict: Confirmed on the findings; location overstated. Hawass's team found the cemetery in 1990 (about 600 skeletons reported); excavation supervisor Okasha reported arthritis in vertebrae and knees and healed fractures showing good medical treatment (secondary press sources; PBS NOVA reports injuries, including hands supported by wooden splints and a leg operation with 14 more years of life). The cemetery lies on the slope south of the Wall of the Crow, not "in the shadow" of Khufu's pyramid. Replacement: "Beside the town were the builders' own graves, small tombs of mud brick built on the slope above it, and that is an honour that would not have been given to slaves." Sources: https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/pyramid/explore/builders.html ; https://discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/egypt-finds-tombs-of-pyramid-builders-and-more-evidence-they-were-free-men ; https://www.cbc.ca/technology/story/2010/01/11/tech-egypt-pyramid.html

18. Gang names. Claim: one gang "Friends of Khufu"; "a gang that worked for his grandson" "Drunkards of Menkaure"; red paint. Verdict: Confirmed. PBS NOVA (Hawass/Lehner): gangs named after kings, such as "Friends of Khufu", and a Menkaure gang whose name translates roughly as "drunkards"; graffiti in the relieving chambers of Khufu's pyramid names the Friends of Khufu. Menkaure was the son of Khafre, who was a son of Khufu, so Menkaure was Khufu's grandson (Wikipedia, Khufu page implies this through Khafre). Red-paint detail is confirmed by popular and Hawass sources but not by a fetched scholarly page. Sources: https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/pyramid/explore/builders.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khufu

19. Merer papyri. Claim: found 2013 at an ancient harbour on the Red Sea; oldest written papyrus; foreman of about forty boatmen; limestone from the east-bank quarries to the pyramid "Horizon of the king"; two or three round trips per ten days; last years of the reign. Verdict: Confirmed. Wikipedia: found 2013 at Wadi al-Jarf by a French mission led by Pierre Tallet and Gregory Marouard; "oldest known papyri with text"; Merer, an inspector with about forty boatmen; white limestone from Tura to Giza; the pyramid's name Akhet-Khufu, "Horizon of Khufu"; two or three round trips every ten days; dated to Khufu's year 26-27. Chapter's "Red Sea harbour" is right. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diary_of_Merer ; https://archaeology.org/issues/116-1401/features/1584-wadi-el-jarf-port-papyrus-khufu-cheops

20. Workforce. Claim: "twenty thousand workers"; permanent core of skilled workers plus rotating gangs of conscripted farmers serving a few months. Verdict: Disputed on the number; Confirmed on the structure. Estimates: Lehner about 5,000; an engineering study 4,000-5,000; Hawass 36,000 permanent and temporary; Wikipedia: about 5,000 permanent salaried workers and the rest on three- or four-month shifts in lieu of tax; others 14,567 average and 40,000 peak; Verner at most 30,000. NOVA: a year-round skeleton crew joined by farmers during the flood, as a labour tax. No scholarly source gives twenty thousand.
 Replacement: "...hand it out fairly to a workforce of tens of thousands." (or "to a workforce that probably ran into the tens of thousands".) Hawass's 36,000 and Lehner's 5,000 are the range; state "tens of thousands" only if citing Hawass; the cautious confident version is "to thousands of workers at any one time." Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_pyramid_construction_techniques ; https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/pyramid/explore/builders.html

21. Copper and timber. Claim: copper from the eastern desert and timber from across the sea. Verdict: Copper mostly Confirmed (Sinai, not the eastern desert); timber could not be verified for Khufu's pyramid. Lehner and Tallet: Wadi al-Jarf port supplied Sinai copper (used for tools). Cedar from Lebanon is attested for Old Kingdom Egypt (the Palermo Stone records a Sneferu expedition) and Khufu's boats, but no page confirmed it for the pyramid itself. Replacement: "They had to bring copper from Sinai across the Red Sea and ..." or drop timber: "They had to bring copper for tools from the mines of Sinai." Sources: https://archaeology.org/issues/116-1401/features/1584-wadi-el-jarf-port-papyrus-khufu-cheops ; https://asianreviewofbooks.com/the-red-sea-scrolls-how-ancient-papyri-reveal-the-secrets-of-the-pyramids-by-mark-lehner-and-pierre-tallet/

22. Abydos retainer burials. Claim: "first kings of Egypt, five centuries before the pyramid", buried in the south; rows of small graves; "one of those kings has more than five hundred of them"; practice then stopped. Verdict: Wrong on the number; Overstated on the interval.
 - Wikipedia (Djer): the tomb "contains the remains of 318 retainers" (Kuhn 2008); the Abydos page gives 118 servants for Merneith; "many dozens" usual. RationalWiki's table lists 317 for Djer; the older reference with about 600 counts both of his funerary complexes together. No source gives more than 500 for one king. The practice largely ended with the First Dynasty; subsidiary graves had all but disappeared by the Second Dynasty around 2730 BC (Smithsonian summary of the 2026 Campbell study, which analysed 46 skulls from Umm el-Qaab).
 - Interval: Djer reigned about 2990 BC, Khufu about 2560 BC, so about four and a half centuries; the First Dynasty as a whole began about five and a half centuries before.
 - Replacement: "The first kings of Egypt, four or five centuries before the pyramid, were buried in the desert in the south of the country. Around the tomb of each one lie rows of small graves, built at the same time as the king's own. One of those kings has more than three hundred of them."
 - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abydos,_Egypt ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/new-research-shows-that-these-ancient-egyptian-servants-may-have-been-brutally-sacrificed-and-buried-alongside-their-pharaohs-180989619/ (search summary; page itself returned 403)

23. Shang China. Claim: "nearly two thousand years later, the first kings of China did the same." Verdict: Overstated. Late Shang (c. 1250-1046 BC) royal tombs at Xibeigang near Anyang held over 100 beheaded victims in the filled areas of the royal tombs; 1,400 sacrificial pits at the cemetery; Fu Hao's tomb had 16 human victims; human sacrifice at "an unprecedented scale" (Wikipedia, Late Shang). Interval from Djer (about 3000 BC) to the Anyang tombs (about 1250 BC) is about 1,750 years. Wikipedia calls the Late Shang "the earliest known literate civilization in China"; Erlitou (and the traditional Xia) is earlier, so "first kings of China" is too strong.
 Replacement: "On the other side of Asia, about seventeen hundred years later, the Shang kings of China did the same, burying hundreds of victims at their royal tombs." Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Shang ; https://brewminate.com/the-tomb-of-fu-hao-war-and-sacrifice-in-ancient-china/

24. Later Egyptian servant burials. Claim: later kings buried with painted pictures of servants on the walls and small carved figures to do their work. Verdict: Confirmed in outline. Shabtis: small figurines meant to serve the dead in the afterlife (Mentuhotep II of the 11th Dynasty onward, common from the 21st). Wikipedia notes that the theory that they replaced human sacrifice is "somewhat improbable" given the centuries between the two. The chapter's "Then ... the practice stopped" is true; its implication that shabtis followed directly on the sacrifices is not, and "later kings ... went to their graves" with these things is fine. Painted tomb scenes of servants are standard in Old Kingdom tombs; no fetched page stated this. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shabti

25. Sargon the cupbearer. Claim: about 4,300 years ago, in Kish, "a man who served as the king's cupbearer". Verdict: Confirmed as tradition. Sumerian King List and the Weidner Chronicle: Sargon, son of a gardener, was cupbearer of Ur-Zababa of Kish before becoming king of Agade; reign c. 2334-2279 BC (about 4,350 years ago). The king list names several kings of Kish after Ur-Zababa, so some scholars doubt the direct succession. The chapter's "preparing to try" is phrased without hedging; "was said to have been the cupbearer" is how the sources put it, but the closing line works as a hook. Safe wording: "In the city of Kish, a man who served as the king's cupbearer was about to try." Sources: https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/sargon-akkad-establishes-akkadian-dynasty ; https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Sargon_of_Akkad

## Other checkable items noticed
- "Among its two thousand graves were sixteen unlike the rest": fine.
- "stayed one, with interruptions, for most of the next three thousand years": about 3100 BC to the Persian/Alexander conquest of 332 BC is about 2,800 years; "nearly three thousand years" is safer.
- "Herodotus ... two thousand years afterwards": fine (about 450 BC, about 2,100 years).
- Eannatum stele date: chapter's "four thousand four hundred and fifty years" is at the low end of 4,450-4,500; fine.
- Sargon dating: "about four thousand three hundred years ago" fits c. 2334 BC (4,350).
