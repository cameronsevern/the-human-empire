## Factual flags

1. **Minor — instruments in the Great Death Pit.**  
   > “They wore headdresses of gold leaf and necklaces of blue stone, and some of them still had their hands on the strings of their harps.”

   The instruments recovered from this pit were **lyres**, not harps. The distinction matters in an otherwise concrete archaeological description. The joint museum project identifies three lyres in situ. [Ur Digitisation Project, figure 5](https://www.austriaca.at/0xc1aa5576_0x003bca72.pdf).

2. **Major — hunter-gatherers are treated as uniformly egalitarian.**  
   > “In a camp of hunters, a man who tried to give orders was laughed at, and a man who would not stop was killed.”

   This makes one social arrangement universal. Documented hunter-gatherer societies included hereditary nobles who directed others’ labour and owned slaves, notably on the Northwest Coast of North America. The correction is to locate this account specifically in egalitarian, mobile hunting bands; hunting and gathering alone did not prevent entrenched authority. [Smith and Codding, *Ecological variation and institutionalized inequality in hunter-gatherer societies*](https://faculty.washington.edu/easmith/Smith%26Codding2021-PNAS.pdf).

3. **Major — Mesopotamian divine kingship.**  
   > “Egypt also took the story about kings a step further than the plain ever did.”

   “Ever” is contradicted by Mesopotamian rulers who became gods during their lifetimes. Naram-Sin was deified, and subsequent kings also received divine status. The Egyptian contrast works for the earlier period being described, but not for all Mesopotamian history. [Morgan Library & Museum, deified Naram-Sin](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/she-who-wrote/mold-fragment-ishtar).

4. **Minor — Khufu’s reign length.**  
   > “It was finished within the king's own reign, which lasted a little over twenty years.”

   The approximately twenty-three-year reign traditionally assigned to Khufu has been overtaken by evidence interpreted as reaching his twenty-sixth or twenty-seventh year. The Merer material invoked later in the chapter is part of that evidence. Distinguish an estimated construction period of twenty years from the king’s longer reign; the latter cannot establish the former. [American Research Center in Egypt, *Scribe*, Fall 2021, discussion of the Wadi el-Jarf papyri](https://arce.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Issue-8-Fall-2021.pdf).

5. **Minor — what Herodotus reported.**  
   > “A Greek traveller who visited Egypt two thousand years afterwards was told so, and the idea has been in films ever since.”

   “So” refers to slaves working under the whip. Herodotus describes Egyptians compelled to work in rotating contingents, with each contingent serving three months. He does not specify a workforce of chattel slaves or describe whipping here. His account is of compulsory labour—closer to the chapter’s later explanation than this contrast suggests. [Herodotus, *Histories* 2.124–125](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Herodotus/2B%2A.html).

6. **Minor — discovery and attribution of the workers’ settlement.**  
   > “However, in the 1990s archaeologists found where the builders had actually lived, and it changed the picture.”

   AERA began discovering and excavating the settlement in **1988**. Also, the surrounding narrative makes its buildings and food remains sound specifically attributable to Khufu’s workforce, whereas the excavated settlement evidence predominantly dates to Khafre and Menkaure. Describe it as evidence about the Giza pyramid builders more broadly. [AERA, fieldwork history](https://aeraweb.org/projects/); [AERA, dating the settlement](https://aeraweb.org/dating-the-lost-city/).

7. **Minor — the location of Djer’s subsidiary graves.**  
   > “One of those kings has more than five hundred of them.”

   Following “Around the tomb of each one,” this places more than five hundred graves around a single tomb. Djer’s total combines separate locations: roughly three hundred around his tomb and 269 around his funerary enclosure elsewhere at Abydos. The total scale is defensible; the described arrangement is wrong. [Alice Stevenson, discussion of the royal cemetery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1538992/1/Stevenson_Locating%20a%20sense%20of%20immortality.book.chapter.pdf); [Laurel Bestock, *The Early Dynastic Funerary Enclosures of Abydos*](https://archeonil.com/images/revue%202008%202010/AN2008-04-Bestock.pdf).

8. **Major — ruling multiple cities before Sargon.**  
   > “A king there ruled one city, and there were a dozen others just like his.”  
   > “To rule two cities, a king would need something that had not been invented: a way of being obeyed in a place where he was not standing.”

   Existing states already administered multiple cities. Lagash itself comprised three major urban centres: Lagash, Girsu and Niĝin. Lugalzagesi also established a larger territorial kingdom before Sargon defeated him. Sargon’s achievement can concern the scale and consolidation of empire, but cannot be the invention of governing beyond one city or beyond the ruler’s physical presence. [Lagash Archaeological Project, historical context](https://web.sas.upenn.edu/lagash/historical-context/); [Kesecker, *Lugalzagesi: the first emperor of Mesopotamia?*](https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/aramazd/article/view/893).

## Clarity flags

- **Unintroduced geography:** “They shared the same two rivers…” Neither river has been named. Identify the Tigris and Euphrates once; “the plain” otherwise remains vague for the intended reader.

- **Explicit signposting:** “There are two reasons, and neither requires anyone to have been especially wicked.” This announces the explanation instead of giving it, contrary to “No signpost sentences.”

- **Ambiguous institutional language:** “Each had its god, its storehouse and its clerks.” Later, “the storehouse” collects labour obligations, supports soldiers and effectively governs. Explain once that this means a temple or royal organisation, not simply a building holding grain.

- **Too many figures for one point:** The pyramid paragraph stacks the block count, block weight, height and duration of the height record. The next paragraph repeats the full block count. This conflicts with “One number per point” and slows an otherwise vivid passage.

- **Anonymity makes tracking harder:** “One gang at this pyramid called itself the Friends of the king. A gang that worked for his grandson called itself the Drunkards of the king.” Naming Khufu once would help readers follow his reign, pyramid, grandson and foreman. The style sheet permits names needed to follow the story.

- **Apparent contradiction:** “No one was fed by it or defended by it.” Earlier paragraphs describe workers being fed exceptionally well through the project. The intended distinction appears to be between supporting construction workers and the completed tomb producing food or providing defence. Make that distinction explicit.

- **Repeated wording:** “keeping the river rising,” “kept the river rising” and “as natural as the river rising” occur across the final sections. The closing echo could work, but the intervening repetition weakens it.

## Overall

A vivid, readable chapter whose central interpretation can remain confident. The most consequential corrections concern hunter-gatherer diversity, Mesopotamian divine kingship and the claim that governing multiple cities began with Sargon. The remaining fixes are mostly local. No files were edited.
