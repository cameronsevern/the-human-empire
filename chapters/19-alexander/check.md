Read the style guide, outline, chapter 19 and chapters 9–11. No files changed. Findings below are ordered by seriousness; confidence refers to the correction.

1. **Factual error; exception-breaking absolute.** “Nevertheless, anyone who wanted a post, a contract or a hearing in court now needed Greek, and ambitious families in every province saw to it that their sons learned it.”

   Egyptian-language contracts and Egyptian courts continued under the Ptolemies. Greek offered access to royal administration and advancement; it was not required for every contract or hearing. **Confidence: high.** [Ptolemy VIII’s decree of 118 BCE](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/ptolemy_viii_decrees.html) explicitly regulates cases involving Egyptian-language contracts.

2. **Factual overstatement central to the argument.** “Only the man at the top had changed.”

   Alexander retained much of the Persian framework, but also installed Macedonian military authorities and, in some places, separate financial officials. The next paragraph itself describes precisely that change at Babylon. Continuity is a defensible argument; unchanged government is not. **Confidence: high.** [E. Badian, “Alexander the Great and an Experiment in Government”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-classical-journal/article/abs/alexander-the-great-and-an-experiment-in-government2/616CAE88EC878B00CC37A298D416DFC6).

3. **Incorrect explanation of the measurement.** “It followed that the road from Syene to Alexandria was one fiftieth of the way round the Earth.”

   The required distance is an arc along a north–south meridian, not the length of a road. The simplified ancient account treats the towns as lying on the same meridian. Explain that assumption before multiplying; the later acknowledgment of their different longitudes comes too late to make this inference valid. **Confidence: high.** [Lucio Russo, discussion of Cleomedes’ account, §3](https://msp.org/memocs/2013/1-1/memocs-v1-n1-p04-p.pdf).

4. **Factual error about the battles.** “The method was the same each time.”

   The Granicus began with a contested river crossing and a cavalry struggle at the bank; the Greek mercenary infantry were attacked after the Persian cavalry broke. That is materially different from the following description of infantry holding the centre while Alexander exploits a gap. **Confidence: high.** [Arrian, *Anabasis* 1.14–16](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/46976/pg46976-images.html).

5. **Factual overstatement; exception-breaking absolute.** “It was the only defeat of his life, and his own men gave it to him.”

   Alexander suffered reverses, including a repulsed assault at the Persian Gates before eventually winning there. Calling the soldiers’ refusal a defeat is legitimate narration; calling it his *only* defeat erases those reverses. **Confidence: high about the reversal; terminology is interpretive.** [Diodorus 17.68](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diodorus_Siculus/17D%2A.html).

6. **Misleading chronology.** “At the time that stone was carved, the south of Egypt had broken away under pharaohs of its own, and it took the Greek kings twenty years to win it back.”

   The revolt lasted approximately 206–186 BCE; the Rosetta decree dates to 196 BCE, halfway through it. The sentence invites readers to count twenty years forward from the stone, when roughly ten remained. **Confidence: high.** [Archaeological Institute of America, “In the Time of the Rosetta Stone”](https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2017/features/egypt-thmuis-rosetta-stone/).

7. **Doubtful reconstruction presented as established provenance.** “A Greek from Libya, paid by a Macedonian king of Egypt, used the records of Egyptian surveyors and a geometry that had been put in order in the same library, in a city that had been a fishing village a hundred years before.”

   We do not securely know that Eratosthenes obtained his distance from Egyptian surveyors’ records, or that Euclid composed the *Elements* in the library. Eratosthenes’ own account is lost; the familiar reconstruction comes through later authors. The sentence makes two attractive connections firmer than the evidence permits. **Confidence: high that these details need qualification or removal; their actual circumstances remain uncertain.** [Russo, §3 and notes](https://msp.org/memocs/2013/1-1/memocs-v1-n1-p04-p.pdf).

8. **False contrast with earlier Greek armies.** “Greek citizens were farmers who went to war for a few weeks in summer.”

   Many were, but the contrast excludes established standing forces, especially the publicly maintained Sacred Band of Thebes—the very city where Philip has just learned soldiering. Philip’s achievement was not introducing professional service to an otherwise uniformly seasonal military world. **Confidence: high.** [*Oxford Classical Dictionary*, “Sacred Band”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550497312).

9. **Factual overstatement in the geometry passage.** “From these he proved everything else, one step at a time, each step resting only on the steps before it.”

   Euclid also relied on unstated assumptions: even his first construction assumes that two circles intersect. The achievement is systematic deductive organisation, not a completely explicit foundation from which every step follows without additional assumptions. “Everything” and “only” also break the style rule on vulnerable absolutes. **Confidence: high.** [David Joyce’s commentary on Euclidean constructions](https://www.euclids-elements.org/other-works/compass-geometry/compass1/).

10. **Misleading relationship to chapter 11, rather than a direct contradiction.** “That had been a language for clerks.”

    Followed by “Common Greek went further, because it was spoken in the street,” this suggests Babylonian was not a spoken vernacular. Chapter 11 makes the narrower point that Akkadian was a learned diplomatic language in the Egyptian and Hittite courts. It was also spoken in Mesopotamia. Preserve that geographical distinction. **Confidence: high.** [Yale’s overview of Akkadian](https://nelc.yale.edu/languages/akkadian).

11. **Overgeneralisation across the successor kingdoms.** “In each, a Macedonian king and a few thousand Greeks ruled a very large number of people who were not Greek.”

    “Each” includes Macedon, just listed as the third kingdom. The foreign ruling-minority model fits Egypt and the Seleucid territories much better than the Macedonian homeland. Limit the claim geographically. “A few thousand” also needs a defined population and period: Alexander alone reportedly left 23,000 military colonists in Bactria and Sogdia. **Confidence: high about the geographical exception; moderate about demographic estimates.** [Pierre Briant, “Alexander the Great”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/alexander-the-great-356-23-bc/).

12. **A general reader loses the chronology.** “What such men cared about in the end is shown by a story from after Alexander's death.”

    This begins a substantial excursion into the successor wars before “The stolen body” returns to Alexander alive in Babylon. The Silver Shields’ unnamed general and unnamed enemy are also introduced before readers understand the struggle they belong to. A clearer time anchor and a short explanation of the competing commanders would make the episode intelligible without requiring more names.

13. **A general reader is denied the identifying name.** “The most famous piece of stone in Egypt is a record of that bargain.”

    The object is never named as the Rosetta Stone. Readers who recognise it must guess; readers who do not cannot connect the account to anything familiar. The “few names” rule allows names needed to follow the story. Also, “in Egypt” misleadingly suggests its present location: it is in the British Museum. **Confidence: high on identification and location.** [British Museum exhibition guide](https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2022-10/Hieroglyphs_unlocking_ancient_Egypt_large_print_guide_The_British_Museum_2.pdf).

14. **A missing step in the practical explanation.** “The reasoning was sound, and anyone with a stick, a sunny day and a friend in a distant town can repeat it.”

    Readers also need the appropriate distance between observing sites and coordinated measurements; an arbitrary distant town and arbitrary observations will not reproduce the calculation described. State those requirements if inviting readers to repeat it. **Confidence: high.** [The assumptions in Cleomedes’ construction](https://msp.org/memocs/2013/1-1/memocs-v1-n1-p04-p.pdf).

15. **Explicit signpost sentence.** “The next step is the heart of it.”

    This announces the importance of the explanation instead of giving it. It directly breaks “No signpost sentences.” The following sentence already supplies the substance.

16. **Source commentary that style.md expressly excludes.** “The ancient writers disagree on whether this was a drunken whim or a decision, which is a fair example of the trouble with them.”

    The final clause interrupts the story to complain about sources. Moreover, the preceding sentence has already committed to the drinking-party version. State the secure event, or briefly explain the competing interpretations if the motive matters; the aside about “the trouble with them” does neither.

17. **Minor uncertainty argued on the page.** “No catalogue survives, and the ancient figures for its size cannot be trusted.”

    This is exactly the discussion of minor-source reliability that “Settle doubt before writing” excludes. The library’s importance does not require adjudicating its reported book count.

I found no clear direct contradiction with chapters 9–11, no hedging stacks, no explicit chapter self-mentions and no prohibited chapter-number cross-references. Item 10 is the principal continuity problem with those earlier chapters.
