## Factual flags

1. **Major — a new sentence reintroduces the “no alternatives” problem.**

   > “On one side, then, stood a quarter of a million people with nowhere to go.”

   The opening has fixed the earlier check’s objection by saying “few countries willing to take them.” This new sentence restores the absolute, while the surviving sentence “no country offered to take them in any number” compounds it. Alternatives were severely restricted, not nonexistent: approximately 35,000–40,000 displaced persons, most Jewish, entered the United States under Truman’s directive between December 1945 and July 1948. The correct point is that admission opportunities were inadequate and many survivors remained stranded. [USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/united-states-immigration-and-refugee-law-1921-1980)

2. **Major — British motives were not exclusively external.**

   > “Its reasons lay outside Palestine.”

   Palestinian Arab opposition to immigration and the danger of renewed violence **inside Palestine** were central considerations. Indeed, the following sentence’s reference to “another revolt” already undermines the new claim. Britain’s reasons included both imperial interests and conditions within Palestine. The government’s contemporary explanation discusses the conflicting demands of Palestine’s communities and their opposition to its proposed settlement. [British government statement, 18 February 1947](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1947/feb/18/palestine)

3. **Major — unresolved displacement is confused with remaining in the same place.**

   > “Caught between the two refusals, the refugees stayed where they were.”

   Refugees remained unable to return home, but many moved elsewhere or were displaced again. Denial of citizenship did not determine continued residence; the chapter itself identifies Jordan as an exception. UNRWA’s contemporary reporting records repeated movements after the 1967 war, sometimes several within one year. The accurate continuing condition is **exile and an unresolved refugee situation**, not geographical immobility. [UNRWA’s 1967–68 report](https://unispal.un.org/pdfs/9B785FA02FE8316F85256A7D00560076.pdf)

4. **Minor — the added sequence misrepresents overlapping developments.**

   > “Next came the people to work the land.”  
   > “Towns followed.”  
   > “A nation, finally, needed a language of its own.”

   Read together, these present land purchase, agricultural immigration, towns, and Hebrew revival as successive stages. Hebrew revival was already under way in the early 1880s, before the Jewish National Fund’s establishment in 1901 and Tel Aviv’s founding in 1909. These developments overlapped; language revival was not the final stage. [Academy of the Hebrew Language](https://eng.hebrew-academy.org.il/overview-of-hebrew/eliezer-ben-yehuda/), [JNF history](https://www.jnf.org/our-history), [Tel Aviv municipal history](https://www.tel-aviv.gov.il/en/abouttheCity/pages/history.aspx)

5. **Minor — the expulsion account merges different means of transport.**

   > “In July 1948, when Israeli forces took the towns of Lydda and Ramle, some fifty thousand people were ordered out of their houses and made to walk to the Arab lines in the heat of midsummer, and a number died on the way.”

   The forced march principally describes Lydda’s expellees. Ramle’s expellees were supplied with buses and trucks; the combined population cannot all be described as having been made to walk. The expulsions and deaths are not the problem—the attribution of the same journey to both towns is. [Benny Morris, “The New Historiography,” in *Making Israel*, p. 27, note 22](https://www.rahs-open-lid.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Morris-Benny-ed.-Making-Israel-University-of-Michigan-Press-2007.pdf)

6. **Minor — universal mobilisation is an incorrect absolute.**

   > “Israel, by contrast, put every fit adult into uniform, and by the summer it had more soldiers in the field than all of them together.”

   Mobilisation was extensive, not universal. For example, military service was deferred for approximately 400 religious students in 1948. The correct claim is that Israel mobilised a large share of its available population. This does not invalidate the separate comparison of army sizes. [Israel Democracy Institute](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/24626)

7. **Minor — the Herzl photograph is misdated and miscredited.**

   > “Theodor Herzl on the balcony of the Three Kings hotel in Basel, at the time of the early Zionist Congresses of 1897 and 1898. Photo: Government Press Office, public domain.”

   The photograph shown was taken by **Ephraim Moses Lilien in December 1901**, during the Fifth Zionist Congress. Government Press Office is the archival source, not the photographer. Surviving correspondence between Lilien and Herzl establishes the date. [Shapell Manuscript Foundation’s photograph and documentation](https://www.shapell.org/manuscript/theodor-herzl-signed-photograph-basel-switzerland/)

## Clarity flags

- **An ambiguous action:**  
  > “In February 1947 the government gave up the attempt…”  
  The nearest antecedent is the attempt to keep order. What Britain referred to the UN was the political problem; it continued administering Palestine. Identify the attempt as finding a political settlement.

- **A qualified statement becomes an absolute:**  
  > “The sellers lived far away, and the cost fell on the people in the fields.”  
  The preceding sentence says sellers were **often** absentee landlords. The summary silently removes that limitation.

- **A picture that obscures the geography:**  
  > “Picture a country in which more than half of one people had gone, and whole villages stood silent.”  
  “Gone” sounds like departure from Palestine altogether, although the preceding sentence places many refugees in the West Bank and Gaza. The loss was of homes and communities, not necessarily departure from the country.

- **An unclear pronoun:**  
  > “In Iraq it came in two steps.”  
  “It” could mean departure, dispossession, or legislation. The following sentences concern two laws.

- **A misleading verb:**  
  > “Its land had been shared out among the others.”  
  “Shared out” suggests a negotiated distribution. The preceding account describes territorial outcomes of war and armistices.

- **Signposts that announce rather than explain:**  
  > “The details showed how hard that would be.”  
  > “The towns on the road to Jerusalem show what driving out could mean.”  
  > “The same events go by two names, and each name carries a memory.”  
  These announce the difficulty, example, or comparison that the next sentences immediately supply. They fall under the style sheet’s prohibition on signpost sentences.

- **Padding through immediate repetition:**  
  > “The two scenes belonged to the same story.”  
  > “The aim, in other words, was to hold the ground before the larger war began.”  
  > “However they had left, a decision of the Israeli government kept them out.”  
  The first adds no connection beyond the juxtaposition already established. The second repeats the preceding military objective. The third is immediately repeated by the cabinet-decision sentence.

- **A schematic explanation interrupts the narrative:**  
  > “First…” / “Second…” / “Third…” / “Finally, they found weapons.”  
  Transitional words are encouraged by the style sheet, but this extended numbered argument makes the war passage read like a case presented point by point. “Finally” also risks implying that weapons acquisition came last, immediately before a sentence dating it to the spring.

- **An unexplained agency:**  
  > “The agency created to feed their people has about six million of them and their descendants on its books.”  
  Naming UNRWA would help readers understand the later photograph credit and distinguish registered refugees from people actually living in camps.

I found no hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions, or explicit chapter cross-references.

## Overall

The revision preserves the chapter’s numerical and quoted material, but it is not entirely fact-neutral: several bridges add chronology, exclusivity, or causal claims. Most earlier corrections remain intact. The main repairs are the renewed “nowhere to go” absolute, Britain’s supposedly external-only motives, and refugees supposedly staying in place.
