## Section 6: Steam meets cotton

### Calico Acts
- Outline: Acts of 1700 and 1721 restricted printed Indian cloth to protect wool and silk.
- Verdict: **confirmed, with detail.**
- 1700 Act: banned import of dyed or printed calicoes from India, China and Persia. Plain white calico and muslin were exempt, and so were cotton thread and fustian.
- 1721 Act: went further and banned wearing or using printed or painted cotton cloth, foreign or domestic. Fine Indian muslins, export goods and white cloth printed in London for re-export were exempt. Fustians (linen warp, cotton weft) were allowed, and a 1736 Act made that explicit. The ban on printed cottons was repealed in 1774. White cotton cloth was never banned.
- Sources differ on whether the wearing ban belongs to a 1720 or a 1721 statute. Use "1721" (royal assent March 1721, per one Cambridge article) and say "from 1721".
- Sources: Wikipedia, Calico Acts, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calico_Acts (search summary). Pseudoerasmus, "The Calico Acts", https://pseudoerasmus.com/2017/01/05/ca/ (read). The same post questions whether the Acts really protected British cotton from Indian competition.

### Dates of the machines
- **Jenny.** Outline: "1760s". Confirmed. Invented c.1764-65 by Hargreaves, patented 12 July 1770 (no. 962). Allen's lecture slides give 1765. Broadberry and Gupta give "introduced 1764, patented 1770". Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinning_jenny (read); Broadberry and Gupta below.
- **Water frame.** Patent 1769. Confirmed.
- **Cromford.** Outline: 1771. Confirmed but fuzzy. Wikipedia gives both 1771 (developed) and 1772 (built, running day and night in two 12-hour shifts). Say "1771-72". The "first factory in the modern sense" claim is an interpretation. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cromford_Mill (read).
- **Mule.** Outline: 1779. Confirmed. Wikipedia gives 1775-79 for the work and 1779 for the introduction. Broadberry and Gupta say "introduced in 1779". Hand-driven mules were limited to about 100 spindles. Once powered, a mule reached 1,200+ spindles by the early 1830s (Timmins 1996, via Broadberry and Gupta).
- **Power loom.** Outline: Cartwright 1785, not practical until the 1820s. Confirmed. Cartwright patented in 1785. Commercial success came with Sharp and Roberts in 1822 (Timmins, via Broadberry and Gupta). Horrocks's loom dates from 1813. Counts: about 2,400 power looms in 1813, 14,150 in 1820, 100,000 by 1833 (Allen). Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_loom; Allen paper below.
- **Self-acting mule.** Outline: Roberts, 1825-30, built during a strike. Confirmed. The first patent was 1825 and a second, improved one 1830. The 1825 machine was not a success. Smiles (Grace's Guide) places the manufacturers' appeals to Roberts in the 1824 spinners' strike, and an NBER conference paper says the same. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinning_mule; https://gracesguide.co.uk/Richard_Roberts_by_Samuel_Smiles (search summary).

### Fall in yarn prices
- Outline: **[CHECK]** fall in the price of yarn, 1780s-1830s.
- Verdict: **confirmed, numbers supplied. Allen's lecture slide and the Harley table disagree on the 40s warp figure.**
- Harley (1998) series, as printed in Broadberry and Gupta Table 9. Real prices in pence per lb, in constant 1825/9 prices:
  - 18s weft: 47 (1780/4), 15 (1810/4), 11 (1820/4), 10 (1825/9). A fall of about 4 times by 1820/4.
  - 40s warp: 168 (1780/4), 22 (1820/4), 20 (1825/9). A fall of nearly 8 times by 1820/4, about 8.4 times by 1825/9.
  - 100s twist: 761 (1785/9) to 52 (1825/9). A fall of about 15 times.
  - Calico fell less than yarn: under 4 times, 1780/4 to 1820/4.
- Allen's lecture slide says "cost of cotton drops by factor of 12 for 40 warp 1780-1825 and by factor of 4 for 18 weft 1770-1825". The 12 does not match Harley's table (about 8). Use the table's figures. The "12" may be a different measure; I could not tell.
- Early period: Harley's abstract (search summary) says coarse weft barely changed from the 1780s to 1800, while warp fell more. So the large falls came mostly after 1790.
- Sources: Broadberry and Gupta, "Cotton Textiles and the Great Divergence", https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/GEHN/GEHNConferences/conf6/Conf6-BroadberryGupta.pdf (read). Allen slides, https://kcborder.caltech.edu/Courses/Ec140/Notes/The%20Industrial%20Revolution2017.pdf (read).

### Labor hours to spin cotton: India by hand versus the machines
- Outline: hours "to spin a pound of cotton".
- Verdict: **corrected. The standard figures are per 100 lb, not per lb.**
- Allen's slides: hours to spin 100 lb of cotton fell from 50,000 (best Indian hand spinners) to 300 in the 1790s, and to 135 in the late 1820s. The 50,000 is a secondary lecture-slide figure. I did not trace it to the primary source (Allen's slides credit it to earlier work on spinning).
- Broadberry and Gupta Table 8 (from Catling 1970), operative hours to spin 100 lb of 80s yarn: 2,000 (1780, Crompton's mule), 1,000 (1790, 100-spindle mule), 300 (1795, power-assisted mule), 135 (1825, Roberts's automatic mule).
- Per pound: roughly 500 hours by Indian hand, 3 hours in the mid-1790s, 1.35 hours in 1825. The yarn counts differ between series, so do not present these as a clean like-for-like comparison.
- Sources: Allen slides (above); Broadberry and Gupta (above).

### Whitney's gin
- Outline: 1793, made short-staple cotton cheap to clean.
- Verdict: **confirmed, with a date detail.** Developed 1793, patented 1794 (Eli Whitney Museum, search summary). The patent was unenforceable and expired in 1807. Early Whitney ginning gave about a quarter bale a day. Steam gins of the late 1850s gave 5-6 bales a day (Lakwete 2003, via EH.net).
- Caveat: roller gins for long-staple cotton existed earlier. Whitney's saw gin is what suited short-staple upland cotton.
- Sources: EH.net, Phillips, "Cotton Gin", https://eh.net/encyclopedia/cotton-gin/ (read); https://www.eliwhitney.org/cotton-gin-and-its-legacies (search summary).

### Share of British raw cotton from the US
- Outline: about 1800, 1830, 1860.
- Verdict: **1830 and 1860 confirmed. 1800 uncertain.**
- Share of UK cotton imports by weight (Mann 1860, p. 112, as tabulated in Irwin, NBER WP 8689, Table 2): 59% (1820), 80% (1830), 82% (1840), 74% (1850), 78% (1859). Brazil and India supplied most of the rest. Use 80% for 1830 and about 75-80% for 1860.
- Another source gives more than 70% from the late 1820s, and 75-80% in the late 1850s, which agrees.
- 1800: I found no direct share. US raw-cotton exports were about 17.8 million lb in 1800 (a Japanese-language site, so weak), against total British imports of about 52 million lb. That implies roughly one-third, but US exports also went to other buyers, so the share of British imports could be lower. Other findings: US cotton was not imported regularly before 1791. Brazil was about 40% of Liverpool's raw cotton in 1791-1801. In 1806-15 US cotton was 57% of foreign cotton entering England. Say "a minority share around 1800, rising to a majority by about 1810" and do not give a percentage. Edwards, *The Growth of the British Cotton Trade 1780-1815* (1967), is the book to check.
- Sources: Irwin, https://www.nber.org/papers/w8689.pdf (read, Table 2). Cambridge JLAS article on Brazilian cotton, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-latin-american-studies/article/abs/rise-of-the-brazilian-cotton-trade-in-britain-during-the-industrial-revolution/F068D00AEB05EA8F6D4EB7437E26E705 (search summary).

### US cotton output growth
- Verdict: **confirmed, about a thousandfold, 1790 to 1860.**
- 500-lb bales (USDA series via EH.net): 3,135 (1790); 73,145 (1800); 334,378 (1820); 731,452 (1830); 1,346,232 (1840); 2,133,851 (1850); 3,837,402 (1860).
- Other sources disagree on the early figures: under 9,000 bales in 1790 and over 200,000 in 1800 (Eli Whitney Museum); 156,000 in 1800 (Mississippi history article). Say "from a few thousand bales in 1790 to nearly 4 million by 1860" and avoid exact early figures.
- Source: https://eh.net/encyclopedia/cotton-gin/ (read).

### Indian textile exports and British cloth exports to India, 1800-1850
- Outline: Indian hand spinners and weavers lost export markets and much of the home market. **[CHECK]**
- Verdict: **partly confirmed. Exports to Britain are documented. British yardage into India is not. The home-market loss is contested.**
- Indian cotton textile exports to Britain, thousand pieces per year (Twomey 1983, via Broadberry and Gupta Table 6):
  - India total: 2,200 (1790s); 1,824 (1800-09); 1,358 (1810-19); 431 (1820s); 271 (1830s); 304 (1840s).
  - Bengal: 787 (1790s); 1,331 (1800-09); 6 (1830s).
- Note that 1800-09 was a high point for Bengal, so the collapse came after about 1810. Total Indian textile exports to all destinations: 4,500 (1790s); 3,000 (1830s); 2,606 (1840s); 2,279 (1850s).
- Duties: the Pseudoerasmus post mentions Indian cloth duties in Britain of 27-59% in 1803 and 71-85% in 1813. I did not verify these against a primary source.
- Share of India's own cloth market taken by British goods (Ellison 1886, via Broadberry and Gupta Table 4): 3.9% in 1831-35, 35.3% in 1856-60. Twomey estimates 10% in 1850. The authors conclude Indian producers supplied more of their home market than British producers until at least the 1860s. This undercuts the strong "India deindustrialized by 1850" story.
- Competitiveness (same paper, Table 10): Lancashire unit labour costs relative to India (India = 100) fell from 240 in 1770 to 32 in 1820. They stress productivity rather than tariffs or Company rule.
- **Not verified:** a popular figure that British cotton cloth exports to India rose from about 818,000 yards in 1814 to over 51 million in 1835, and that Indian cloth exports to Britain fell from 1.25 million pieces (1814) to 306,000 (1835). It circulates on low-quality sites and I found no primary source. The piece counts above are the better-sourced alternative. Do not use the yardage figures without checking Parliamentary Papers or Parthasarathi.
- Positions in the debate: Parthasarathi (*Why Europe Grew Rich and Asia Did Not*, 2011) stresses tariffs and Company policy. Broadberry and Gupta stress British productivity and say Indian producers held their home market well into the 1860s. I read only the second.
- Sources: Broadberry and Gupta (above, read); https://pseudoerasmus.com/2017/01/05/ca/ (read).

## Section 7: Every party's perspective

### Typical mill hours
- Verdict: **confirmed.** In Crabtree's testimony, the ordinary day was 6 a.m. to 8 p.m. (14 hours) with an hour at noon, and 5 a.m. to 9 p.m. (16 hours) in brisk trade. That is a child in a woollen or blanket mill in the 1820s. The 1833 Act capped ages 13-18 at 12 hours a day and 69 a week, and those under 13 at 9 hours and 48 a week.
- Voth, *Time and Work in England 1750-1830*, finds annual hours rose about 20% (1750s to 1830s), mostly from fewer idle days (the end of "St Monday") rather than a longer day. Daily hours were roughly flat at 10-11 hours in his data (search summary of reviews; not read).
- Sources: Hanover College Sadler excerpt; Factories Act text (below). Voth reviews, https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/Book_Reviews/Voth%20-%20jeh.pdf (search summary).

### Share of children in the cotton workforce
- Verdict: **uncertain. Depends on the age definition and source.**
- Under 13: about 13-20% of the textile factory workforce in 1835. Nardinelli counts 56,000 children under 13, about 16%. Horrell and Humphries give about 20%. Chapman (1904) gives 13.4% in 1835, falling to 4.7% in 1838. Nardinelli's count fell from 56,000 to 33,000 between 1835 and 1838.
- Under 18: about 43% in the 1835 Factory Reports.
- A 2026 EHR article (You and Tertzakian) says there were nearly 60,000 children under 13 in textile factories in 1851 and that the census undercounted them. I read only the blog summary.
- Say "about one in six under 13 and about two in five under 18". I read none of the primary papers. These come from search summaries and one blog page.
- Sources: https://ehs.org.uk/child-labour-in-cotton-factories-during-the-industrial-revolution/ (read); https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:4acf3f21-c45a-41a2-91d8-49161bc0fd6c/files/mc260029c68e95a48e4efd7de2be41178 (search summary); https://www.galbithink.org/child.htm (search summary).

### 1833 Factory Act (3 & 4 Will. IV c.103)
- Verdict: **supplied, from the statute text. Read.**
- Covered cotton, wool, worsted, hemp, flax, tow, linen and silk mills driven by water or steam. Lace was excepted. Silk mills had looser rules.
- Under 9: no employment, from 1 Jan 1834 (silk excepted).
- Under 13: at most 9 hours a day and 48 a week, phased in by age: under 11 after six months, under 12 after 18 months, under 13 after 30 months. A surgeon's certificate of age was required. Two hours of schooling a day, with a weekly schoolmaster's voucher.
- Ages 13-18: at most 12 hours a day and 69 a week. No night work between 8:30 p.m. and 5:30 a.m. 1.5 hours for meals on a 12-hour day. Christmas Day, Good Friday and 8 half-days off.
- Four paid inspectors, who could enter mills, examine children and take evidence on oath. Penalties £1-£20.
- Adult men were not covered. One summary says the 9-13 limit was 12 hours; that is an error.
- Source: https://education-uk.org/documents/acts/1833-factories-act.html

### Quotation 1: sworn testimony, Sadler Committee (1832)
- Witness: **Matthew Crabtree**, aged 22, blanket manufacturer, examined by the Select Committee on the Bill to Regulate the Labour of Children in the Mills and Factories (Sadler Committee), 1832. The Parliamentary Papers reference is 1831-32, vol. XV. I did not see the date of his examination. The text is a reformatted excerpt (Hanover College, from the electronic text at the Victorian Web), not the original Parliamentary Paper page. Check wording against the original before print.
- Copied exactly from the Hanover text. Questions are in roman, answers follow "--".

> Will you state the hours of labour at the period when you first went to the factory, in ordinary times?--From 6 in the morning to 8 at night.Fourteen hours?--Yes.With what intervals for refreshment and rest?--An hour at noon.When trade was brisk what were your hours?--From 5 in the morning to 9 in the evening.Sixteen hours?--Yes.

### Quotation 2: Solomon Northup, *Twelve Years a Slave* (1853), Chapter XII
- Source text: Documenting the American South, https://docsouth.unc.edu/fpn/northup/northup.html. Chapter XII ("Of hoeing, of picking, of treating raw hands... weighing"). Pages 165-168 in that edition. I joined the line breaks and left the wording and punctuation unchanged. Checked against the page text.

> In the latter part of August begins the cotton picking season. At this time each slave is presented with a sack. A strap is fastened to it, which goes over the neck, holding the mouth of the sack breast high, while the bottom reaches nearly to the ground. Each one is also presented with a large basket that will hold about two barrels. This is to put the cotton in when the sack is filled. The baskets are carried to the field and placed at the beginning of the rows.
>
> When a new hand, one unaccustomed to the business, is sent for the first time into the field, he is whipped up smartly, and made for that day to pick as fast as he can possibly. At night it is weighed, so that his capability in cotton picking is known. He must bring in the same weight each night following. If it falls short, it is considered evidence that he has been laggard, and a greater or less number of lashes is the penalty.
>
> An ordinary day's work is two hundred pounds. A slave who is accustomed to picking, is punished, if he or she brings in a less quantity than that.

Also in Chapter XII, the gin-house:

> The day's work over in the field, the baskets are "toted," or in other words, carried to the gin-house, where the cotton is weighed. No matter how fatigued and weary he may be--no matter how much he longs for sleep and rest--a slave never approaches the gin-house with his basket of cotton but with fear. If it falls short in weight--if he has not performed the full task appointed him, he knows that he must suffer. And if he has exceeded it by ten or twenty pounds, in all probability his master will measure the next day's task accordingly.

(In the source the dashes are em dashes.) Patsey in the same chapter: "five hundred pounds a day was not unusual for her." The line "After weighing, follow the whippings" is also in this chapter. Chapter XIII also describes whippings at weighing time.
- Verdict: **confirmed (exact text found).** The outline says "1853" and gives no chapter. It is Chapter XII.

### Baptist versus Olmstead and Rhode
- Verdict: **confirmed.**
- Olmstead and Rhode, "Biological Innovation and Productivity Growth in the Antebellum Cotton Economy", *Journal of Economic History* 68(4), 2008, pp. 1123-71. Plantation records for 142 plantations and 509 crop-years. The average daily picking rate rose about fourfold between 1801 and 1862. They credit mainly new cotton varieties (larger bolls, easier picking). Picking rates rose very little on Sea Island plantations, and output per slave in rice and tobacco did not rise much either, which they say is hard to square with a general rise in whipping.
- Baptist, *The Half Has Never Been Told* (2014): rising picking rates came from a "pushing system" and a "whipping machine", with enslaved people devising picking innovations that planters captured. I read the Olmstead side of this and a reviewer's summary of Baptist (JEH roundtable, 2015, review by Olmstead, p. 919ff). I did not read Baptist's book or his reply ("The Response", *Slavery & Abolition* 36(1), 2015).
- Olmstead's further points: 600,000+ daily picking entries from plantation records; individual daily output varied 30% or more; Baptist has only quotas rising. State that it is a dispute with heated edges, not a settled split.
- Sources: NBER WP 14142, https://www.nber.org/papers/w14142 (read abstract). JEH roundtable, https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2015-murray.pdf (read).

### Real wages 1780-1850 and "Engels' pause"
- Verdict: **confirmed.**
- Allen (2009, *Explorations in Economic History* 46(4), 418-35): using Crafts-Harley GDP and Feinstein's wage index, output per worker rose 46% between 1780 and 1840, but the real wage only 12%. 1840 to 1900: output per worker +90%, real wage +123%. Growth rates per year: real wage about 0.39% in 1760-1800, flat in 1800-30, 0.86% in 1830-60 (output per worker 1.12%). The profit rate roughly doubled and capital's share rose. Allen's term "Engels' pause" refers to Engels' *Condition of the Working Class in England in 1844*.
- Challenge: a CAGE working paper (WP 474, 2020) puts output per worker +38% and the real wage +18% for 1780-1840 (search summary only, not read).
- Source: https://www.nuffield.ox.ac.uk/Users/Allen/engelspause.pdf (read).

### Optimist and pessimist positions
- Verdict: **confirmed in outline, authors only partly verified.**
- Pessimists: Engels and the classical economists. In recent work Allen and Feinstein (a plateau from the 1780s to the 1850s, under 15% gain for the average working-class family).
- Optimists: Clark (2001, 2005, 2007) holds that real wages grew faster than Feinstein found and GDP slower, so workers gained more than output per capita (Allen's account of him, read). Lindert and Williamson are usually named as optimists too, but I did not verify their position.
- Height and mortality evidence pushes toward the pessimistic side. See the next two items.
- Source: Allen (above).

### Life expectancy in Manchester and Liverpool, c.1840
- Verdict: **corrected: give a range and name the sources.**
- Szreter and Mooney (1998): life expectancy at birth about 25.7 (Liverpool, c.1841) and 25.3 (Manchester, c.1841).
- Davenport (2020, *Economic History Review* 73(2), 455-85), recalculated for the core registration districts, 1838-44: Liverpool 27, Manchester 28. Her figures for 1851-60 (Szreter and Mooney's): Liverpool 27, Manchester 30. For comparison, the unweighted mean of seven large towns was 33. She says the Liverpool figure of 25.7 used a single year (1841), and the Manchester one used only the districts the Registrar-General deemed urban. She finds no robust evidence of a big fall in urban life expectancy in the 1830s-40s. Szreter and Mooney dispute this (EHR 2021).
- So "about 25-28 years at birth in the core districts of Manchester and Liverpool c.1840" is safe. The level is low either way; the dispute is about whether it fell.
- Do not quote Chadwick's 1842 "average age at death" (labourers 15 in Liverpool, 17 in Manchester, from a secondary table, search summary) as life expectancy. It is the mean age of those who died and is not comparable.
- Sources: Davenport, https://www.campop.geog.cam.ac.uk/research/projects/migrationmortalitymedicalisation/publications/davenportechr2019foropenaccess.pdf (read, Table 3). Szreter and Mooney, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:ehsrev:v:51:y:1998:i:1:p:84-112 (search summary).
