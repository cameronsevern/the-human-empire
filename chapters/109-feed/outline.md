# Chapter 109 — Who decides what you see online?

**Line under the title:** Myanmar, the United States and China, from 2004 to 2025.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years. Where the evidence on effects is mixed, set out both sides plainly.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

A piece of software decides, and it belongs to one of a handful of companies. It chooses, out of everything that could be shown to each person, the items most likely to keep that person looking, because that person's attention is what the company sells to advertisers. Lies, rumours and propaganda are as old as speech, and this book has met forged pamphlets, a forged protocol and a murderous radio station. What is new is that anyone can publish to everyone at no cost, that falsehood travels faster than it ever could, that each person is shown something different, and that the rules which decide what spreads are the private property of a few firms. How much this has changed what people believe is disputed, and some of the strongest fears are not well supported. What it did in one country, where a single social network was in effect the whole internet, is not in dispute.
