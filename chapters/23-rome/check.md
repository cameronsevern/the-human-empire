Read the requested files; no files edited. Findings are ranked by seriousness. Confidence labels concern the factual correction, not confidence in ancient casualty estimates.

1. **The central claim wrongly includes incorporated citizens.** “Rome asked for no silver at all.” (Line 33.)

   Rome’s Italian *allies* generally supplied troops rather than tribute, but communities incorporated as citizens—including citizens without voting rights—also bore Roman taxation. The paragraph groups these different arrangements together, making the chapter’s central contrast too broad. **High confidence.** Source: James Tan, “The dilectus-tributum system,” in [*Romans at War*](https://www.academia.edu/40691209/Romans_at_War_Soldiers_Citizens_and_Society_in_the_Roman_Republic).

2. **Carthage’s army was not simply purchased.** “Carthaginian citizens rowed in the fleet, but they were too few to fill an army, and so the city bought one.” (Line 71.)

   Carthage combined mercenaries, levies from subject populations, allied contingents and, on occasion, citizen troops. In particular, treating African subjects and Numidian allies as interchangeable hired professionals distorts the comparison with Rome. **High confidence.** Source: [Jonathan Prag’s study of Carthaginian recruitment](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Acb02e469-f570-48f3-bb1b-a45a6c44e9f5/files/m6be60b2ed19838d65b3ec8d72ef9925d).

3. **The explanation of the Greek kingdoms’ defeats has a direct counterexample.** “A king who was beaten once had no second army behind the first.” (Line 143.)

   Antiochus III—the Syrian king just mentioned—lost at Thermopylae in 191 BCE, then fielded another substantial army at Magnesia. Limited capacity to replace losses is defensible; inability to field a second army is not. **High confidence.** Source: [Bar-Kochva, *The Seleucid Army*, chapters 13–14](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/seleucid-army/battle-of-magnesia-190-bc/68772C1D9DE667069C0CE8F21E2CFFC6).

4. **Overseas alliances disappear from the account.** “It did not offer the Spaniards or the Greeks treaties of alliance and a path to citizenship.” (Line 145.)

   This bundles two different propositions. Rome did not generally extend its Italian citizenship arrangements overseas, but it certainly made overseas alliances. Nor did it immediately make all Greeks tributaries: the settlement proclaimed in 196 BCE expressly freed specified Greek communities from tribute. **High confidence.** Source: [Polybius 18.46](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/18%2A.html).

5. **Fabius is placed after the wrong catastrophe.** “That was the strategy of the general who took charge after Cannae, an elderly senator whom the Romans nicknamed ‘the Delayer’.” (Line 129.)

   Fabius became dictator after Lake Trasimene in **217 BCE**, before Cannae in **216**. His avoidance strategy preceded the decision to seek battle at Cannae. Moving him afterwards removes an important change of Roman policy. **High confidence.** Source: [Polybius 3.87–90](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/3%2A.html).

6. **Pyrrhus’s final Roman battle is omitted.** “He met the Romans twice and beat them both times.” (Line 61.)

   He won at Heraclea and Asculum, but subsequently fought at Beneventum in 275 BCE, where the Romans repulsed him. He then left Italy. As written, the complete miniature biography makes him depart after nothing but victories. **High confidence.** Source: [Plutarch, *Pyrrhus* 25–26](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Pyrrhus%2A.html).

7. **The Athens comparison contradicts chapter 18.** “Athens, at the head of its league, took silver and gave nothing back, and it guarded its own citizenship so jealously that a man needed two Athenian parents to hold it.” (Line 31.)

   [Chapter 18](chapters/18-democracy/chapter.md) explains that the league supplied protection against Persia and initially accepted either ships or money. It even says Athenian naval power prevented Persian tax collection for nearly seventy years. “Gave nothing back” erases that protection; the criticism should concern coercion and unequal control. **High confidence in the contradiction.**

8. **Tribute does not entail perpetual impoverishment or no return.** “A subject who pays tribute grows poorer each year, receives nothing for it, and has every reason to wait for the day his master stumbles.” (Line 43.)

   This is an absolute economic claim, not merely a forceful judgment. Payment does not establish that total wealth declines annually, and chapter 18 supplies a counterexample to “receives nothing.” [Chapter 22](chapters/22-wall/chapter.md) also presents tribute as a potentially economical purchase of peace, despite its failures. The defensible point concerns the incentives of extraction versus participation, not an inevitable outcome. **High confidence.**

9. **Sicily’s settlement is made falsely uniform.** “First, Rome now owned land outside Italy, and it did not offer the Sicilians an alliance.” (Line 93.)

   Syracuse remained an allied kingdom after 241 BCE; Sicilian communities had differing treaty and tax statuses. The following sentence about taking a tenth of “their” harvest likewise needs a narrower subject. The development of the tithe system is less securely dated than this immediate transition suggests. **High confidence on the exceptions; moderate on the precise fiscal chronology.** Source: [Jonathan Prag, “Cities and civic life in late Hellenistic Roman Sicily”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ccgg_1016-9008_2014_num_25_1_1822).

10. **The ending makes unequal booty a general rule without sufficient support.** “They still paid for their own service, they took the smaller share of the plunder, and they could not vote on the next war.” (Line 157.)

    Allied soldiers sometimes received smaller distributions, but equal distributions are also explicitly recorded. A smaller share cannot simply be asserted for the army that destroyed Carthage. The voting grievance stands independently. **High confidence that the generalization is unsafe.** Sources: [Livy 40.43 and 41.13](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/44318/44318-h/44318-h.htm); [discussion of the evidence](https://brill.com/downloadpdf/book/edcoll/9789047430391/Bej.9789004160446.i-589_007.pdf).

11. **Roman recovery at sea was not uninterrupted.** “Each time, the Romans built another.” (Line 87.)

    Eventually they renewed their naval effort, but they also abandoned major fleet operations for substantial intervals. Polybius describes nearly five years of withdrawal before the final fleet. This omission matters because the paragraph uses immediate replacement to demonstrate inexhaustible resources. **High confidence.** Source: [Polybius 1.39 and 1.59](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/1%2A.html).

12. **Cannae’s entire army is put inside the encirclement.** “Eighty thousand men had been squeezed into a crowd so tight that most of them could not lift their weapons.” (Line 115.)

    The total army and the surrounded infantry are different quantities. Cavalry fought separately, and Polybius places ten thousand infantry on camp duty. “Most” being unable to lift weapons is also stronger than the evidence warrants. **High confidence on the numerical conflation.** Source: [Polybius 3.113–117](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/3%2A.html).

13. **The Trebia was not frozen over.** “In the first winter he smashed one Roman army on a frozen river.” (Line 107.)

    The Romans waded through cold, rain-swollen, breast-high water and fought beyond the river. The present wording suggests a battle on ice. **High confidence.** Source: [Polybius 3.72](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/3%2A.html).

14. **The harbour description is probably anachronistic.** “Its harbour held covered docks for more than two hundred warships.” (Line 69.)

    The famous naval harbour described as accommodating 220 ships belongs to late Punic Carthage. Excavation dates the circular harbour’s stone shipsheds to the late third or early second century BCE, later than this introduction to the First Punic War. **High confidence that the date needs qualification.** Source: [Hurst, *Excavations at Carthage 1977–8*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquaries-journal/article/excavations-at-carthage-19778-fourth-interim-report1/0CE50F90B3D6B5382B01A8D76A55DB63).

15. **A disputed explanation of ship losses is presented as established mechanics.** “The raven made a ship top-heavy, and Roman admirals knew little about weather.” (Line 85.)

    The boarding bridge’s effect on stability is disputed; its responsibility for the storm losses is not established. Polybius instead emphasizes commanders disregarding navigational advice. Retain the documented storm and decision-making without making the bridge its settled cause. **High confidence that the causal implication is doubtful.** Sources: [“Corvus,” *Encyclopedia of the Roman Army*](https://www.academia.edu/27950958/_corvus_In_The_Encyclopedia_of_the_Roman_Army), [Polybius 1.37](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/1%2A.html).

16. **Polybius’s fifty-three years did not all fall within his lifetime.** “In his lifetime, as he put it, the Romans had brought almost the whole of the known world under their rule in less than fifty-three years.” (Line 17.)

    His period runs approximately **220–168 BCE**; he was born around **200 BCE**, possibly somewhat earlier. “In his lifetime” is the error, rather than the attributed claim about the known world. **High confidence.** Sources: [Polybius 3.1–4](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/3%2A.html), [Oxford edition introduction](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780191612602_A23588695/preview-9780191612602_A23588695.pdf).

17. **The opening’s rules of war conflict with the reader’s recent experience.** “Since the death of Alexander, the kings who had divided his empire had fought one another constantly, and their wars had rules that everybody understood.” (Line 11.)

    The following sequence—one defeat, negotiations, survival—sounds like a universal convention. [Chapter 19](chapters/19-alexander/chapter.md) has just described forty years of succession wars, murdered royal families and a victorious army handing over its own general. Negotiated settlements were possible; “everybody” accepting this predictable script is unsupported. This is both a continuity problem and an exception-breaking absolute.

18. **The argument excludes obvious peacetime benefits.** “The only way for Rome to draw any benefit from its allies was to lead them to war, and so it did, in nearly every year for which a record survives.” (Line 45.)

    Available allied manpower also supplied deterrence and security without being deployed on a new campaign. The chapter itself describes roads, defensible colonies and durable political ties. “Only” and “any benefit” turn an argument about incentives for expansion into an untenable necessity.

19. **“Latin colonies” arrives as an unexplained category.** “In the tenth year of the war, envoys from twelve of Rome’s thirty Latin colonies came before the consuls and said that they had nothing left.” (Line 133.)

    Earlier, “Latins” meant neighbouring towns sharing Rome’s language; “colonies” meant Roman-planted settlements. The reader needs one sentence explaining that Latin colonies were a particular legal category, distinct from colonies of Roman citizens. Otherwise the decisive eighteen seem to be a subset whose relationship to Rome keeps changing.

20. **The cause of the second war is compressed beyond usefulness.** “About 2,245 years ago, a quarrel over a Spanish town gave both cities the war they expected.” (Line 101.)

    A general reader cannot tell whose town it was, what Hannibal did, or why Rome became involved. Naming Saguntum is optional; explaining the siege and Rome’s relationship with the town is not. “Give the reader room” calls for the missing causal step.

21. **The explanation explicitly announces itself.** “It is worth pausing on why this worked better than tribute, because the difference is the whole secret.” (Line 41.)

    This directly breaks “No signpost sentences” and intrudes on the narrative to announce an explanation. “The whole secret” also claims an exclusive explanation that the chapter’s later discussion of fear, local elites and military strategy does not sustain.

22. **The narrator refers to the book’s previous coverage.** “Every empire met so far had an answer ready.” (Line 31.)

    “Met so far” points to the reader’s progress through the book rather than to historical events. It is an implicit cross-reference under style.md. The named comparisons can carry the transition themselves.

I found no clear hedging stacks or explicit “this chapter” references. The main style problems are signposting and absolutes, not the narrator’s confidence.
