# Chapter 60 — Why is Latin America so many countries?

**Line under the title:** Spanish and Portuguese America, from 1780 to 1830.
**Length:** 3,800 to 4,600 words.
**Voice:** `style.md`. Answer the question early. No hedging; replace weak examples. Lead with transitions. Give the reader room. Plain numbered years.

## The idea the chapter leaves the reader with

Spanish America did not rise against its king. It lost him. When Napoleon kidnapped the king of Spain in 1808, the one thing that held an empire of mountains, jungles and ranked castes together was gone, and each city had to decide for itself who ruled. The men who took charge were American-born landowners who wanted to govern without Spain and feared the Indians, the enslaved and the poor below them more than they feared Madrid. Fifteen years of war, much of it between Americans, ended Spanish rule and left power with whoever commanded an army in each region. Bolívar's union fell apart along the old administrative lines. Brazil, where the royal family itself had moved in, kept its monarch and its slaves and stayed in one piece.

## The story

1. **Opening: two ships.** Answer first. Caracas, July 1808: a French ship and a British ship arrive on the same day with different news.
2. **Born in the wrong place.** The Spanish empire in America: its size, its ranks of birth and colour, and the grievance of the American-born rich.
3. **The last Inca.** The Andean rising of 1780 and what it taught the landowners. Haiti.
4. **In the king's name.** 1810: councils take power in city after city, loyal to a king who is not there. How loyalty turned into independence.
5. **The priest's bell.** Mexico: a rising of the poor that the rich put down, and an independence made by conservatives.
6. **The plainsmen.** Bolívar: twice beaten by other Americans. Help from Haiti. The cowboys change sides. Over the mountains in 1819.
7. **Ten thousand mules.** San Martín crosses the Andes from the south. A meeting with no witnesses. The last battle.
8. **"I am staying."** Brazil: a court that crossed the ocean, and a prince who kept the country and its slaves.
9. **Ending: ploughing the sea.** Why the union broke. The strongmen. Who gained. British loans and an American warning. Bolívar's last journey.

## Left for later

The United States' war with Mexico. The end of slavery in Brazil and Cuba. Twentieth-century revolutions in Mexico and Cuba.

- Population about 1800 and its make-up; how many viceroys had been American-born; the four viceroyalties.
- The rising of 1780 to 1781: its leader, the dead, his execution, the bans that followed.

- San Martín: career, the 1817 crossing (men, mules, height, days), the battle of 12 February 1817, the Scottish admiral, Lima, the meeting of July 1822, his exile. Ayacucho 1824.
- Brazil: the court's flight (date, numbers, escort), 1822, "I am staying", the cry at the stream, slavery until 1888.
- The break-up: 1830, Central America, Uruguay. Bolívar's death (date, age, illness) and the "ploughed the sea" line.
- British loans of the 1820s and the defaults. The British minister's "New World" remark. The American president's message of 1823.
