## Factual flags

1. **Major — new claim: creoles lacked power.**

   > “Their wealth did not bring them power, however.”

   Creoles possessed substantial local political power, particularly through municipal councils. Their disadvantage in appointments to the highest imperial offices did not make them powerless. The correct distinction is between considerable local authority and restricted access to imperial leadership. This new sentence reintroduces an overstatement after the earlier wording about appointments was improved. [Chile’s National Library, “El Cabildo colonial”](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-592.html).

2. **Minor — new chronology excludes earlier juntas.**

   > “The moment to act on that argument came two years later.”

   Following the discussion of 1808, this makes 1810 the beginning of action. Quito’s elites had already deposed the royal representative and formed a junta professing loyalty to Ferdinand on **10 August 1809**. The major wave described here came in 1810; action on the argument began earlier. [Quito municipal museums, *Quito del siglo XIX*](https://fundacionmuseosquito.gob.ec/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Quito-Del-Siglo-XIX.pdf).

3. **Minor — the Chilean transfer did not follow the stated pattern.**

   > “The pattern was the same each time. The leading citizens met at the town hall, deposed the Spanish officials, and set up a council that swore loyalty to King Ferdinand.”

   In Santiago, the governor Mateo de Toro y Zambrano relinquished his authority to the assembly and was then chosen to preside over the junta. His predecessor García Carrasco had been removed in July, before the September assembly. The common outcome was a junta professing loyalty to Ferdinand; the mechanism was not identical. [Chile’s National Archives, including the original assembly minutes](https://www.archivonacional.gob.cl/18-de-septiembre-de-1810-acta-de-la-primera-junta-de-gobierno).

4. **Major — an earlier flag remains unfixed and contradicts the chapter.**

   > “The regions had traded little with one another and had never been governed together.”

   The trade clause has been corrected, but “never been governed together” has not. Regions that became separate countries shared viceregal governments. New Granada, for example, included territories in present-day Colombia, Ecuador and Panama. The chapter itself also says that Argentina, Bolivia, Paraguay and Uruguay emerged from lands governed from Buenos Aires. The defensible claim is that these regions lacked a durable common national government, not that they had never shared government. [Spanish State Archives, administrative history of New Granada](https://pares.cultura.gob.es/BicentenarioIndependencias/catalogo/description/7016904).

5. **Minor — uninterrupted Anglo-Spanish hostility.**

   > “The whole country had risen against the French, councils of patriots had taken charge in the king's name, and Britain, Spain's enemy for the past twelve years, was now its ally.”

   “For the past twelve years” overlooks the peace established at Amiens in **1802**, before renewed hostilities in **1804**. Britain had been Spain’s enemy for much of that period, not continuously throughout it. [Spain’s National Museum of Underwater Archaeology, *Nuestra Señora de las Mercedes*](https://www.cultura.gob.es/mnarqua/en/colecciones/yacimientos/ns-mercedes.html).

6. **Minor — Spanish participation is categorically excluded.**

   > “It was not Spaniards who destroyed it. It was other Venezuelans.”

   The chapter shortly afterwards identifies their leader as “a Spanish adventurer.” José Tomás Boves was indeed Spanish-born and commanded the movement against the Second Republic. The civil-war interpretation is sound, but predominantly Venezuelan forces and Spanish leadership are compatible facts; the categorical exclusion contradicts both the evidence and the chapter. [Fundación Empresas Polar, biography of Boves](https://bibliofep.fundacionempresaspolar.org/dhv/entradas/b/boves-jose-tomas/).

7. **Minor — San Martín’s departure is put in the wrong sequence.**

   > “When they came out, San Martín went home, resigned all his offices, and sailed for Europe, where he lived quietly for another twenty-six years.”

   He returned to **Lima**, resigned as Protector in **September 1822**, subsequently travelled through Chile to Mendoza, and sailed for Europe in **February 1824**. The sentence places going home before resignation and makes the departure appear an immediate consequence of leaving the meeting. The twenty-six years from 1824 to his death in 1850 are correct. [Argentine Foreign Ministry, San Martín biography](https://eunes.cancilleria.gob.ar/es/17-de-agosto-aniversario-de-la-muerte-del-general-jos%C3%A9-de-san-mart%C3%ADn).

8. **Minor — the royal library did not sail with the court.**

   > “In November 1807, as a French army approached Lisbon, the prince who ruled Portugal loaded his court, his treasury, his library and some ten thousand courtiers onto every ship in the harbour.”

   The Royal Library was left behind and shipped to Brazil in **three consignments during 1810–1811**. Its inclusion in the November 1807 embarkation is incorrect. [Brazilian National Library, manuscript collections guide](https://www.gov.br/bn/pt-br/atuacao/colecoes-e-servicos-aos-leitores/manuscritos/guia_colecoes_manuscritos.pdf).

## Clarity flags

- **New signposts that announce rather than explain:**

  > “The conclusion they drew was a cautious one.”  
  > “Their argument rested on a particular view of the empire.”  
  > “It started from a difficulty.”  
  > “The outcome, at least, is known.”

  Each postpones the substance to the next sentence. These fall under the style sheet’s prohibition on announcing an explanation. This is different from useful transitions that state the connection themselves, such as “Policy had made the separation worse.”

- **Padding and repeated information:**

  > “It was a lesson about their own position.”

  “Lesson” has just appeared twice, and the following sentence explains their position.

  > “The first defeats came quickly.”

  This follows “he was beaten again and again” and precedes the defeats themselves.

  > “There he found help, and it came with a condition.”

  The next sentence supplies both the help and the condition.

  > “Nevertheless they held back. They also had a great deal to lose.”

  The surrounding passage already uses “They also knew”; the repeated “They also” is noticeable.

- **An unclear antecedent:**

  > “These were the burdens that weighed on the people at the bottom, and they answered.”

  “They answered” requires the reader to resolve “they” past “burdens” and infer an unstated call. Also, the preceding social hierarchy puts enslaved Africans “at the bottom,” while this passage principally concerns Indigenous communities.

- **A potentially misleading movement metaphor:**

  > “Many Brazilians had no wish to go back.”

  The intended meaning is a return to colonial status, but it follows an instruction that Pedro physically return to Portugal. The reader can initially take “go back” geographically.

- **An imprecise comparison:**

  > “Bolívar dreamed of something similar on a larger scale.”

  After the account of Brazilian monarchy, territorial continuity and slavery, “something similar” leaves the reader to identify political unity as the intended comparison.

- **An absolute broken by the next action:**

  > “In the rainy season, when no army moved, he led about two thousand men across the flooded plains…”

  His army immediately supplies an exception. The intended point is that campaigning was unusually difficult or unexpected in that season.

- **Stacked statistics:**

  > “In January 1817 his army of five thousand men set off through passes higher than any in the Alps, with ten thousand mules to carry its food and its dismantled cannon.”

  This combines a date, troop strength, altitude comparison and animal count, followed by crossing time and losses. It strains the “one number per point” rule.

No hedging stacks, chapter self-mentions or explicit body cross-references found.

## Overall

The revision preserves the original factual details but is not entirely fact-neutral: some bridges introduce stronger claims. The main corrections concern creole political power and the surviving assertion that the regions had never shared government. Several added signposts also slow the narrative rather than improve its flow. No files were edited.
