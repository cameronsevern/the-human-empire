# Style sheet

This is how every chapter of *The Human Empire* is written. The reader is a curious adult with no history background who reads for pleasure and wants to understand how the world got this way. The closest model for the reading experience is Yuval Noah Harari's *Sapiens*: a confident narrator, big ideas stated plainly, one vivid example in place of five. The book borrows that voice. It does not borrow looseness with facts.

## The voice

- **Say the idea, then show it.** State the point outright in a short sentence. Then make it stick with one concrete case: a person, a machine, a day, an object.
- **Tell a story.** A chapter follows a few people and things through time. The explanation arrives through what happened to them. It is not a case built point by point, and it is not a survey of everything known.
- **Be a narrator with a view.** Say what was strange, what was foolish, what was cruel, what worked. Dry humour is welcome. Outrage and sermonising are not.
- **Compare to things the reader knows.** Sizes, costs and speeds are set beside something familiar today.
- **"We" means humans in general.** The narrator never says "I". "Imagine…" and an occasional "you" are fine.
- **Lead with transitions.** Open sentences with words that show how they connect to the last one: however, by contrast, as a result, instead, in fact, first, second, finally, meanwhile, nevertheless. Vary them, and prefer them to a bare "But" or "And".
- **Give the reader room.** Do not compress. Before a new idea, spend a sentence putting the reader in the right frame of mind: what was just settled, what is puzzling, what to picture. Walk through reasoning one step at a time, and say why a fact matters before or just after giving it. Extra words that carry the reader from one thought to the next are welcome. Extra facts are not.
- **Plain words.** Short sentences mixed with a few longer ones. Each historical term is explained in the sentence where it first appears, or left out.

## Selection

- **Few names.** Name a person only if the reader will meet them again or needs the name to follow the story. Everyone else is "an ironmonger", "a mill owner", "one historian".
- **Few dates.** Give a date when the order of events matters. Otherwise "a few years later" does the job.
- **One number per point.** Pick the figure that makes the point and round it. Never stack three statistics where one will do.
- **Cut what does not move the story.** A true and interesting fact that sends the reader sideways belongs in another chapter or nowhere.
- **Length.** Most chapters run 5,000 to 7,000 words. Shorter is fine.

## Doubt

- **Settle doubt before writing.** If a detail is uncertain and does not carry the argument, write it in a form that is safe ("dozens of horses") or cut it. The page never discusses whether a minor source can be trusted.
- **Show a disagreement only when it is the story.** Where historians really are split on something that matters, say so in a sentence or two, in plain words, without a roll call of scholars: "Some historians think… Others point out…". Then say which view the evidence favours, or that it is open, and move on.
- **Fix a weak example by replacing it.** If the facts of a story do not cleanly support the point, do not qualify the story. Find a better one. If a claim is too strong, rewrite it as something true that can still be said with confidence.
- **No hedging stacks.** One "probably" is honest. "It may perhaps be suggested" is noise.
- **No absolutes that one exception breaks.** Prefer "mostly", "at first", "almost" where they are true. Avoid "only", "first", "never", "nobody", "everything" unless they are exactly right.

## Structure

- **The chapter never mentions itself.** No "this chapter", no "the question here", no "as we will see", no "we return to this below".
- **No signpost sentences.** Never announce that an answer, a point or a surprise is coming: no "That weakness is the answer", "So here is the short answer", "Here is the strange part". Say the thing itself.
- **No cross-references in the body.** Do not write "chapter 26 described". If an earlier subject matters, mention it the way a storyteller would: "Song China, seven centuries earlier, had…".
- **Headings come from the story.** Short, concrete, and a little intriguing. Never the names of the planning elements ("What people knew", "Consequences", "A plausible alternative").
- **The planning elements are a checklist for the outline, not a form.** A counterfactual, a what-was-known passage, or a survey of each party's position appears only where the story raises it, and is woven in.
- **Openings vary.** A machine, an object, a person at a moment of decision, a strange fact.
- **Endings land on something.** An image, a consequence, or an open question the next part of the story will pick up. No summary of what was just said, and no announcement of the next chapter.
- **Paragraphs are short.** Bulleted lists and bold run-in labels do not appear in the body.

## Explaining how things work

A reader should be able to explain the device to a friend afterwards. Describe what it does in the order it happens, using everyday objects for comparison. Leave out parts and refinements that the story does not need. A diagram carries the detail.

## Quotations and sources

- A quotation is used only if it has been checked against a reference, and it is short.
- Sources are not argued with in the body, and the chapter has no sources list. The references consulted are kept in a lookups file beside the chapter.

## A sample

> In 1712 an ironmonger named Thomas Newcomen built a machine beside a coal mine in the English Midlands. It was the size of a house. It boiled water, and used the steam to rock a huge wooden beam up and down, a dozen times a minute, pumping water out of the mine.
>
> By any sensible measure it was a terrible machine. Of every hundred shovelfuls of coal fed into it, ninety-nine did nothing but warm the sky. Almost anywhere else on Earth it would have been a joke. At a coal mine it was a bargain, because a mine is full of broken coal that nobody will buy.
>
> That is how the age of steam began: with a machine so wasteful that it was mostly useful to the people digging up its fuel. For more than a century afterwards, waterwheels still did more of Britain's work than steam did.
