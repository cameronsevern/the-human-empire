I found two factual errors and four design or style issues. No files were edited.

1. **False character limit** — [profile.md:16](toolbox/17-bamboo-book/profile.md:16)

   > “A strip holds two or three dozen characters at most.”

   Capacity varied with strip length and handwriting. The *Xing qing lun* manuscript, for example, has approximately **38 characters per strip**, exceeding the stated maximum of 36. “A strip holds a few dozen characters” would preserve the point without the false ceiling. This also breaks the style rule against absolutes that exceptions invalidate. The chapter repeats the same error; it is not a contradiction between the two. [Source: Thies Staack, *Reconstruction of Early Chinese Bamboo and Wood Manuscripts*, p. 17](https://www.academia.edu/24110722/Reconstruction_of_Early_Chinese_Bamboo_and_Wood_Manuscripts_A_Review_1900_2010_).

2. **False claim that strips lacked ordering clues** — [profile.md:27](toolbox/17-bamboo-book/profile.md:27)

   > “When they rotted or snapped, the book fell into loose strips, and nothing on a strip said which one came next.”

   Some manuscripts had **sequence numbers**, and lines on the backs can also help reconstruct their order. The Anhui University *Classic of Poetry* manuscript, for example, has original slip numbers running from 1 to 117. Broken bindings could create considerable reconstruction work, but the categorical “nothing” is wrong and breaks the same style rule. [Source: *A Brief Description of the Anhui University Shi Jing Manuscript*](https://doi.org/10.1163/24689246-00402010).

3. **Unintroduced reference to a burial** — [profile.md:25](toolbox/17-bamboo-book/profile.md:25)

   > “The first emperor of China, who ruled about a century after these books were buried, is said to have set himself a daily quota of reports to read.”

   No books have been identified as buried on this page. “These books” silently reaches back to the Guodian books in the chapter. The chronology is consistent with the chapter, but the reference breaks the instruction to give readers context before introducing a new idea. Naming “the burial at Guodian” would make the sentence intelligible.
