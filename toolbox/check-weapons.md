I found the following substantive issues. I found **no hedging stacks or forbidden signpost sentences** in the five pages. Confident narration alone is not counted as a fault. No files were edited.

**[12 — The siege ram](toolbox/12-siege-ram/profile.md)**

- **Card — misleading chronology:** “The siege tower, the catapult and the cannon.” Siege towers were already used alongside rams in the Bronze Age; they were not a subsequent development after the Assyrian machine profiled here. Correct the sequence to acknowledge contemporary towers. [Mesopotamian siegecraft, in *Warfare in the Ancient Near East to 1600 BC*](https://erenow.org/ww/warfare-in-the-ancient-near-east-to-1600-bc/10.php).

- **Text — false exhaustive list; breaks the rule on absolutes:** “For a long time, the only ways past one were to climb it, dig under it, or wait for the people inside to starve.” Surprise attacks and negotiated surrender also opened fortified cities. The three listed methods are important siege methods, not the only routes inside. Bronze Age correspondence records both alternatives. [Same source](https://erenow.org/ww/warfare-in-the-ancient-near-east-to-1600-bc/10.php).

- **Text — wrong; chapter contradiction; absolute:** “What they did was make the ending certain.” Rams increased the attacker’s chances; they did not guarantee capture. At Plataea, defenders disabled the attacking rams, and the attackers resorted to blockade. [Thucydides 2.76–78](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/thucydides-historian/siege-of-plataea/). The accompanying chapter explicitly says: “Jerusalem did not fall.” [Chapter 12](chapters/12-assyria/chapter.md:103).

- **Text — same overstatement carried forward:** “A city could no longer count on its wall, and its rulers had to weigh that against the price of opening the gates.” Walls remained useful protection, including against armies with rams. The defensible claim is that rams made walls more vulnerable and resistance more costly, not that walls ceased to offer dependable protection. The Plataea example above supplies the counterexample.

I found no clear factual error in the five callout labels. In particular, the chain-catching tactic is supported by Assyrian siege representations; the chapter’s uncertainty about the excavated Lachish chain does not make the general tactic doubtful.

**[18 — The trireme](toolbox/18-trireme/profile.md)**

- **Text — misleading generalisation; absolute:** “A trireme carried no cargo and hardly any soldiers.” This needs restricting to the lightly loaded fighting configuration being described. Triremes carried marines, and some were fitted as troop transports: Thucydides lists forty Athenian troopships alongside sixty fast warships in the Sicilian expedition. “Little room for cargo and a small contingent of marines” would describe the intended configuration more accurately. [Thucydides 6.43](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AThucydides%2C_translated_into_English_Vol_2.djvu/220).

- **Text — wrong scope; loses the chapter’s qualification:** “A man needed money to fight on land, since he had to buy his own armour.” Fighting as a self-equipped hoplite required resources; fighting on land did not universally require armour. Poorer men also served as light troops. [Hans van Wees, “Citizens and Soldiers in Archaic Athens”](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050358/3/Van%20Wees_Citizens%20and%20soldiers.pdf). The chapter’s narrower sentence is: “A man needed money to stand in the line at Marathon, since he had to buy his own armour.” [Chapter 18](chapters/18-democracy/chapter.md:85).

- **Text — overexclusive wording, internally inconsistent:** “A rower needed nothing except a strong back.” As a claim about equipment costs, the contrast works. As written, it excludes the skill and coordinated practice that the preceding paragraph expressly requires. State that a rower did not need to buy expensive armour.

No clear factual errors found in the card or callout labels. The approximate origin date is within a defensible scholarly range; I would not flag it merely because the earliest history is disputed.

**[19 — The long pike](toolbox/19-long-pike/profile.md)**

- **Text — unsupported necessity and political exclusivity:** “It also needed men who drilled all year, and so it belonged to kings who could pay them.” Philip’s training arrangements do not establish that pike formations inherently required year-round professionals or royal government. The Achaean League fielded a phalanx, and later Swiss forces used pike squares. The safe claim concerns what Philip’s funding enabled his own army to do. [Polybius 11.11–18](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/11%2A.html); [Swiss National Museum teaching material](https://www.landesmuseum.ch/landesmuseum/ihr-besuch/schulen/dauerausstellungen/geschichte-schweiz/190621_ufs_geschichte_ch_arbeitsblaetter_englisch.pdf).

- **Diagram label 4 — incomplete explanation:** “The rows behind hold theirs up, out of the way.” Holding the rear pikes upward is correct. However, the principal ancient explanation also gives them an active protective function: the mass of raised shafts intercepted missiles. “Out of the way” leaves out that function rather than stating a demonstrably false position. [Polybius 18.30](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/18%2A.html).

No direct contradiction with chapter 19 found. The final sentence broadens the chapter’s account of Philip into an unjustified general rule.

**[21 — The crossbow](toolbox/21-crossbow/profile.md)**

- **Card — mechanically overabsolute:** “Holds a drawn bow ready, so that aiming takes no strength.” The catch removes the need to sustain the bow’s draw weight. The shooter still has to support and steady the weapon. The accurate distinction is **aiming without holding the string drawn**, not aiming without muscular effort. [The Met, *A Deadly Art: European Crossbows, 1250–1850*, introductory mechanical account](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/A_Deadly_Art_European_Crossbows_1250_1850.pdf).

- **Card — wrong universal claim:** “The trigger of every later gun.” Early hand cannons were fired by applying an ignition source to a touch-hole; they had no trigger. Nor does a shared release principle establish direct descent from the Chinese bronze mechanism for every firearm trigger. [NRA Museums, hand cannon, c. 1350](https://www.nramuseum.org/the-museum/the-galleries/ancient-firearms/case-1-the-handcannon-the-matchlock/medieval-hand-cannon%2C-ca-1350.aspx).

- **Text — overstates the disappearance of chariot warfare:** “That mattered in a China where wars were no longer fought by noblemen in chariots, and each state filled its ranks with farmers by the tens of thousands.” Mass infantry displaced the earlier aristocratic dominance; chariots continued in Qin armies. “No longer dominated by noblemen in chariots” would preserve the intended point. [Virginia Museum of Fine Arts, *Terracotta Army* educator guide](https://vmfa.museum/wp-subsite/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Terracotta-Army-Educator-Resource-Guide.pdf). The chapter says wars were no longer “an affair of” chariot nobles—a social transformation—and later explicitly includes wooden chariots in the buried army. [Chapter 21](chapters/21-qin/chapter.md:138).

- **Text — unverified precision:** “A farmer could learn to aim one in a few weeks.” Less training than a powerful hand bow is defensible; this particular Qin-era timetable needs evidence. The chapter repeats it, but its lookup entry supplies no source for the number of weeks. Treat this as **unverified, not disproved**. The general training distinction is supported by [the Met’s crossbow study](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/A_Deadly_Art_European_Crossbows_1250_1850.pdf); that does not establish a Chinese training schedule.

No clear factual error found in the five diagram labels.

**[22 — The layered bow](toolbox/22-layered-bow/profile.md)**

- **Card — false universal:** “The bows of every horse people, until the gun.” Mounted archers did not universally use wood–horn–sinew bows. Japanese mounted archery used long laminated bamboo bows, for example. Narrow this to the many Eurasian mounted-archery traditions that used this construction. [Minneapolis Institute of Art, Japanese longbow](https://collections.artsmia.org/art/127185).

- **Text — misleading material property; absolute:** “Sinew resists stretching better than any wood, and horn resists squeezing.” Sinew’s useful property is its ability to sustain substantial elastic stretching without failure, not universal superiority in resisting extension. Its stiffness is lower than that of the bow woods compared in Kooi’s study. Describe sinew as tolerating stretching and horn as tolerating compression. [B. W. Kooi, “The Design of the Bow,” table 4 and discussion on p. 18](https://www.bio.vu.nl/thb/users/kooi/kooi94.pdf). This error is also present in chapter 22; it is not a contradiction between them.

- **Card and text — incorrect physical quantity:** “Stores the force of a long bow in a short one.” Also: “A bow built up from all three stores far more force for its length than a bow of plain wood, and so it can be a good deal shorter and still hit as hard.” A bow stores **elastic energy**, not force. Layering allows substantial energy storage in compact limbs; actual performance also depends on geometry, draw length and arrow mass. [Kooi, same study](https://www.bio.vu.nl/thb/users/kooi/kooi94.pdf). The chapter repeats the terminology error.

- **Text — overbroad scope; tension with the chapter:** “A farming state had no ready answer to that.” This can describe a particular army’s predicament, but not farming states generally. Zhao adopted mounted archery during the Warring States period. [National Museum of China, “Wearing Hu Dress and Shooting on Horseback”](https://en.chnmuseum.cn/Portals/0/web/exhibition/exhibitions/161120Chines-Epic/en.html). Chapter 22 also describes successful Han cavalry campaigns. Its point is that the response was expensive, not unavailable. [Chapter 22](chapters/22-wall/chapter.md:117). Because “ready” allows for difficulty and delay, this is a scope problem rather than an unequivocal contradiction.

The diagram correctly places sinew on the target side and horn on the archer’s side.
