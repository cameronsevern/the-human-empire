I checked the seven profiles, their script labels and date lines, and the matching chapters. No files were edited. Below, “doubtful” means a specific evidential problem, not merely confident narration.

**1. [The hearth](toolbox/01-hearth/profile.md)**

- **Card — outdated earliest date:** “Common by about 400,000 years ago; the oldest traces are a million years old.”  
  The million-year evidence remains valid, but “the oldest” needs updating. A 2026 Wonderwerk study reports recurrent, opportunistic fire use in deposits dated between 1.07 and 1.79 million years ago. These are traces of burning interpreted as human fire use, not surviving stone-ring hearths. Keep those categories distinct. [Research team’s account and paper reference](https://www.mncn.csic.es/en/communication/scientists-discover-oldest-evidence-opportunistic-and-recurrent-fire-use-our).

- **Text — factual overreach and exception-breaking absolute:** “Fire was also the first time an animal took energy from outside its own body and set it to work.”  
  Animals already exploit external energy: soaring birds extract energy from moving air. The distinctive claim should concern humans maintaining combustion and using its heat, rather than animals’ first use of external energy. [Experimental study of albatross energy harvesting](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0041449).

- **Text — factual error and absolute:** “Every engine and power station since has done the same thing with more fuel.”  
  Hydroelectric stations use moving water, not fuel; “every” therefore fails. Restrict the comparison to fuel-burning engines and power stations. [US Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/hydropower-basics).

**Chapter comparison:** No direct contradiction found. Chapter 1 repeats both energy sentences, so those are shared errors.

**2. [The voyaging canoe](toolbox/02-voyaging-canoe/profile.md)**

- **Card — misleading predecessor and chapter contradiction:** “Rafts and small canoes, within sight of land.”  
  This makes sight-of-land travel the condition before the double canoe. Ocean-going outrigger canoes also crossed substantial open-water distances. More directly, chapter 2 describes the much earlier settlement of Australia: “They built boats or rafts, loaded them with men, women and children, and steered for land they could not see, perhaps following the smoke of distant bushfires or the flight of birds.” The card needs to distinguish earlier craft from earlier navigational limits. [Te Ara’s account of outrigger and double-hulled canoes](https://teara.govt.nz/en/canoe-navigation/page-1).

- **Card — category mistaken for successor:** “The catamaran.”  
  The illustrated double-hulled canoe already is a catamaran. “Modern sailing catamarans” would identify the intended later development. [Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/canoe-navigation/page-1).

- **Text — unsupported universal intention:** “The people who sailed them were settlers bringing a whole way of life, with no expectation of going home.”  
  Settlement voyages are a valid focus, but voyaging also included planned return journeys. Neither the craft nor the presence of settlement supplies establishes that everyone aboard expected never to return. Describe these particular voyages as carrying settlers, without assigning an unrecorded intention to all voyagers. [University of Waikato research on ancestral return voyaging](https://www.waikato.ac.nz/news-events/news/research-into-ancestral-sea-voyaging/).

- **Text — chronological absolute:** “Last of all, about seven hundred years ago, they reached New Zealand, and the journey that began with a walk out of Africa was over.”  
  New Zealand was among the last substantial landmasses settled, but Polynesian settlement continued: the ancestors of Moriori reached the Chatham Islands around 1500 CE. “Last of all” and “was over” erase that later movement. [Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/te-ohanga-onamata-a-rohe-economic-regions/print).

**Chapter comparison:** The return-intention and final-settlement problems are substantially shared with chapter 2. The clear contradiction is the card’s sight-of-land limit.

**3. [The bead](toolbox/03-bead/profile.md)**

- **Card and diagram — misleading first-date framing:** “More than 75,000 years ago, in Africa”; diagram: “about 75,000 years ago.”  
  The card’s lower bound is literally true, and 75,000 years is appropriate for Blombos. It is misleading as the page’s only chronology under “First made,” however: shell beads from Bizmoune Cave date to at least 142,000 years ago. Identify the diagram as a Blombos example and give the older evidence in the card. [Original research](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abi8620).

- **Text — factual error:** “In one grave in Russia, three people were buried with about thirteen thousand beads of mammoth ivory sewn onto their clothes.”  
  At Sungir, the adult man occupied one grave; the two children shared another. The roughly 13,000 beads belong to the combined burials, not one grave containing those three people. [Trinkaus and Buzhilova’s burial study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/diversity-and-differential-disposal-of-the-dead-at-sunghir/B7672FB594E94A505A35E10C869F3808).

- **Text — exception-breaking absolute:** “A bead works only if everybody who sees it understands what it stands for.”  
  Recognition by the relevant people is sufficient. An outsider’s failure to understand a marriage or group symbol does not stop it working within that community. Chapter 3 itself describes recognition through “the right kind of beads”; it does not require universal understanding.

- **Diagram label 3 — factual overstatement and absolute:** “The same hole, in the same place, on every shell.”  
  Blombos beads exhibit several perforation types and variation in their dimensions and locations. Repeated choices and patterned manufacture are defensible; exact uniformity on “every shell” is not. [Vanhaeren and colleagues’ specimen analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S004724841300033X).

**Chapter comparison:** Chapter 3’s opening does not explicitly say the three shared one grave, and its illustration identifies “the two children’s grave at Sungir.” The profile introduces the erroneous single-grave claim.

**4. [The spear](toolbox/04-spear/profile.md)**

- **Diagram — unsupported dating of the assembled technology:** “about 400,000 years ago”; label 3: “Glue made from tree resin holds it in a notch”; label 4: “Sinew is bound over the join.”  
  Stone-tipped spears may be older than this, but that does not establish this particular resin-and-sinew construction at 400,000 years ago. Surviving adhesive evidence is substantially later; the oldest well-established birch-tar evidence is about 191,000 years old, and tar is not simply tree resin. Present this as a reconstruction of a later composite spear, or supply evidence for the dated assembly. [Review of archaeological adhesive evidence](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6825292/), [research on early stone-tipped spears](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4146534/).

- **Text — unsupported prehistoric generalization:** “In the earliest wars, too, most of the killing was done with spears, by raiders who surrounded a camp while it slept.”  
  Archaeology does not establish that weapon share or sleeping-camp tactic for “the earliest wars.” Jebel Sahaba provides evidence of repeated violence involving composite projectiles, including arrows or spears, not a count proving that most victims were speared asleep. [Original Jebel Sahaba study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-89386-y).

**Chapter comparison:** The second sentence transfers chapter 4’s twentieth-century Arnhem Land evidence into earliest prehistory. The chapter explicitly introduces an observer working “In the late 1920s” and cautions: “Arnhem Land was bloodier than most places, and it would be wrong to take it as the average.” This is an unjustified expansion of the chapter’s evidence, rather than a numerical contradiction.

I would **not** reject “More than 400,000 years ago” simply because Schöningen has been redated: the older Kathu Pan stone-point evidence must also be considered.

**5. [The grinding stone](toolbox/05-grinding-stone/profile.md)**

- **Card — unsupported origin claim:** “More than 23,000 years ago, at the eastern end of the Mediterranean.”  
  Ohalo II establishes use there around 23,000 years ago, not invention there. Grinding tools with plant residues occur in Europe around 30,000 years ago; a tool from Grotta Paglicci carries evidence of oat processing around 32,600 years ago. The lower bound is true, but the “First made” field turns one example into a geographical origin. [Revedin and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2973873/), [Paglicci study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1505213112). The diagram’s “about 23,000 years ago” is acceptable if explicitly tied to the Ohalo example.

- **Text — evidence generalized beyond its stated case:** “The skeletons from the first farming villages show what it cost them, in toes, knees and backs.”  
  The specific anatomical argument used here comes from Abu Hureyra. Name that village rather than implying this precise combination has been demonstrated generally across the first villages. [Molleson’s original account](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-eloquent-bones-of-abu-hureyra/).

**Chapter comparison:** Chapter 5 correctly introduces the skeletal example with “The clearest evidence comes from a single village.” The profile broadens that evidence. No direct contradiction otherwise.

“Once people lived on grain, the grinding never stopped” reads as ordinary figurative language for recurring labour; I would not flag it as a literal claim of uninterrupted grinding.

**6. [The ration bowl](toolbox/06-ration-bowl/profile.md)**

- **Card and diagram — disputed function presented as a measured fact:** “Measures out one worker’s food”; label 4: “Filled, it holds one worker’s ration.”  
  Worker-ration distribution is an influential interpretation, but these statements additionally assume a known individual serving and a measuring function. Residue analysis at Shakhi Kora identified animal-based foods and supports a wider range of food uses than a standardized grain allowance. A defensible description is a mass-produced food bowl, with ration distribution identified as an interpretation. [Perruchini and colleagues’ research](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/284715/).

- **Text — false uniformity and unsupported inference:** “A bowl made by the thousand, all of one size, means somebody was feeding workers by the thousand, out of a storehouse.”  
  The bowls were not all one size. Published measurements from Chogha Mish range from roughly 0.4 to 2.6 litres. Nor does a cumulative total of thousands of vessels establish thousands of simultaneous recipients or one particular storehouse arrangement. **“All of one size” also breaks the style rule on absolutes.** [Measurements and archaeological contexts](https://www.academia.edu/123756153/Beveled_Rim_Bowls_at_Tell_Qraya_and_Beyond).

- **Text and diagram — one reconstruction treated as the universal production/use sequence:** “This bowl was pressed into a mould in a few moments, baked, used and thrown away.” Label 2: “Pressed in a mould in moments, not thrown on a wheel.”  
  Rapid manufacture without wheel-throwing is defensible. The exact forming method remains debated, including hand-forming and moulding; routine disposal after a single use is also an interpretation, not an established lifecycle for every bowl. Remove the unsupported precision about method, timing and disposability, or anchor it to a particular experiment or assemblage. [Potts’s review](https://is.muni.cz/el/phil/jaro2013/PAPVA_43/um/Potts_2009.pdf), [contextual study](https://www.academia.edu/123756153/Beveled_Rim_Bowls_at_Tell_Qraya_and_Beyond).

**Chapter comparison:** Chapter 6 and the profile both say, “Each one probably held a worker’s ration of food.” The card and diagram remove that uncertainty and add a precise measuring claim. The single **“probably” is appropriate**, not a hedging violation. The manufacturing/disposal problem is shared with the chapter.

**7. [The Nile boat](toolbox/07-nile-boat/profile.md)**

- **Text — historical error, chapter contradiction and exception-breaking absolute:** “That is a large part of why Egypt became one kingdom so early, and stayed one.”  
  Egypt repeatedly fragmented during the Intermediate Periods. Chapter 7 retains the necessary qualification: “It stayed one, with interruptions, for most of the next three thousand years.” Restore that limited claim. The Third Intermediate Period alone included centuries of divided rule. [Metropolitan Museum account](https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/egypt-in-the-third-intermediate-period-1070-712-b-c).

No further clear factual error emerged in this page’s card, date line or callout wording.

Across all seven pages, I found **no hedging stacks or clear prohibited signpost sentences**. “That is why beads matter…” explains the preceding argument; it does not announce a forthcoming answer. The substantive style breaches are the unsupported absolutes identified above.
