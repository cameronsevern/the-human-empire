Title: THE SILK REEL. Date line: about 2,000 years ago. How silk thread was reeled in a farmhouse in Han dynasty China, shown as a plain side view of the equipment only, with no people and no hands. At the left, a small low stove of baked clay with a little wood fire glowing in its opening, and on it a wide shallow pan of hot water, the water a pale blue-grey with two faint wisps of steam. Five white oval silkworm cocoons, each about the size of a peanut shell, float in the pan. From each cocoon one very fine strand rises; the five strands slant upwards and meet at a small ring held above the pan on a thin wooden arm fixed to an upright post standing beside the stove. From the ring a single slightly thicker thread runs to the right and is wound onto the reel: a simple wooden frame of four arms in a cross on a horizontal axle, carried on a plain wooden stand and turned by a small crank handle at the axle, with pale silk thread wound in a band round the ends of the four arms. A small curved arrow beside the frame shows the direction in which it turns. The four-armed turning frame is the terracotta-accented part. In the top right corner, separate from the rest, a single green mulberry leaf with one pale silkworm caterpillar on it. Everything is plain wood, clay and bronze of ancient China: no treadle, no gears, no metal machinery, no spinning wheel with a drive band. Place the labels in clear space so that no label overlaps the pan, the strands, the frame, the leaf or another label. Callouts: 1 'Hot water softens the gum that holds a cocoon together'; 2 'A strand from each cocoon, too fine to use alone'; 3 'A ring gathers the strands into one thread'; 4 'The turning frame pulls the thread and winds it up'; 5 'A silkworm on a mulberry leaf'.
