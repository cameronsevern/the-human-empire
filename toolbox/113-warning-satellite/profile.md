# The early-warning satellite

| | |
|---|---|
| **First made** | Soviet Union, first launched 1972, on combat duty from 1982 |
| **Made of** | A half-metre telescope, infrared sensor, engines and radio: 2.4 tonnes |
| **What it does** | Watches the enemy's missile fields for the hot flame of a launch |
| **Before it** | America's Midas satellites; Midas 7 saw missile launches in 1963 |
| **After it** | Russia's Tundra satellites, the first launched in November 2015 |

## Why it mattered

The Soviet sensors could pick out a rocket's flame against the cold black of space, but not against the warm, cloudy Earth. The satellites therefore did not look straight down. Each one hung near the top of a long, looping orbit, almost 40,000 kilometres up, and watched the American missile fields edge-on, so that a rising rocket would show against space.

A satellite was in the right place for only about six hours a day, so it took at least four to keep watch round the clock, and the system was built for nine. Each sent its pictures by radio, as they were taken, to a control centre about seventy kilometres south-west of Moscow.
