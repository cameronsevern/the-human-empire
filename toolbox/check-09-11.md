**[09 — The clay envelope](toolbox/09-clay-envelope/profile.md)**

1. **Wrong chronology — high confidence.**  
   > “Clay envelopes went out of use when people began to write on skins and on sheets of papyrus, which cannot be wrapped in mud.”

   Papyrus writing predates the envelopes described here: surviving written papyri reach back to the twenty-sixth century BCE. These materials coexisted; their introduction cannot explain clay envelopes’ disappearance. Describe the eventual replacement of clay documents without making it coincide with the invention of other writing surfaces. [University of Hamburg: papyrus](https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/profiling-guide/materials/writing-surfaces/papyrus.html).

2. **Doubtful invention date — medium-high confidence.**  
   > “First made | About 4,000 years ago, on the plain of Iraq”

   Four thousand years ago fits the Old Babylonian examples, but envelopes enclosing written tablets are attested in the second half of the third millennium BCE. Distinguish the date of the illustrated type from its first manufacture. [Study of enclosed cuneiform tablets](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-026-02568-7).

3. **Unbreakable absolute — high confidence.**  
   > “A document closed with wax, a letter in a gummed envelope, and the foil under the cap of a medicine bottle all rest on the same idea: a thing has not been touched if the seal is whole.”

   An intact-looking seal is evidence, not proof, of non-interference. The defensible principle is that opening should leave visible evidence. This also affects the card’s “tamper-proof lid” and “where nobody can change it.” These are exceptions-breaking absolutes under `style.md`. [Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration’s definition of tamper-evident packaging](https://www.tga.gov.au/products/regulations-all-products/labelling-and-packaging/code-practice-tamper-evident-packaging-therapeutic-goods).

4. **Overgeneralised procedure — medium confidence.**  
   > “The same words are written again on the outside, so that anybody can read the terms without opening it.”

   Contract envelopes could repeat the text in full **or in abbreviated form**. Exact duplication is a valid example, but the page presents it as the universal mechanism. The matching chapter makes the same simplification, so this is not a chapter contradiction. [Study of legal envelopes from Old Babylonian Alalah](https://www.academia.edu/166226867/Clay_Envelopes_Inscribed_with_Legal_Texts_from_Old_Babylonian_Alalah_Level_VII_).

5. **Prompt ambiguity — high confidence.**  
   > “If the two sides ever disagree, a judge breaks the envelope and reads the tablet inside.”

   Step 5 clearly means the contracting parties. Prompt label 5 says **“If the two ever disagree”**, which can mean the two written versions. That changes the apparent trigger for opening—and the inner version cannot ordinarily be compared before opening. Name the parties in the label. Steps 1–4 otherwise match their labels.

6. **Missing required photograph — high confidence.**  
   There is no sentence to quote for this omission: the required **“The real thing”** section is absent. A suitable licensed photograph is already documented in [chapter 09’s image ledger](chapters/09-law/images/ledger.md): `contract-tablet-envelope-berlin.jpg`, Einsamer Schuetze, CC BY-SA 3.0. Two photograph files also already exist in the toolbox folder.

   **Chapter check:** I found no direct contradiction with chapter 09.

**[10 — The spoked wheel](toolbox/10-spoked-wheel/profile.md)**

1. **Wrong mechanical explanation — high confidence.**  
   > “Its wire spokes are too thin to be squeezed, so the hub hangs from the spokes above it and does not stand on the ones below.”

   Bicycle spokes are prestensioned. Under an ordinary radial load, the principal change is a **reduction in tension in the bottom spokes**, rather than extra tension in the upper spokes. The wires generally remain in tension; they do not become wooden-style compression struts. The “hangs from above” explanation misidentifies how the added load is carried. [Experimental paper, *Bicycle Wheel as Prestressed Structure*](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9399%281993%29119%3A3%28439%29).

2. **False absolute and chapter conflict — high confidence.**  
   > “Give it two light wheels and a small platform, and it will pull two men faster than anything on earth had moved before.”

   Chariots were not faster than every earlier animal or moving object. Even if this means human travel, chapter 10 has already introduced mounted riders before its chariot section. Its concrete chariot claim is two horses carrying two people at over thirty kilometres an hour. Use a bounded speed comparison rather than a universal record. The single-horse implication here also differs from the chapter’s two-horse example, although one-horse vehicles are not inherently impossible.

3. **Wrong generalisation about horses — high confidence.**  
   > “A horse will not, since it is built to run and it tires quickly under a dead weight.”

   Horses can haul heavy wagons for sustained journeys. What lighter wheels enable is faster travel with less vehicle weight; inability to haul is not the distinction. The chapter repeats a milder version of this claim, so agreement with it does not resolve the factual problem. [National Army Museum: horses transporting heavy loads over long distances](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/horse-power-first-world-war).

4. **Unsupported exclusion of other improvements — high confidence.**  
   > “The wooden spoked wheel lasted about four thousand years with one large improvement, an iron tyre shrunk onto the rim while hot, which holds the whole wheel tight as it cools.”

   Iron tyres were important, but not the only substantial improvement. Dished wheels—whose spokes sit at an angle—improved resistance to sideways forces. Sectional rims and reinforced hubs also developed. “One large improvement” violates the rule against an absolute defeated by an established counterexample. [Worshipful Company of Wheelwrights: craft history](https://wheelwrights.org/craft/).

5. **Mechanical oversimplification — high confidence.**  
   > “The wood between the hub and the rim only has to stop the two from moving closer together, and a few thin rods can do that as well as a thick plank can.”

   “Only” excludes the sideways forces that wheels also encounter. Spokes must maintain the rim’s position under those forces as well as support vertical loading; their dimensions and construction matter. The same [wheelwrights’ account](https://wheelwrights.org/craft/) explicitly describes sideways thrust as a wheel-design problem.

6. **Missing required photograph — high confidence.**  
   The required **“The real thing”** section is absent. [Chapter 10’s image ledger](chapters/10-wheel/images/ledger.md) already records `tutankhamun-chariot.jpg`, Rüdiger Stehn, CC BY-SA 2.0. It would need a caption making clear that this surviving wheel is later than the earliest steppe examples.

   **Prompt check:** All five numbered steps match their corresponding labels. The extra explanation in step 4 is elaboration, not a mismatch.

**[11 — The copper slab](toolbox/11-copper-slab/profile.md)**

1. **Unsupported weight standardisation — high confidence.**  
   > “The slabs were also all of about the same weight. A clerk on the quay did not have to weigh a shipment lump by lump. He could count it, and check a few.”

   Known oxhide ingots vary substantially—roughly 17–40 kg in Jones’s survey. Counting could support a rough estimate, but the asserted sampling procedure is not established. Similar form does not establish interchangeable weight. [Jones, *Oxhide Ingots*, pp. 84–85](https://www.academia.edu/7971537/Oxhide_Ingots_Copper_Production_and_the_Mediterranean_Trade_in_Copper_and_Other_Metals_in_the_Bronze_Age_MA_Thesis_Department_of_Anthropology_Texas_A_and_M_University_2007).

2. **End date too abrupt — high confidence.**  
   > “The shape went out of use when the palaces fell.”

   Oxhide ingots continued into the period after the collapse around 1200 BCE; research places their production, exchange and use through approximately 1100–1000 BCE. Their disappearance was not simultaneous with the palaces’ destruction. [Research on oxhide-ingot chronology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ojoa.12077).

3. **Doubtful manufacturing detail — medium-high confidence.**  
   > “Molten copper was poured into an open mould cut in stone, and the top of the metal cooled in the air.”

   Open casting is sound; stone as the general mould material is not established. A stone mould survives at Ras Ibn Hani, but sand or clay-based moulds are considered likely for many oxhide ingots. Remove “cut in stone.” [Jones, pp. 226–229](https://www.academia.edu/7971537/Oxhide_Ingots_Copper_Production_and_the_Mediterranean_Trade_in_Copper_and_Other_Metals_in_the_Bronze_Age_MA_Thesis_Department_of_Anthropology_Texas_A_and_M_University_2007).

4. **Modern comparison overstates uniformity — high confidence.**  
   > “Metal still leaves a smelter as blocks of a standard size and weight, stamped with the maker's mark, and a gold bar in a bank vault is still counted before it is weighed.”

   Recognised specifications do not mean identical weights. London Good Delivery gold bars can contain 350–430 fine troy ounces. The claimed counting-before-weighing sequence is unsupported and does not demonstrate the proposed ancient continuity. [LBMA technical specifications](https://www.lbma.org.uk/publications/good-delivery-rules/technical-specifications).

5. **Unproven carrying limit — high confidence that the absolute is unsupported.**  
   > “It is as heavy as one man can carry any distance, and no heavier.”

   There is no single human carrying limit, and ingot weights vary. Shoulder carrying is depicted, but transport could also involve paired porters or pack animals. Say it was manageable cargo, not precisely the maximum possible load. [Jones, pp. 85–86](https://www.academia.edu/7971537/Oxhide_Ingots_Copper_Production_and_the_Mediterranean_Trade_in_Copper_and_Other_Metals_in_the_Bronze_Age_MA_Thesis_Department_of_Anthropology_Texas_A_and_M_University_2007).

6. **Uncertain attribution; minor prompt difference — medium confidence.**  
   > “Many slabs carry a mark, cut or stamped in by the maker or the shipper.”

   The marks exist, but who applied particular marks and their functions are not always known. State the observable marking without assigning its author. Prompt label 4 also says only **“A mark cut in”**; this is narrower than “cut or stamped,” though compatible with the particular illustrated example. [Jones, discussion of ingot marks](https://www.academia.edu/7971537/Oxhide_Ingots_Copper_Production_and_the_Mediterranean_Trade_in_Copper_and_Other_Metals_in_the_Bronze_Age_MA_Thesis_Department_of_Anthropology_Texas_A_and_M_University_2007).

7. **Missing required photograph — high confidence.**  
   The required **“The real thing”** section is absent. [Chapter 11’s image ledger](chapters/11-bronze/images/ledger.md) already records `uluburun_oxhide_ingot.jpg`, Zunkir, CC BY-SA 4.0.

   **Chapter check:** “One man carries a slab on his shoulder” differs from the chapter’s “handles so that two men could carry it,” but these are compatible methods, not a contradiction. The remaining numbered labels match their steps.
