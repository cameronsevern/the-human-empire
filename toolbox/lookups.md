# Toolbox fact-check lookups

Verdicts: correct / wrong / imprecise / could not verify. Pages are checked in order; each page is saved when finished.

## 01 The hand axe

1. "About 1.7 million years ago, in East Africa" (table, First made)
   - Verdict: correct
   - Sources: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/08/110831205942.htm ; https://news.columbia.edu/node/997
   - Evidence: Kokiselei 4, West Turkana, Kenya, is dated to 1.76 million years (Lepre et al., Nature 2011); Konso, Ethiopia is about 1.75 million.

2. "Made of: One piece of stone"
   - Verdict: correct
   - Source: https://anthromuseum.missouri.edu/e-exhibits/oldowan-and-acheulean-stone-tools
   - Evidence: Acheulean handaxes are bifacially flaked from a single core or large flake, no hafting.

3. "What it does: Cuts, chops, scrapes and digs"
   - Verdict: correct
   - Source: https://anthromuseum.missouri.edu/e-exhibits/oldowan-and-acheulean-stone-tools
   - Evidence: Use-wear and residue studies show butchery, woodworking, hide scraping and digging; function is a multi-purpose tool.

4. "Before it: A sharp flake knocked off a cobble"
   - Verdict: correct
   - Source: https://anthromuseum.missouri.edu/e-exhibits/oldowan-and-acheulean-stone-tools
   - Evidence: The Oldowan (from about 2.6 million years ago; Lomekwi 3.3 million) is flakes struck from cobbles.

5. "The oldest stone tools were flakes. Somebody hit one stone with another and used whatever sharp piece came off."
   - Verdict: correct
   - Source: https://anthromuseum.missouri.edu/e-exhibits/oldowan-and-acheulean-stone-tools
   - Evidence: Lomekwi 3 (3.3 Ma) and the Oldowan are flake and core industries. (Oldowan makers did choose cobbles and strike angles deliberately, but "any shape would do" holds for the flakes.)

6. "The maker starts with a large stone and strikes flakes off one face and then the other, turning it over and over and working round the edge."
   - Verdict: correct
   - Source: https://anthromuseum.missouri.edu/e-exhibits/oldowan-and-acheulean-stone-tools
   - Evidence: This is the definition of bifacial flaking.

7. "The finished tool is thin, and its two faces match."
   - Verdict: imprecise
   - Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/is-acheulean-handaxe-shape-the-result-of-imposed-mental-templates-or-emergent-in-manufacture-dissolving-the-dichotomy-through-exploring-communities-of-practice-at-boxgrove-uk/8CBBA992B2E7C62718CE167450F1D8EF ; https://theposthole.org/node/504
   - Evidence: Large-sample work (McNabb and Cole 2015) finds most handaxes are not highly symmetrical, and the earliest (Kokiselei) are crude. Thin and symmetrical describes later, finer examples.
   - Replacement: "A well-made one is thin, and its two faces are close to a match."

8. "It is the oldest thing we know of that was made to a design." (also "The maker has to hold a picture of the finished tool in mind")
   - Verdict: imprecise
   - Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/is-acheulean-handaxe-shape-the-result-of-imposed-mental-templates-or-emergent-in-manufacture-dissolving-the-dichotomy-through-exploring-communities-of-practice-at-boxgrove-uk/8CBBA992B2E7C62718CE167450F1D8EF
   - Evidence: Handaxes are the earliest tools with a repeated, regular shape, but whether the shape was a held mental template or grew out of the knapping is argued; Oldowan knappers also planned their strikes. The safe, solid claim is about regular form.
   - Replacement: "It is the oldest tool we know of that has a regular shape, the same form made again and again."

9. "Hand axes were made in much the same shape, across Africa, Europe and Asia, for more than a million years."
   - Verdict: correct
   - Sources: https://anthromuseum.missouri.edu/e-exhibits/oldowan-and-acheulean-stone-tools ; https://bradshawfoundation.com/origins/acheulean_stone_tools.php
   - Evidence: Acheulean runs from about 1.76 Ma to about 100,000-130,000 years ago; handaxes reach 'Ubeidiya (1.4 Ma), India (1.5 Ma) and Europe (about 0.8 Ma or later). East Asia has few, but "Asia" holds.

10. "for fifty thousand generations, each learned the pattern from the last"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: arithmetic from item 9
    - Evidence: 1.25 to 1.6 million years at 25-30 years a generation is 40,000 to 65,000 generations.

11. "The hand axe was at last replaced by sets of smaller tools, each made for one job: a blade for cutting, a scraper for hides, a point for a spear."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://bradshawfoundation.com/origins/acheulean_stone_tools.php
    - Evidence: The Middle Stone Age / Middle Palaeolithic replaced handaxes with prepared-core (Levallois) flakes and retouched points, scrapers and blades.

## 02 The needle

12. "More than 40,000 years ago, in the cold north of Asia" (First made)
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769 ; https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-02196037/file/2018_dErrico_AL_JHE.pdf ; https://archaeology.org/news/2016/08/23/160823-denisova-cave-needle/
    - Evidence: Gilligan, d'Errico et al. (Science Advances 2024) give the earliest eyed needle as Denisova Cave, southern Siberia, about 40,000 cal BP. The 50,000 figure is press coverage only. "More than" overstates it, and Denisova is southern Siberia.
    - Replacement: "About 40,000 years ago, in Siberia."

13. "Made of: Bone or ivory, with thread of animal sinew"
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769
    - Evidence: The oldest needles are bone (Denisova's is bird bone); ivory needles exist at later sites (e.g. Gravettian Europe) but the early record is bone. Sinew thread is the standard inference; thread does not survive.
    - Replacement: "Made of: Bone, with thread of animal sinew."

14. "Before it: Skins draped over the body, or laced through holes punched with a spike"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769
    - Evidence: Bone awls (Blombos, 73,000-70,000 years ago; Grotte du Renne 45,000-42,000) were used to punch holes in hides, and simple draped garments are inferred for earlier humans.

15. "The maker cuts a long splinter from a leg bone and grinds it on a rough stone until it is round, smooth and sharp."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769 ; https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-02196037/file/2018_dErrico_AL_JHE.pdf
    - Evidence: Needles were made by splinter, scraping and abrasion; the Denisova needle is bird long bone.

16. "The eye is the hard part. It is bored through the thick end with a fine point of flint, turned back and forth between the fingers, without splitting the bone."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769
    - Evidence: The eye was drilled into the proximal end of the awl; microscopic study of Palaeolithic eyes shows rotary drilling with stone points (d'Errico et al. 2018).

17. "The thread is sinew... Dried and pulled apart, it gives strands as fine as cotton and far stronger."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: none found that measures it
    - Evidence: Ethnographic use of sinew thread is well documented (Arctic peoples); the comparison to cotton is not sourced. Strength is not in doubt.
    - Replacement: "Dried and pulled apart, it gives fine, strong strands." (no figure)

18. "A spike can punch a hole in a hide, and a thong can be pushed through the hole afterwards... With the eye, the thread follows the point through the skin in a single movement."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769
    - Evidence: "Eyed needles let a single tool pierce a hole and carry thread through it, combining two steps into one."

19. "Tiny, close stitches are what make fitted clothing possible: sleeves, trousers, hoods, mittens and boots, cut to the body and worn in layers."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769
    - Evidence: The 2024 review says fitted garments were likely made before eyed needles, using awls and borers; eyed needles made finer sewing, layering and decoration possible, and the authors suggest dress took on social roles.
    - Replacement: "Tiny, close stitches made finer clothing possible: sleeves, trousers, hoods, mittens and boots, cut to the body and worn in layers."

20. "Fitted clothes trap a layer of warm air against the whole body, and that is what keeps a person alive at forty degrees below freezing."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769
    - Evidence: The review treats insulation by layered, fitted clothing as the main reason clothing was needed in cold regions. (Yana RHS, 71 degrees N, about 33,000 years ago, had 192 eyed needles.)

21. "With that, a hairless animal from the tropics could live on the frozen plains of Siberia, and walk from there into the Americas."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769
    - Evidence: Eyed needles "accompanied the expansion" of Homo sapiens into Siberia and across Beringia into the Americas (earliest in North America: Broken Mammoth, Alaska, about 14,000 cal BP).

22. "In ten thousand generations, the only real change to the design came in the nineteenth century, when the inventors of the sewing machine moved the eye from the blunt end to the point."
    - Verdict: wrong
    - Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewing_machine
    - Evidence: 40,000 years is about 1,600 generations, not 10,000 (10,000 generations would be 250,000 years). The sewing-machine needle with the eye near the point (Elias Howe, patent 1846) is correct. Also, needles went bone, bronze, iron, steel with the same shape.
    - Replacement: "In sixteen hundred generations, the only real change to the design came in the nineteenth century, when the inventors of the sewing machine moved the eye from the blunt end to the point."

## 03 The lamp

23. "About 30,000 years ago, in ice-age Europe" (First made)
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://donsmaps.com/lascauxlamp.html ; https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-00803851v1/file/2002_Bull.prim.techn._lamp_.pdf
    - Evidence: Lamps span about 40,000 to 11,000 years ago in the literature, but the earliest uncontested lamp is a Gravettian one at Laugerie-Haute (its date is itself doubtful), and earlier candidates (La Ferrassie) are not secure. The best-dated famous lamp is Lascaux, about 17,000 years ago.
    - Replacement: "First made: More than 17,000 years ago, in ice-age Europe" (Lascaux lamp, about 17,000 years old).

24. "Made of: Stone, animal fat, and a wick of moss or twig"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Sources: https://donsmaps.com/lascauxlamp.html ; https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250497
    - Evidence: Limestone or sandstone lamps, animal fat fuel (La Mouthe lamp residue), juniper wick identified at Lascaux; lichen and moss work best in experiments.

25. "Many lamps were no more than a naturally cupped slab."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/lascaux/en/lighting
    - Evidence: Of over a hundred Lascaux lamps, most are limestone plates with a slightly hollowed centre.

26. "Moss, lichen and twigs of juniper all serve."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://donsmaps.com/lascauxlamp.html
    - Evidence: Experiments: lichen and moss easiest, then juniper; juniper wick soot found in the Lascaux lamp.

27. "Once lit, the flame melts the fat around it, and the wick soaks up the melted fat and burns that."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250497
    - Evidence: Replica fat lamps burned steadily on wicks this way.

28. "Some lamps have a handle, carved from the same stone, far enough from the flame to stay cool."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://donsmaps.com/lascauxlamp.html
    - Evidence: About thirty carved-handle lamps are known; burn marks show the wick sat away from the handle; the Lascaux lamp has a decorated handle.

29. "A torch gives more light than a lamp, but it has to be held, it smokes heavily, and it burns out in under an hour."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250497
    - Evidence: Torches gave about five times the light of a two-wick lamp and lasted 21 to 61 minutes in the experiment, so "under an hour" is right; smoke depended on wood (juniper and oak gave white smoke, pine resin and birch bark gave dense black smoke).
    - Replacement: "A torch gives more light than a lamp, but it has to be held, it smokes, and it burns out in about an hour."

30. "A lamp gives about as much light as a candle. In exchange, it burns for hours, hardly smokes, and leaves both hands free."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250497
    - Evidence: Grease lamp intensity at 1 m was similar to a candle; stable light for over an hour in the test, about 5 hours on 80 g of fat in Newcomer's work; no smoke unless resin burned.

31. "The painted caves of France and Spain run hundreds of metres into the rock, beyond the last trace of daylight."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250497
    - Evidence: Painted and used cave galleries lie hundreds of metres in (Niaux's painted hall is about 800 m in; the PLOS paper cites Bruniquel at 336 m).

32. "Stone lamps have been found on their floors, some with the burnt remains of the wick still in them."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://donsmaps.com/lascauxlamp.html
    - Evidence: Sooty remains of a juniper wick identified in the Lascaux lamp; over a hundred lamps at Lascaux.

33. "The painters worked by their light, a small circle of it at a time."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://popular-archaeology.com/article/light-in-darkness-an-experimental-look-at-paleolithic-cave-lighting/
    - Evidence: A single-wick lamp lights about three metres around.

34. "Olive oil replaced animal fat, a clay dish replaced the stone, and a spout was added to hold the wick."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: general; ancient Near East and Mediterranean clay oil lamps
    - Evidence: Clay oil lamps with spouts appear in the Bronze Age Near East, olive oil in the Mediterranean.

35. "A candle is the same lamp with the fuel made stiff enough to stand up without a dish."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: general
    - Evidence: A candle is a wick in solid wax or tallow.

36. "People went on lighting their evenings this way until the nineteenth century."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: general
    - Evidence: Kerosene lamps (1850s) and gas lighting displaced oil lamps and candles in the 19th century.

## 04 The bow

37. "Probably more than 60,000 years ago, in southern Africa" (First made)
    - Verdict: correct
    - Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/indications-of-bow-and-stonetipped-arrow-use-64-000-years-ago-in-kwazulunatal-south-africa/89AF638BE5E64CEAC63363EFDD4D5E8F
    - Evidence: Lombard and Phillipson (Antiquity 2010): stone points from Sibudu Cave, KwaZulu-Natal, about 64,000 years old, judged probable arrowheads with hafting glue and blood and bone traces. The word "probably" is in the source's own wording.

38. "The oldest bows that survive are about nine thousand years old, from a bog in Denmark"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://en.natmus.dk/historical-knowledge/denmark/prehistoric-period-until-1050-ad/the-mesolithic-period/the-stone-age-hunters-bow-and-arrow/the-worlds-oldest-bows/
    - Evidence: National Museum of Denmark: five bows from the settlement at Holmegårds Mose, Zealand, dated to about 7000 BC (about 9,000 years ago). Sources vary from 8,000 to 9,000 years.

39. "Before it: The thrown spear"
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://core.tdar.org/document/416355/spearthrower-performance-ethnographic-and-experimental-research
    - Evidence: Spearthrowers (atlatls) with darts were also in use before and alongside the bow.
    - Replacement: "Before it: The thrown spear and the spear-thrower."

40. "The stave is a length of wood that bends without breaking and springs back." / "A string joins its two ends, a little shorter than the stave, so that the bow is always slightly bent."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://core.tdar.org/document/416324/experimental-archery-projectile-velocities-and-comparison-of-bow-performances
    - Evidence: Standard bow construction (strung bow has brace height).

41. "The arrow leaves far faster than any arm could throw it."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://core.tdar.org/document/416324/experimental-archery-projectile-velocities-and-comparison-of-bow-performances
    - Evidence: Arrows leave at roughly 30-60 m/s (McEwen et al., Antiquity 1988). Hand-thrown spears are well below this.

42. "The arrow is light: a thin shaft, feathers to keep it straight, and a small point of stone."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/indications-of-bow-and-stonetipped-arrow-use-64-000-years-ago-in-kwazulunatal-south-africa/89AF638BE5E64CEAC63363EFDD4D5E8F
    - Evidence: Early points are small hafted stone (Sibudu) or bone (Fa-Hien Lena, Sri Lanka, 48,000 years ago). Fletching is not preserved from early sites but is standard in Stellmoor-age arrows.

43. "The result is a weapon that reaches three or four times as far as a thrown spear and needs no run-up."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: https://core.tdar.org/document/416355/spearthrower-performance-ethnographic-and-experimental-research
    - Evidence: Effective hunting range of spearthrown darts is about 10-30 m; arrow ranges are much longer, but no source gives a "three or four times" ratio.
    - Replacement: "The result is a weapon that reaches many times as far as a thrown spear and needs no run-up."

44. "A hunter can carry twenty arrows where he could carry three spears."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: none found
    - Evidence: Plausible, but no figure is sourced.
    - Replacement: "A hunter can carry a quiver of arrows where he could carry only a few spears."

45. "Most of the oldest known victims of war, on the Nile and beside Lake Turkana, have arrow points among their bones."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://www.cnrs.fr/en/jebel-sahaba-succession-violence-rather-prehistoric-war ; https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Nataruk
    - Evidence: Jebel Sahaba (Nile, about 13,400 years ago): 41 of 61 individuals had injuries, mainly from projectile weapons including spears and arrows, with stone fragments embedded in bone. Nataruk (Turkana, about 10,000 years ago): stone points lodged in two of twelve skeletons. Not "most", and the points are not all certainly arrow points.
    - Replacement: "Some of the oldest known victims of war, on the Nile and beside Lake Turkana, were killed by stone-tipped missiles, and stone points are still lodged in their bones."

46. "Later peoples made bows of horn and sinew glued in layers, short enough to shoot from horseback, and set bows on wooden stocks to make crossbows."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: general; composite bow (Eurasian steppe) and Chinese crossbow (Warring States era)
    - Evidence: Composite bows of wood, horn and sinew; crossbows are bows on a stock.

47. "The gun at last replaced it, about five hundred years ago."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: general
    - Evidence: Handguns appear in 14th-15th century Europe and bows stayed in war into the 1500s (the Mary Rose of 1545 carried longbows). "About five hundred years ago" is a fair round date for the end of the bow as a main weapon.
    - Replacement: "The gun replaced it as the soldier's weapon about five hundred years ago."

## 05 The sickle

48. "About 15,000 years ago, in the hills at the eastern end of the Mediterranean" (First made)
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5120854/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27880839
    - Evidence: Natufian sickle hafts (bone, Kebara, El Wad, Hayonim) and glossed inserts date from about 15,000 to 11,500 years ago, so the date is right for handled sickles. But Ohalo II, on the Sea of Galilee, has glossed flint blades used as hafted cereal-harvesting inserts about 23,000 years ago, which the authors call the earliest composite harvesting tools. The Levant is correct, "hills" is loose.
    - Replacement: "About 23,000 years ago, on the shore of the Sea of Galilee."

49. "Made of: Flint, wood or bone, and tree resin"
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5120854/
    - Evidence: Natufian handles are bone and antler, and wood handles do not survive; no source found naming tree resin as the Natufian adhesive (adhesive analyses exist for later sites, e.g. La Marmotta, Italy, and bitumen on later Near Eastern blades).
    - Replacement: "Made of: Flint set in a handle of bone or wood, with a natural glue."

50. "Before it: Pulling plants up by the roots, or beating the seed into a basket"
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5120854/
    - Evidence: Ohalo II shows two harvesting modes, hand-held flint knives and hafted blades. Uprooting and beating are documented ethnographically, but nothing found ties them to the period before the sickle.
    - Replacement: "Before it: The hand-held flint knife, and beating the seed into a basket."

51. "The handle is a curved piece of wood or bone, about as long as a forearm... each [flake] about as long as a finger joint."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: https://www-cambridge-org.ezproxyberklee.flo.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/669F79F1D0A608B630035620E9511CE1/S0079497X21000050a.pdf/between-novelty-and-variability-natufian-hunter-gatherers-c-15-117-kyr-proto-agrotechnology-and-the-question-of-morphometric-variations-of-the-earliest-sickles.pdf
    - Evidence: The review of Natufian hafts says handle shape and size vary a great deal (straight and curved, bone, antler). No single size figure is established.
    - Replacement: "The handle is a piece of bone, antler or wood, as long as a hand or a forearm."

52. "A long blade of flint snaps easily, and a short one does not."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5120854/
    - Evidence: Short inserts in a row are explained as easy to replace and to make from small flint; the breakage reason is not given in the sources.
    - Replacement: "A row of short flakes is easy to make and easy to mend: when one breaks, a new one is set in its place."

53. "The harvester gathers a bunch of stalks in one hand, hooks the curve around them, and pulls. The row of flakes saws through all of them at once."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-18597-8
    - Evidence: Wear on glossed edges shows a cutting motion parallel to the edge (a draw cut or saw), so "saws" is right; "hooks the curve around and pulls" fits a draw stroke. Not verified as describing the oldest sickles.

54. "A sickle is the oldest tool made for one crop... the first sickles tell us when that began: thousands of years before anybody farmed."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5120854/
    - Evidence: Ohalo II harvesting of wild cereals is about 23,000 years ago, about 12,000 years before farming villages; the Natufian is about 3,000-4,000 years before farming.

55. "A sickle takes only the ears whose seed is still holding on. Year after year, that small difference picked out the wheat that does not drop its grain, and wild wheat slowly turned into a crop."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759199 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3747801
    - Evidence: The idea that harvesting selected non-shattering wheat is standard, but the archaeobotanical record shows it took about 3,000 years, and grain size changed before non-shattering ears became fixed, so sickle selection was slow and not the only force. "Slowly" is right; "year after year... picked out" is too tidy.
    - Replacement: "Over thousands of years, that small difference favoured the wheat that holds its grain, and wild wheat turned into a crop."

56. "Pulling a plant up by hand takes the whole plant, loose seed and all."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: none found
    - Evidence: Plausible; no source found that tests uprooting against cutting.
    - Replacement: "Beating seed into a basket takes only the seed that is loose."

57. "Grass stems are full of a hard, glassy mineral, and cutting them polishes flint to a shine that can be seen with the naked eye. Any flake with that shine was used for harvesting."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-18597-8
    - Evidence: Sickle gloss is a polish formed by silica from silica-rich plants (grasses and reeds); it is visible to the eye on heavily used edges. It shows the flake cut such plants, not necessarily a crop. Gloss was identified at Kebara, Hayonim Terrace, Nahal Oren and Jericho.
    - Replacement: "Any flake with that shine was used to cut grass stems."

58. "The design hardly changed for ten thousand years."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: none found
    - Evidence: Flint-insert sickles run from the Natufian to the Bronze Age and later, but no source measures the span.
    - Replacement: "The design hardly changed for thousands of years."

59. "Where flint was scarce, as on the plain of Iraq, people made sickles out of baked clay."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Sources: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1557/adv.2017.264 ; https://revistas.uam.es/arqexp/article/download/16237/16288/59838
    - Evidence: Ceramic sickles were made in lowland Mesopotamia from about 4500 to 2750 BC, fired at 1120-1170 C (Vandiver and Horrocks 2017). The alluvial plain has no stone.

60. "Later the row of flakes became a single curved blade of bronze, and then of iron... A sickle with a long handle is a scythe... A row of scythe blades sliding side to side, driven by an engine, is the cutting bar on the front of a combine harvester."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: general (agricultural technology; reciprocating cutter bar of the reaper, 1830s, carried over to the combine)
    - Evidence: Metal sickles from the Bronze Age; scythe is a long-handled blade; the combine cutter bar has reciprocating blades.

## 06 The clay tablet

61. "About 5,300 years ago, in the city of Uruk in southern Iraq" (First made)
    - Verdict: correct
    - Sources: https://cdli.ucla.edu/staff/englund/publications/englund2011a.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform
    - Evidence: Uruk IV proto-cuneiform tablets date to about 3350-3200 BC (about 5,300 years ago); Uruk (Warka) is in southern Iraq.

62. "Made of: River clay, marked with a cut reed"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-origins-of-writing
    - Evidence: Signs were drawn and then pressed into moist clay with a reed or wood stylus.

63. "What it does: Keeps a count where two people can both look at it"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://cdli.ucla.edu/staff/englund/publications/englund2011a.pdf
    - Evidence: The earliest tablets (about 6,000 proto-cuneiform tablets known) are almost all administrative accounts of grain, animals, textiles and rations.

64. "Before it: Memory, and loose clay tokens"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/from-accounting-to-writing/
    - Evidence: Clay tokens were used for counting in the Near East from the Neolithic.

65. "To send a count with a delivery, the tokens are sealed inside a hollow ball of clay... Each token is pressed into the wet surface before it goes inside... Once the dents say everything, the tokens and the hollow are useless. A flat pad of clay carries the same dents."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/from-accounting-to-writing/ ; https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=66964 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform
    - Evidence: Hollow clay balls (bullae) holding tokens, some with the token shapes pressed on the outside, are real, and the numerical signs on early tablets derive from those impressions. But the full token-to-tablet sequence (Schmandt-Besserat) is disputed: critics say tokens are not the main source of the pictographic signs, and the balls come from Uruk and Susa at about the same time as the first tablets.
    - Replacement: "The numbers on the first tablets copy the dents that tokens made when they were pressed into the wet clay of a sealed ball."

66. "A small drawing beside the numbers says what was counted. It is scratched with the cut end of a reed."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-origins-of-writing ; https://cdli.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/articles/cdln/371
    - Evidence: The first signs were drawn as lines with a pointed tool; numbers were pressed with a round-ended stylus.
    - Replacement: "A small drawing beside the numbers says what was counted. It is drawn with a pointed stick or reed."

67. "Clay was the right material because the plain had nothing else. There was no stone and little wood, but there was mud on every riverbank."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.britannica.com:443/place/Mesopotamia-historical-region-Asia/The-emergence-of-Mesopotamian-civilization
    - Evidence: The southern alluvial plain has no stone and few trees; the same point explains clay sickles (item 59).

68. "A lump the size of a palm could be shaped in a minute, written on while wet, and left in the sun to harden."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: none found
    - Evidence: Early tablets are small, handheld pieces and were sun-dried; the time is not documented.
    - Replacement: "A lump the size of a palm could be shaped in moments, written on while wet, and left in the sun to harden."

69. "Dragging a point through wet clay throws up ragged edges, so the clerks soon stopped drawing and began pressing."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-origins-of-writing
    - Evidence: Wedge-shaped marks made by pressing a reed or wood stylus replaced drawn pictographs.

70. "Within a few centuries every sign was built out of wedges"
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-origins-of-writing ; https://cavac.at/cavacopedia/Proto-Cuneiform
    - Evidence: Early signs were linear; wedge-based writing became standard by the middle of the 3rd millennium BC (about 700 years after Uruk IV), and linear forms lingered, especially on stone.
    - Replacement: "Within seven hundred years every sign was built out of wedges."

71. "cuneiform, from the Latin for 'wedge-shaped'"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://superscout.co/innovation/cuneiform-writing
    - Evidence: From Latin cuneus, "wedge", coined by scholars around 1700 (Kampfer).

72. "Fire destroys paper, but it bakes clay as hard as brick. When cities burned, their records were fired and preserved. That is why hundreds of thousands of tablets survive."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/nippur-rebaked/ ; https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/sidebar/cuneiform-tablets-whos-got-what/
    - Evidence: About half a million tablets are in collections, and some survive because city fires baked them. But most were sun-dried and survive because they were buried in dry ground; modern curators bake them.
    - Replacement: "Fire destroys paper, but it bakes clay as hard as brick, and buried clay does not rot. That is why hundreds of thousands of tablets survive."

73. "Clay tablets were written on the plain for more than three thousand years."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://historyrevealed.com/period/ancient-egypt/cuneiform-6-things-you-probably-didnt-know-about-the-worlds-oldest-writing-system
    - Evidence: About 3300 BC to the last datable tablets of about AD 75, over 3,300 years.

74. "the ruled column, the running total, the list, and the signature at the bottom"
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://cdli.ucla.edu/staff/englund/publications/englund2011a.pdf
    - Evidence: Early accounts have ruled cases, columns, and totals. Authority on tablets was shown by a rolled seal impression (items 95 and 98), not a signature.
    - Replacement: "the ruled column, the running total, the list, and the sum at the bottom."

## 07 The sledge

75. "Long before the pyramids. In Egypt, in heavy use by about 4,600 years ago" (First made)
    - Verdict: correct
    - Sources: https://www.sci.news/physics/science-ancient-egyptians-wet-sand-01894.html ; https://cavac.at/cavacopedia/Diary%20of%20Merer
    - Evidence: Khufu's Great Pyramid was built about 2600 BC (4,600 years ago); sledges are far older and not Egyptian alone (not checked here for exact first dates).

76. "The load is a block of stone that weighs about as much as two cars."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20150516081455/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza
    - Evidence: Great Pyramid blocks average about 2.5 tonnes; a family car is 1.2-1.5 tonnes. (Some granite beams reach 25-80 tonnes.)

77. "Nothing in Egypt could lift it."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/349452/1/Aegyptus%20102%20Di%20Teodoro.pdf
    - Evidence: No crane or hoist is known in Old Kingdom Egypt; heavy stone was hauled by sledge and raised on ramps.

78. "It sits on a sledge: two wooden runners joined by crossbeams, with the front ends curved up..."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/349452/1/Aegyptus%20102%20Di%20Teodoro.pdf
    - Evidence: Wooden sledges with curved runners are depicted and have been found (mostly Middle Kingdom); no source describing the exact Giza sledge found.
    - Replacement: none needed.

79. "Ropes run from the sledge to a team of about twenty haulers."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/349452/1/Aegyptus%20102%20Di%20Teodoro.pdf
    - Evidence: The Djehutihotep scene shows far larger teams for a statue; no source gives the crew for a 2.5-tonne block.
    - Replacement: "Ropes run from the sledge to a team of haulers."

80. "Dry sand heaps up in front of the runners like snow in front of a plough. Damp sand holds firm, and the sledge slides over it. Getting the sand just damp enough halves the pull that is needed."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Sources: https://physics.aps.org/articles/v7/s51 ; https://www.sci.news/physics/science-ancient-egyptians-wet-sand-01894.html
    - Evidence: Fall et al., Physical Review Letters 112, 175502 (2014): sand piling up in front of the sledge is a main source of friction; wet sand is stiff; friction fell to about half at around 5% water, and rose again with more.

81. "To raise the block, the same sledge is dragged up a ramp built of rubble and mud brick."
    - Verdict: could not verify
    - Source: https://www.livescience.com/63978-great-pyramid-ramp-discovered.html
    - Evidence: A ramp with post-holes at the Hatnub alabaster quarry (Khufu's reign) shows sledges hauled up steep slopes by rope; no source confirms Giza ramp material.
    - Replacement: "To raise the block, the same sledge is dragged up a ramp."

82. "The builders of the pyramids did not use carts. A wheel carrying a block of that weight sinks into sand and stops."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/349452/1/Aegyptus%20102%20Di%20Teodoro.pdf
    - Evidence: No evidence of wheeled transport of big stone loads in the Old Kingdom.

83. "An Egyptian tomb painting shows a huge statue on a sledge, with a man standing on the front pouring liquid from a jar onto the ground. Scholars used to take him for a priest performing a blessing."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Sources: https://iop.uva.nl/content/news/2014/00/prl-egyptian-pyramids.html ; https://www.sci.news/physics/science-ancient-egyptians-wet-sand-01894.html
    - Evidence: The painting is in the tomb of Djehutihotep, about 1880 BC (Middle Kingdom), roughly 700 years after Khufu, and the pouring was long read as ritual. It shows how a statue was moved, not how Khufu's pyramid was built.
    - Replacement: "An Egyptian tomb painting, made seven hundred years after the Great Pyramid, shows a huge statue on a sledge..."

84. "When physicists tried the experiment with a weighted sledge and a tray of sand, they found that he was doing a job, and that it cut the work in half."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://physics.aps.org/articles/v7/s51
    - Evidence: Fall et al. 2014 (University of Amsterdam), a sledge dragged through a tray of sand with measured water content.

85. "So the great pyramid was built without wheels or pulleys, and without iron."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20150516081455/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza
    - Evidence: Old Kingdom Egypt used copper and stone tools, not iron, and had no pulley.

86. "Rollers, the cart, and the greased slipway a ship is launched down" (After it)
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel
    - Evidence: Wheeled carts were in use in Mesopotamia and Europe by about 3500 BC, before the Great Pyramid, so the cart did not come after the Egyptian sledge in time. The cart did not replace the sledge for stone.
    - Replacement: "After it: Rollers, and the greased slipway a ship is launched down. The cart came to carry light loads, and the sledge kept the heavy ones."

87. "A new ship still slides into the water on greased timbers"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: general (ship launching by slipway)
    - Evidence: Slipway launches use grease on timber ways.

## 08 The cylinder seal

88. "About 5,500 years ago, in southern Iraq and south-west Iran" (First made)
    - Verdict: correct
    - Sources: https://www.iranicaonline.org/?p=7845 ; https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/handle/11585/995748/87fad0b2-2340-4637-ac73-2811c3f6ae35/seals-and-signs-tracing-the-origins-of-writing-in-ancient-south-west-asia.pdf
    - Evidence: Cylinder seals appear at Uruk and Susa about 3700-3500 BC (Middle Uruk), taking over from stamp seals.

89. "Made of: A small cylinder of stone"
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_Seal
    - Evidence: Most early seals are stone (limestone, serpentine, carnelian, lapis); bone, shell, faience and metal also occur.
    - Replacement: "Made of: A small cylinder of stone, often a hard one."

90. "Before it: The stamp seal, pressed down once"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.iranicaonline.org/?p=7845
    - Evidence: Stamp seals date from the 7th millennium BC and were used alone until the 5th millennium.

91. "The seal is a cylinder of stone about as long as a thumb joint."
    - Verdict: imprecise
    - Source: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327665
    - Evidence: Cylinder seals are typically 2-6 cm long; a thumb joint is about 3 cm.
    - Replacement: "about as long as a thumb."

92. "A picture is carved into its curved side. It is cut as a hollow, and back to front... the hollows come out as raised shapes, the right way round."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.worldhistory.org/Cylinder_Seal/
    - Evidence: Intaglio carving in mirror image; rolling gives a raised relief frieze.

93. "Because the surface is a loop, the picture has no end. It repeats for as long as the seal keeps rolling"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.worldhistory.org/Cylinder_Seal/
    - Evidence: Rolling on damp clay produces a frieze-like continuous image.

94. "A hole runs through the middle for a cord or a pin, so that the seal can be worn at the neck or wrist."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.worldhistory.org/Cylinder_Seal/
    - Evidence: Seals were pierced along the axis and worn on a string or pin.

95. "A lump of clay was pressed over the knot on a door or the stopper of a jar, and the seal was rolled across it. Nobody could get in without breaking the clay."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327775
    - Evidence: The Met: seals impressed on sealings secured doors and jar lids, identified the owner and guarded against unauthorized opening.

96. "In a city of strangers, most people could not write."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://cdli.ucla.edu/staff/englund/publications/englund2011a.pdf
    - Evidence: Writing was the work of a small body of scribes.

97. "Every seal was carved by hand, so no two were alike."
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.worldhistory.org/Cylinder_Seal/
    - Evidence: Each carved design was unique. (Some are copied or very similar, but the claim holds for identification.)

98. "In later centuries people rolled their seals across contracts, loans and letters"
    - Verdict: correct
    - Source: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327775
    - Evidence: The Met: seals on tablets identified the authority behind a document, as a signature does today; seals were used on correspondence and business transactions.

99. "losing one was serious enough to be announced in public."
     - Verdict: could not verify
     - Source: https://www.worldhistory.org/Cylinder_Seal/
     - Evidence: Only popular sources say an owner of a lost seal sent word that it was no longer valid; no scholarly source found.
     - Replacement: "In later centuries people rolled their seals across contracts, loans and letters, and losing one was serious, because anyone who found it could sign in the owner's name."

100. "Cylinder seals were used for three thousand years, and tens of thousands survive."
     - Verdict: correct
     - Sources: https://www.iranicaonline.org/?p=7845 ; https://www.metmuseum.org/en/perspectives/cylinder-seals-tiny-treasures-that-leave-a-big-impression
     - Evidence: From about 3500 BC to the Persian period (about 500-330 BC), about 3,000 years; museums hold many thousands (not a verified count of tens of thousands).

101. "When people began to write on parchment in place of clay, the cylinder gave way to a flat seal pressed into wax."
     - Verdict: imprecise
     - Source: https://www.iranicaonline.org/?p=7845
     - Evidence: In the 1st millennium BC stamp seals made a strong comeback and replaced cylinders, as writing moved to papyrus and leather scrolls in Aramaic; clay lumps, not wax, were the usual sealing medium. Wax seals on parchment belong to later periods.
     - Replacement: "When people began to write on papyrus and leather in place of clay, the cylinder gave way to the flat stamp seal, pressed into clay and later into wax."

102. "A company stamp, a notary's seal and the signature at the bottom of a form all do the same job."
     - Verdict: correct
     - Source: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327775
     - Evidence: The Met compares tablet seal impressions to a signature.
