# Lookups: the coin die

1. Lower die set in an anvil, blank laid on it, upper die struck with a hammer; dies of bronze or iron.
   - Verdict: correct. https://www.britannica.com/money/coin/Ancient-minting
2. "One lower die was good for more than ten thousand coins."
   - Verdict: correct as a floor. Estimates run from about 15,000 (modern experiment) to 20,000 and more; for one Delphi issue 23,000 to 47,000. https://www2.lawrence.edu/dept/art/BUERGER/ESSAYS/PRODUCTION6.HTML ; https://coinweek.com/coinweek-ancient-coins-series-ancient-coins-made/
3. "The upper die ... wore out about twice as fast as the lower one."
   - Verdict: correct. The upper (reverse) die took the blows and lasted about half as long. Same sources.
4. Die pairings used to put coins in order (die studies).
   - Verdict: correct; standard numismatic method, also in the chapter's lookups (Robinson 1951).
5. "An engraver spent days cutting": not verified. Cut.
6. Screw press in the sixteenth century, steam in the eighteenth.
   - Verdict: correct. Screw presses in mints from about 1550; Boulton's steam presses at Soho from 1788. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coining_(mint) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho_Mint
7. "A modern press strikes several hundred coins a minute."
   - Verdict: correct. The US Mint gives up to 750 a minute; not refetched in this pass. https://www.usmint.gov/learn/production-process/coin-production
8. Earliest upper die a plain punch, engraved within a few generations: as in the chapter.
9. Photo: PAS KENT-2EEAF0, upper die for Gallo-Belgic A gold staters, late 2nd century BCE, 29.5 mm high. The PAS record says the die made these staters "or ancient imitations of this coinage". https://finds.org.uk/database/artefacts/record/id/577854
10. Card "Silver weighed out on scales at every sale". Codex: wrong as an absolute; payments were also made in barley, wool, labour and on credit, as the chapter says. Reworded.
11. "So the coin's value is fixed before it is struck." Codex: weighing fixes weight, not value. Reworded; electrum added.
12. "Once and hard." Codex: doubtful; Sellwood's striking experiments needed two blows for deep designs, and Britannica says "one or more". Text now allows a second blow. Label 4 in the picture still reads "One hard blow of the hammer". https://www.britnumsoc.org/publications/Digital%20BNJ/pdfs/1962_BNJ_31_10.pdf
