Verdicts: correct / wrong / imprecise / could not verify. Replacements are written to drop in as they are.

## 12 The siege ram

| # | Claim | Verdict | Source | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "First made: In use in Assyria by about 2,850 years ago" | imprecise | https://www.worldhistory.org/image/3034 ; https://raco.cat/index.php/Historiae/article/download/286613/374826/ | Wheeled rams first appear in the reliefs of Ashurnasirpal II (865 to 860 BCE, about 2,880 years ago), so the Assyrian date holds. But "first made" is wrong for rams in general: Beni Hasan (c. 1900 BCE), Mari tablets (18th c. BCE) and Buhen (17th c. BCE) show earlier, simple rams. |
| 2 | "Made of: A timber frame on wheels, with a long beam" | correct | https://www.worldhistory.org/image/3034 | Nimrud relief shows a wheeled frame (four- or six-wheeled) with a lever-swung ram; the Lachish reliefs show the same type. |
| 3 | "What it does: Breaks a hole in a city wall" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battering_ram | Standard description. |
| 4 | "Before it: Ladders, tunnels, and waiting for hunger" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege | Escalade, mining and blockade were the older methods. Simple hand-held rams also came earlier (see 1). |
| 5 | "After it: The siege tower, the catapult and the cannon" | imprecise | https://www.worldhistory.org/image/3034 | Siege towers are not later: they appear beside rams in Assyrian reliefs and earlier at Mari. The catapult (Syracuse, about 399 BCE) and cannon do come after. |
| 6 | "The carvings of the siege of Lachish show the machines rolling up ramps with their beams against the brickwork" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lachish_reliefs | The Nineveh reliefs show engines on the ramp against the walls of Lachish (701 BCE). |
| 7 | "the defenders throw down torches and an Assyrian soldier pours water over the front" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lachish_reliefs | Reliefs show defenders hurling burning torches and Assyrians ladling water onto the engines. |
| 8 | "At Lachish the ramp alone took about three million stones" | correct | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11090153/ | Garfinkel and colleagues (2024) model the ramp at about 3 million stones, roughly 20,000 tons; Ussishkin's earlier figure was 13,000 to 19,000 tons. It is an estimate, not a count. |
| 9 | "A wall of mud brick stops an army of spearmen" | could not verify | n/a | General statement; Lachish's upper wall was mudbrick on a stone base (Ussishkin). Not checkable as a number. |

Replacement for 1: "Simple rams about 3,800 years ago; the wheeled ram of Assyria by about 2,880 years ago".
Replacement for 5: "The catapult, the torsion engine and the cannon".

## 18 The trireme

| # | Claim | Verdict | Source | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | "First made: About 2,600 years ago, in the eastern Mediterranean" | imprecise | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme | Thucydides has Corinth building triremes in the late 8th century BCE; Clement credits Sidon; the first firmly dated use is about 525 BCE (Polycrates of Samos, 40 ships). Origin is disputed between Greece and Phoenicia, so "eastern Mediterranean" is safe but the age is too young by a century or more. |
| 11 | "Made of: Light timber, with a beak sheathed in bronze" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme | Fir, pine and oak hull; bronze-sheathed ram (the Athlit ram is bronze over a timber core). |
| 12 | "What it does: Turns 170 rowers into a single weapon" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme | 170 rowers: 62 thranites, 54 zygites, 54 thalamites; about 200 crew in all. |
| 13 | "Before it: Warships with one or two rows of oars" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme | Penteconter (one row, 25 oars a side) and the Phoenician bireme (two rows). |
| 14 | "After it: Larger galleys, with several men to each oar" | imprecise | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinquereme | Quadriremes and quinqueremes began at Syracuse about 399 BCE under Dionysius I, while triremes were still in service, and took over in the Hellenistic period. "Several men to each oar" fits some of the bigger ones but is only one reading of the "fours" and "fives". |
| 15 | "in a sprint it moved about as fast as a man can run" | imprecise | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympias_(trireme) | Olympias trials reached 8 to 9 knots (about 4.5 m/s), roughly a distance runner's pace, well short of a sprinter. |
| 16 | "all hundred and seventy men pulled as one, which took months of practice" | could not verify | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme | Crew skill and long training are stated by ancient sources, but "months" is not given as a figure. |
| 17 | "A man needed money to fight on land, since he had to buy his own armour" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoplite | Hoplites supplied their own panoply. |
| 18 | "The fleet of Athens took more than thirty thousand men to pull it" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme | 200 ships (483/2 BCE; about 300 seaworthy in 431) at 170 rowers is 34,000 or more. |
| 19 | "far more than the city had armoured soldiers" | wrong | https://fgh.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0003/tlg001/1st1K-eng1/2.13.5-2.15.2 | Thucydides 2.13: 13,000 field hoplites plus 16,000 in garrison, 29,000 in all. Comparable, not far fewer. |
| 20 | "the safety of the city came to rest on its poorest citizens" | imprecise | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme | The thetes rowed, but crews also had metics, hired foreigners and some slaves; a typical Peloponnesian War crew was about 80 citizens, 60 metics and 60 foreign hands. |

Replacement for 10: "About 2,700 years ago, in Greece or Phoenicia".
Replacement for 14: "Larger galleys, the fours and fives, with more men to the oars".
Replacement for 15: "in a sprint it moved at about the pace of a distance runner".
Replacement for 19: "about as many as the city had armoured soldiers".
Replacement for 20: "the safety of the city came to rest on its poor citizens and the foreigners who rowed beside them".

## 19 The long pike

| # | Claim | Verdict | Source | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21 | "First made: About 2,380 years ago, in Macedon" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa | Introduced under Philip II (reigned 359 to 336 BCE); 2,380 years back is 356 BCE, inside his reign. |
| 22 | "Made of: A wooden shaft with an iron point" | imprecise | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa | Shaft of cornel or ash; iron head about 13.5 cm and often a metal butt-spike. The butt is why the weapon balanced. |
| 23 | "Puts five spear points in front of every man in the front rank" | imprecise | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa | Five ranks of pikes projected beyond the front rank, so the front man faced five points only on a narrow line ahead; rear ranks held theirs angled upward. |
| 24 | "Before it: The spear a little taller than a man" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dory_(spear) | The hoplite dory was about 2 to 2.4 m. |
| 25 | "After it: The pike blocks of later European armies" | imprecise | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa | Swiss, Landsknecht and later pike blocks are a fair comparison (Machiavelli drew it), but they came about 1,700 years later; the Hellenistic phalanxes carried it until Pydna, 168 BCE. |
| 26 | "The Macedonian pike was more than five metres long" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa | 5 to 7 m; Connolly's reconstruction 5.8 m, later ones about 6.3 m. |
| 27 | "took both hands, so the shield shrank and hung from a strap" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_phalanx | Two-handed; small shield (about 60 cm) suspended from the neck. |
| 28 | "In a block sixteen rows deep" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_phalanx | The syntagma was 16 by 16. |
| 29 | "It worked because Philip paired it with heavy cavalry... anvil... hammer" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_phalanx | Phalanx pinned, Companion cavalry struck; the standard account of Philip and Alexander. |

Replacement for 22: "A cornel-wood shaft with an iron head and an iron butt".
Replacement for 23: "Puts five spear points ahead of the front rank of the block".
Replacement for 25: "The Hellenistic pike phalanx, and 1,700 years later the pike blocks of Swiss and other European armies".

## 21 The crossbow

| # | Claim | Verdict | Source | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | "First made: More than 2,400 years ago, in China" | imprecise | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_crossbows | True but low: cast bronze locks from Qufu tombs (Lu state) date to the 6th century BCE, and bronze bolts at Yutaishan to the mid-5th. About 2,500 years is safe. |
| 31 | "Made of: A wooden stock, a short bow, and a bronze lock" | correct | https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1098 | Bronze trigger mechanisms are the mark of the Chinese crossbow. |
| 32 | "Before it: The bow held drawn by the archer's own arm" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossbow | Hand bow. |
| 33 | "After it: The trigger of every later gun" | wrong | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matchlock | The matchlock's serpentine and lever work like a crossbow trigger, but flintlocks, percussion locks and modern triggers have other lines of descent. "Every" is too much. |
| 34 | "A farmer could learn to aim one in a few weeks" | could not verify | n/a | Repeated in popular accounts; no primary figure found. |
| 35 | "wars were no longer fought by noblemen in chariots, and each state filled its ranks with farmers by the tens of thousands" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_period | Mass conscript infantry replaced chariot nobles in the Warring States. |
| 36 | "The clay soldiers buried beside the First Emperor carried real crossbows, with bronze triggers" | correct | https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DB32EADD77493B84692756138D21D036/S0003598X00050262a.pdf/crossbows_and_imperial_craft_organisation_the_bronze_triggers_of_chinas_terracotta_army.pdf | Antiquity (2010) studies bronze triggers excavated from the pits; the wooden stocks rotted, the clay figures held real weapons. |

Replacement for 30: "About 2,500 years ago, in China".
Replacement for 33: "The crossbow of later Europe, and the trigger lever of the first matchlock guns".

## 22 The layered bow

| # | Claim | Verdict | Source | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 37 | "First made: More than 3,500 years ago, in western Asia and on the steppe" | imprecise | https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-025-09750-4 | Šaffa (2026) puts the full composite bow in the second millennium BCE, probably in the Near East, spreading quickly across Eurasia; the oldest bows in hand are Egyptian, about 1500 to 1300 BCE (Tutankhamun). Steppe origin is not supported. "More than 3,500" is not firm either. |
| 38 | "Made of: Wood, horn and sinew, held with glue" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_bow | Standard construction. |
| 39 | "What it does: Stores the force of a long bow in a short one" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_bow | Higher draw force for length. |
| 40 | "Before it: The bow cut from a single piece of wood" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self_bow | Self bow. |
| 41 | "After it: The bows of every horse people, until the gun" | imprecise | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_bow | True of the Asian and Near Eastern horse peoples; American Plains horsemen used self and sinew-backed bows. Guns replaced it from the 15th century on, though Turkish archers served to about 1591 and some Asian use went on later. |
| 42 | "the side that faces the target is stretched and the side that faces the archer is squeezed. Sinew resists stretching... horn resists squeezing" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_bow | Sinew on the back (tension), horn on the belly (compression). |
| 43 | "A short bow can be swung from one side of a horse's neck to the other" | correct | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_archer | Short bows allow shooting on either side from horseback. |

Replacement for 37: "About 3,500 years ago, in western Asia; the horse peoples of the steppe took it up soon after".
Replacement for 41: "The bows of the horse peoples of Asia, until the gun".
