I found three factual issues and two design issues. No files were edited.

1. **Papyrus is misidentified as paper; the chapter makes the distinction correctly.**  
   > “Some are of papyrus, a kind of coarse paper made in Egypt from a marsh reed.”

   [Profile, line 15](toolbox/14-scroll/profile.md:15). Papyrus is made from layered strips of plant stem, rather than the dispersed fibres used in paper. “A writing material like coarse paper” would be accurate. [Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/Paper-exhibit/feature/funerary-text-on-papyrus) distinguishes the materials explicitly.

   The [chapter](chapters/14-one-god/chapter.md:54) says: “Pressed and dried, they became a sheet rather like thick, coarse paper.” The profile turns that comparison into an identification.

2. **The joining instruction is physically imprecise for papyrus.**  
   > “The sheets are joined edge to edge into one long strip.”

   [Profile, line 16](toolbox/14-scroll/profile.md:16). Papyrus sheets were glued with their edges **overlapping**, commonly by 1–2 centimetres, rather than simply meeting edge to edge. The explanation should specify overlapping edges. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/papyrus-making-in-egypt).

   This imprecision also occurs in the chapter and label 2; it is a shared factual problem, not a mismatch between them.

3. **Scrolls did not disappear outside Judaism. This also breaks the rule against demonstrably false absolutes.**  
   > “The word outlived the object for everybody else.”

   [Profile, line 31](toolbox/14-scroll/profile.md:31). Scrolls continued in Christian liturgy, administration and other uses throughout the Middle Ages; Chinese handscrolls also remained an established format. The correct distinction is that the codex displaced the scroll as the usual Mediterranean book format, while scrolls survived for particular purposes. [Harvard’s scroll introduction](https://medievalscrolls.fas.harvard.edu/intro/); [Metropolitan Museum’s Chinese handscroll account](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/chinese-handscrolls).

   “Everybody else” violates [style.md’s “No absolutes that one exception breaks” rule](style.md:30). This is a concrete counterexample, not an objection to confident narration.
