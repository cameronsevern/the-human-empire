# The rehydration sachet

| | |
|---|---|
| **First made** | Dhaka, 1968, in a cholera trial; WHO's global programme from 1978 |
| **Made of** | About 20 grams of salt, glucose, potassium chloride and citrate |
| **What it does** | Puts back the water and salt that diarrhoea drains away |
| **Before it** | Salt water dripped into a vein, first tried in Leith, 1832 |
| **After it** | A weaker mixture, from the early 2000s, given alongside zinc tablets |

## Why it mattered

The trick lies in the lining of the small intestine. Its cells have a carrier that pulls in sugar and sodium together, one molecule of sugar with two of sodium, and water follows the salt. Cholera's poison makes the gut pour out salt and water, but it leaves this carrier working. In 1978 the medical journal The Lancet called the discovery potentially the most important medical advance of the century.

It does not stop the diarrhoea. It keeps the body supplied with water and salt until the infection passes, usually within a few days. Even so, in 2015 only about two in five children with diarrhoea in the world were given oral rehydration.
