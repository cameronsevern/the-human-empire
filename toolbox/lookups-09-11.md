## Page 09: The clay envelope (09-clay-envelope/profile.md)

**1.** "About 4,000 years ago, on the plain of Iraq" (First made).
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform ; https://emuseum.toledomuseum.org/objects/68885/sumerian-clay-tablet-in-its-sealed-envelope (Ur III, ca. 2100-2000 BCE, Umma)
- Evidence: Wikipedia's Cuneiform article dates the practice of sealing tablets in envelopes to Early Dynastic IIIb (c. 2500-2340 BC). Dated museum examples are Ur III (about 2100-2000 BCE) in southern Iraq. They are common in the Old Babylonian and Old Assyrian periods (about 1900-1700 BCE), and the Old Assyrian ones were made in Anatolia (Kanesh), not Iraq. The page's date is fair for the peak, but "first made" is earlier.
- Replacement: "About 4,000 years ago, on the plain of Iraq, and soon after in Assyrian trading towns in Turkey" (table), or for the body "Clay envelopes were in use by about 2100 BCE and were common by 1900 BCE."

**2.** "Made of: A thin sheet of the same clay as the tablet inside."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/325846 ; https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_2010-6022-17 (blocked to my fetch, seen in search snippet)
- Evidence: museum entries describe envelopes ("cases") as clay shells around a clay tablet.

**3.** "Before it: The hollow clay ball that held a merchant's counting tokens."
- Verdict: correct, with a long gap.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulla_(seal) ; https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/from-accounting-to-writing/
- Evidence: hollow clay bullae holding tokens, with seal impressions, date to about 3500-3300 BCE (Uruk, Susa). That is about a thousand years before tablet envelopes. Whether the envelope descends directly from the bulla is Schmandt-Besserat's account and is disputed, though the shape and sealing idea are clearly related.
- Replacement (if wanted, plainer): "Before it: The hollow clay ball that held counting tokens, a thousand years earlier."

**4.** "The agreement is written on a tablet: who owes what to whom, by when, and who was watching."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/325846 ; https://www.worldhistory.org/image/2779/a-clay-tablet-with-its-envelope/
- Evidence: loan and sale tablets list parties, amounts, dates and witnesses.

**5.** "A thin sheet of clay is wrapped round the tablet and pinched shut."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform
- Evidence: the envelope was a clay sheet wrapped round the dried or nearly dry tablet; the ROM and BM photos (see ledger) show the seams.

**6.** "The same words are written again on the outside, so that anybody can read the terms without opening it."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://www.wisdomlib.org/mesopotamian/book/the-civilization-of-babylonia-and-assyria/d/doc3950.html ; https://cdli.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/artifacts/359512 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform
- Evidence: the envelope usually carries a copy of the tablet's text (King, Civilization of Babylonia and Assyria). Old Assyrian envelopes often repeat the text in full or in part, and some envelopes carry only the receiver's name and a seal, or a summary. Also the point was less "anybody can read it" than that the copy could be checked against the inside.
- Replacement: "The same words, or a short version of them, are written again on the outside, so that anybody can read the terms without opening it."

**7.** "The parties and the witnesses roll their seals across the outside. Each seal print stands for a person who agreed."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/325846 (envelope sealed with the seals of the two witnesses) ; https://harvardartmuseums.org/art/146548 (sender's seal impressed eight times)
- Evidence: seals of witnesses and of the contracting parties (often the debtor, or the sender of a letter) are rolled on the envelope.

**8.** "If the two sides ever disagree, a judge breaks the envelope and reads the tablet inside. That inner text is the one that counts."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://www.wisdomlib.org/mesopotamian/book/the-civilization-of-babylonia-and-assyria/d/doc3950.html ; https://emuseum.toledomuseum.org/objects/68885/sumerian-clay-tablet-in-its-sealed-envelope (search snippet: broken open on delivery and read)
- Evidence: King's account says the envelope holds a duplicate; if its authenticity were questioned, agreement of the two texts guaranteed the inner original. That supports "the inner text is the original and the check." Museum notes also say letters and many documents were broken open on delivery. No source I could reach says in so many words that a judge broke the envelope or that the inner text "counts" over the outer.
- Replacement: "If the two sides ever disagree, the envelope is broken open and the tablet inside is read against the outside. The inner text is the original."

**9.** "A scribe with a damp cloth and a stylus can turn a debt of ten shekels into a debt of twenty."
- Verdict: could not verify.
- Sources: none found for this specific scenario.
- Evidence: clay can be re-wetted and re-written, which is the general reason given for sealing. The specific method is the author's illustration, not a claim about a known case.

**10.** "A merchant could lend silver to a man he would not see again for a year, in a town six weeks away."
- Verdict: correct (as an illustration).
- Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/works-of-art/66.245.10 ; https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03088949v1/file/10-Michel-preprint.pdf ; https://www.ancient-history-sites.com/?p=149054
- Evidence: the Assur to Kanesh caravan route was about 1,000 km and took about six weeks (sources give five to eight). Silver loans and debt notes are the commonest Kanesh envelope texts (Istanbul "silver debt tablet and envelope", ledger of Chapter 11).

**11.** "Clay envelopes went out of use when people began to write on skins and on sheets of papyrus, which cannot be wrapped in mud."
- Verdict: imprecise (cause unverified; timing implied is too early).
- Sources: https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/publications/mesopotamia/2020-08-31.html ; https://cdli.earth/artifacts/428996 (Nimrud envelope set, Neo-Assyrian) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform
- Evidence: Aramaic on papyrus and skin was used beside cuneiform from the early first millennium BCE. But tablet-and-envelope sets are still found at Nimrud in the Neo-Assyrian period (911-612 BCE), and Wikipedia says the practice lasted to the end of cuneiform tablets. Envelopes were most common in the early second millennium and thinned out after. I found no source stating that skins and papyrus caused their end.
- Replacement: "Clay envelopes were used for about two thousand years. They faded out as clay tablets themselves gave way to ink on skin and sheets of papyrus, which cannot be wrapped in mud."

**12.** "Later peoples wrote a contract twice on one sheet, then rolled and sealed one half and left the other open to read."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: https://apps.lib.umich.edu/files/collections/papyrus/k12/reading/Zenon/doubledocs.html ; https://trismegistos.org/seals/overview_A.html
- Evidence: "double documents" on papyrus carry the same text twice; the inner copy (scriptura interior) is rolled, tied and sealed, and the outer copy (scriptura exterior) is left open to read. Trismegistos treats this as a Greek practice taken up in Egypt (Ptolemaic and Roman). The sources disagree on which half (upper or lower) was sealed, but both describe one sealed copy and one open copy, which is all the page claims. Note the Greek and Egyptian double document is on papyrus, not on clay.

**13.** "A document closed with wax, a letter in a gummed envelope, and the foil under the cap of a medicine bottle all rest on the same idea."
- Verdict: correct (general statement, not a dated fact).
- Evidence: all are tamper-evident seals. No lookup needed.

## Page 10: The spoked wheel (10-spoked-wheel/profile.md)

Numbering continues from page 09.

**14.** "About 4,000 years ago, on the grasslands east of the Ural mountains" (First made).
- Verdict: correct (with a caveat about "chariot").
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoked_wheel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintashta_culture ; https://www.discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/chariot-racers-of-the-steppes
- Evidence: the earliest physical evidence for spoked wheels is from Sintashta graves in the southern Urals (east side of the range), dated about 2000 BCE; Sintashta chariots appear between c. 2050 and 1750 BCE. What survives is the impression of wheels in soil, not the wood. Some scholars (Littauer and Crouwel) doubt that the Sintashta vehicles were true chariots. Small bronze wheel models from Acemhoyuk in Anatolia date to the early 18th century BCE, so Sintashta is the earliest, not the only early, evidence.

**15.** "Made of: Wood, with rawhide to bind the joints."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chariot_of_Tutankhamun.jpg (museum label text: body of bent wood bound with rawhide) ; https://tutankhamun.griffith.ox.ac.uk//scientific-notes/taa-i38-chariots/taa-i3841recto ; https://www.colleendarnell.com/blog/ancient-egyptian-chariots-an-introduction
- Evidence: rawhide binding is documented on Egyptian chariots (wet rawhide shrinks as it dries and tightens the joint; Carter also records glue and leather linings). For Sintashta only the soil impressions of wheels survive, so nothing is known about rawhide there.
- Replacement: "Made of: Wood, with rawhide or leather to bind and cover the joints (Egyptian chariots)."

**16.** "Ten or twelve thin spokes run out from the hub."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://www.discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/chariot-racers-of-the-steppes ; https://www.colleendarnell.com/blog/ancient-egyptian-chariots-an-introduction ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariot
- Evidence: Sintashta wheels had 8 to 12 spokes (Anthony, as reported by Discover). Egyptian chariot wheels had four spokes early on and six from the middle of the 18th Dynasty (the Tutankhamun and Florence wheels, see photos). Hittite wheels had four; Greek four to eight.
- Replacement: "Between four and twelve thin spokes run out from the hub: eight to twelve on the first steppe wheels, four or six on Egyptian chariots."

**17.** "They are all cut to exactly the same length, or the wheel will not be round."
- Verdict: correct (basic geometry; with the hub centred, equal spokes and a round rim give a round wheel).
- Evidence: no lookup needed.

**18.** "The rim is made of wood that has been soaked or steamed until it is soft, bent into a circle, and left to dry in that shape."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariots_of_Tutankhamun ; https://tutankhamun.griffith.ox.ac.uk//scientific-notes/taa-i38-chariots/taa-i3841recto ; https://the-past.com/ancient-egypt/136/feature/pharaohs-chariot-wheel-the-power-behind-the-throne/ (paywalled)
- Evidence: Egyptian wheels used artificially bent wood, glue and rawhide; the felloe was built from curved wooden pieces, and Carter describes spokes as V-shaped bent sections. Many sources say heat or steam was used to bend the timber, but I found no source that gives "soaked or steamed" for Sintashta, where only wheel impressions survive. The wheel was not always a single bent hoop: Egyptian felloes came in segments.
- Replacement: "The rim is made of wood that has been heated or steamed until it bends, curved into a circle or a few curved pieces, and left to dry in that shape."

**19.** "Each spoke sits in a socket in the hub and another in the rim."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: https://tutankhamun.griffith.ox.ac.uk//scientific-notes/taa-i38-chariots/taa-i3841recto
- Evidence: Carter's note says spokes are set into the nave and fixed to the felloe by tenon and mortise.

**20.** "When the wheel carries a load, the spokes underneath are squeezed between the two."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoke
- Evidence: Wikipedia: under load on the hub "the lowermost wooden spoke shortens and compresses"; wooden spokes carry load in compression, wire spokes only in tension.

**21.** "The solid wheel that it replaced weighs several times as much."
- Verdict: could not verify.
- Evidence: no source with weights found. Plausible (a plank wheel is a solid disc) but unmeasured here. If needed, "weighs far more" is safer than "several times".

**22.** "The wood between the hub and the rim only has to stop the two from moving closer together."
- Verdict: correct (for a wooden wheel carrying load in compression).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoke
- Evidence: see item 20.

**23.** "An ox will drag a heavy wagon all day at walking pace. A horse ... tires quickly under a dead weight."
- Verdict: could not verify.
- Evidence: plausible general statement, no source checked.

**24.** "Give it two light wheels and a small platform, and it will pull two men faster than anything on earth had moved before."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariot ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariots_of_Tutankhamun
- Evidence: chariots carried two men (driver and archer). "Faster than anything had moved before" is plausible but unproven; a horse alone, or a rider, moves as fast. The claim is better made about vehicles than about men.
- Replacement: "Give it two light wheels and a small platform, and it will pull two men faster than any vehicle had moved before."

**25.** "A spoked wheel needs a specialist who can bend timber without cracking it, cut a dozen spokes to match, and fit two dozen joints."
- Verdict: correct (twelve spokes give 24 joints; matches 8-12 spoke steppe wheels; Egyptian wheels had only 4 or 6).
- Sources: see item 16.

**26.** "For the next thousand years, the kings who wanted chariots had to keep such men in their palaces."
- Verdict: could not verify.
- Evidence: New Kingdom Egyptian, Hittite and Mycenaean palaces kept chariot workshops (the Linear B chariot tablets from Knossos and Pylos list wheelwrights and wheels), but I did not look up a source for the thousand-year span. Palace-run chariot workshops are well attested for the Late Bronze Age.

**27.** "The wooden spoked wheel lasted about four thousand years with one large improvement, an iron tyre shrunk onto the rim while hot."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoked_wheel
- Evidence: wooden-spoked wheels stayed in use largely unchanged until the 1870s; iron rims appear on Celtic chariots in the 1st millennium BCE. The shrink-fit method is how wheelwrights fitted tyres (heated hoop slightly smaller than the wheel, then cooled). Four thousand years from 2000 BCE reaches about the 1870s-1900s.

**28.** "Farm carts, stagecoaches and field guns all rolled on it, and so did the first motor cars."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benz_Patent-Motorwagen
- Evidence: Benz's first car (Nr. 1, 1886) had steel wire-spoked wheels with solid rubber tyres; wooden wheels came with Nr. 2 and later cars, with iron tyres on the rear wheels. Many early cars did run on wooden wheels.
- Replacement: "...and so did most of the first motor cars."

**29.** "The bicycle wheel turned the idea inside out. Its wire spokes are too thin to be squeezed, so the hub hangs from the spokes above it and does not stand on the ones below."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoke ; https://www.sheldonbrown.com/wheelbuild.html (cites Forester on the "hangs from the top spokes" myth)
- Evidence: wire spokes work only in tension, so first half is right. But "the hub hangs from the top spokes" is a simplification argued over by bicycle engineers: every spoke stays in tension, the lowest spokes just lose some, and the rim near the ground flattens slightly.
- Replacement: "Its wire spokes are too thin to be squeezed, so they carry the load by pulling: every spoke is held tight, and the ones under the hub lose a little of their pull."


## Page 11: The copper slab (11-copper-slab/profile.md)

Numbering continues from page 10. Main sources: Pulak, "The Uluburun Shipwreck and Late Bronze Age Trade" (Metropolitan Museum catalogue essay; PDF at https://www.blackwoodconservation.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Uluburun-Shipwreck-and-Late-Bronze-Age-Trade.pdf, text read directly), and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck .

**30.** "About 3,500 years ago, around the eastern Mediterranean" (First made).
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: Pulak PDF above; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot
- Evidence: Pulak: "the earliest evidence for full-size oxhide ingots comes from sixteenth-century B.C. sites on Crete and depictions in Egyptian tomb paintings." Wikipedia gives Late Minoan IB, c. 1500-1450 BC. That is about 3,500 to 3,600 years ago. They were cast on Cyprus and traded across the Mediterranean.

**31.** "Made of: Nearly pure copper, cast in an open mould."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: Pulak PDF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot
- Evidence: Pulak: analyses show raw or "blister" ingots of pure copper. Wikipedia: about 99% copper, porous, with slag inclusions, cast in open moulds with the smooth side against the mould.

**32.** "Before it: Copper traded as small round cakes, lumps and scrap."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: Pulak PDF (bun ingots on the wreck); https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/AUTHORS/Graziadio2014-CopperOxhideIngots.pdf ; search summary of a Levant ingot study: plano-convex ingots in the Middle Bronze Age II to Late Bronze Age I, oxhide in Late Bronze Age II.
- Evidence: bun (plano-convex) ingots are the earlier form in the Levant, from the Middle Bronze Age (for example at Hazor). They are small, 6 kg or so on the Uluburun wreck, and bun ingots kept being made alongside oxhides. The Wikipedia article does not say buns came first, so this rests on the Levant study and on Pulak calling some small Uluburun ingots an "earlier type". "Lumps and scrap" are fine for hacksilber-style metal.
- Replacement: "Before it: Copper traded as small round cakes called buns, and as lumps and scrap."

**33.** "The slab is nearly pure copper, about sixty centimetres long and as heavy as a full suitcase."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: Pulak PDF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot
- Evidence: Uluburun oxhide ingots average just under 24 kg (cleaned, 20.1 to 29.5 kg; originals were heavier before corrosion). Pulak's catalogue examples measure 77.1 x 43.7 cm (20.4 kg, four handles) and 62.2 x 37.8 cm (two handles). So most are 60 to 80 cm long. The brief's "25 to 30 kg" is the top of the range; the average is about 24 kg. A suitcase works as a comparison for 20 to 30 kg.
- Replacement: "The slab is nearly pure copper, about seventy centimetres long and as heavy as a full suitcase, around twenty-five kilograms."

**34.** "Its four corners are drawn out into handles. They give a grip for lifting, and a place to tie a rope."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: Pulak PDF note 2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot
- Evidence: Pulak says the shape probably evolved to let the ingots be loaded onto specially designed saddles or harnesses for transport by pack animals over long distances. Wikipedia: the protrusions probably made them easier to carry overland on pack animals. Not stated as a grip for men or a tie point for rope, though that is a reasonable reading. Also: of the Uluburun copper ingots, 317 have four handles and 31 have only two.
- Replacement: "Its four corners are drawn out into handles. They give a grip for lifting, and a way to strap the slab onto a pack animal."

**35.** "One face is rough and blistered. Molten copper was poured into an open mould cut in stone, and the top of the metal cooled in the air."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot ; Pulak PDF
- Evidence: the rough face is the open top that cooled in air, and the smooth face lay against the mould. That part is right. But the only mould found is a limestone one from Ras Ibn Hani in Syria; Craddock and others say limestone suits large simple shapes, while Bass and others argue ingots were normally cast in clay moulds. Pulak notes that ingots show evidence of multiple pours.
- Replacement: "One face is rough and blistered. Molten copper was poured into an open mould, and the top of the metal cooled in the air. The one mould found, at Ras Ibn Hani in Syria, is cut in stone."

**36.** "Many slabs carry a mark, cut or stamped in by the maker or the shipper."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: Pulak PDF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot
- Evidence: at Uluburun at least 162 copper oxhide ingots are incised with one to three marks on their top faces. Marks were made after casting or cooling, not at the smelter: Pulak says they were "probably made at a point of export or receipt rather than at primary production centers." At Cape Gelidonya 24 ingots carry stamps made while the metal was soft. Meanings are unknown; they are not known to be maker's marks.
- Replacement: "Many slabs carry a mark, cut or stamped in by someone handling the metal before it was loaded."

**37.** "One man carries a slab on his shoulder, with a hand on a corner to steady it. Egyptian tomb paintings show lines of men bringing them in just that way."
- Verdict: correct, with one change: the paintings are in a few tombs; the best known is Rekhmire.
- Sources: Pulak PDF (fig. 101: "Wall painting with Syrian carrying copper oxhide ingot. Thebes, Tomb of Rekhmire (TT 100), Dynasty 18, reigns of Thutmose III-Amenhotep II"); https://metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/544617 (Met facsimile "Syrians Bringing an Ingot and a Chariot, tomb of Rekhmire") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot (painted scenes 15th to 12th century BC)
- Evidence: in TT100 one register shows men carrying ox-hide ingots, labelled chiefs of Keftiu and the islands of the Great Sea, and another labelled chiefs of Retenu (Syria). The Rekhmire scenes show ingots carried on the shoulder. "Lines of men" is a fair summary of the procession; each tomb shows only a few. I did not confirm the hand-on-corner detail on the painting itself.

**38.** "Bronze tied the kingdoms of the eastern Mediterranean together, and this is the form in which the main ingredient travelled."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: Pulak PDF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluburun_shipwreck
- Evidence: the Uluburun cargo was about 10 tons of Cypriot copper in 354 ingots, plus about a ton of tin; alloying it all would give about 11 tons of bronze at 10 to 1. Lead-isotope work shows that most contemporary Mediterranean copper ingots came from Cyprus. Tin travelled in similar ingot shapes too, so "the main ingredient" is copper.

**39.** "A smelter in the hills of Cyprus turned green ore into metal."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://websites.ucy.ac.cy/aru/documents/PhD-theses-summaries/VanBrempt_PhD-summary_EN.pdf (Cyprus Late Bronze Age smelting) ; Pulak PDF (Apliki, Solea axis ores)
- Evidence: Cypriot copper came mainly from sulphide ores such as chalcopyrite, mined at places like Apliki, and smelted at sites such as Politiko-Phorades in the foothills of the Troodos. The oxidised, green copper minerals are a smaller part. Also, Politiko-Phorades was a primary smelting site that made matte. The oxhide ingots themselves were probably cast later at coastal centres.
- Replacement: "A smelter in the hills of Cyprus turned copper ore into metal."

**40.** "It is as heavy as one man can carry any distance, and no heavier."
- Verdict: could not verify.
- Evidence: a Biblical Archaeology Society caption says an ingot of about 36 pounds (16 kg) "could be hefted on one shoulder with little difficulty". Uluburun ingots average 24 kg. The statement is an opinion about design, not a measured fact. Pulak says the shape was made for pack animals, not for men.
- Replacement (if the link to men is dropped): "It is a load that one man can lift, and that two can sling on a pack animal."

**41.** "It is flat, so that it lies in the bottom of a hull and keeps the ship steady."
- Verdict: correct in outline.
- Sources: Pulak PDF
- Evidence: on the wreck the ingots were stowed in four transverse rows low in the hull, laid on beds of brushwood (dunnage) with the mould sides down, so as to increase friction and stop shifting. Their weight was ballast.

**42.** "On the wreck off the Turkish coast, the slabs were stacked in overlapping rows like roof tiles, so that they could not slide in a rough sea."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: Pulak PDF
- Evidence: Pulak: "Four distinct rows of copper ingots stretched transversely across the hull, overlapping like roof shingles. The direction of overlap alternated from layer to layer, apparently to prevent slippage." Each row had up to 12 ingots, stacked 8 to 11 layers deep. Wikipedia's herringbone-pattern wording is also fine. The wreck is off Uluburun, near Kas, southern Turkey, about 1320 BCE or the late 14th century BCE.

**43.** "The slabs were also all of about the same weight. A clerk on the quay did not have to weigh a shipment lump by lump. He could count it, and check a few."
- Verdict: imprecise (this is the claim most likely to be wrong).
- Sources: Pulak PDF ("Although they were not of a standardized quantity of metal, their broadly common weight groupings would have simplified accounting procedures"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot (Bass: Gelidonya weights were not standard; Pulak: weights allowed "a rough but quick reckoning"); https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/4949165 (links a heavier Uluburun group to a talent of about 29.6 kg)
- Evidence: weights varied from about 20 to 29.5 kg, average 24 kg. Pulak writes that they were raw copper "probably subject to weighing and evaluation during each commercial transaction". Bass argued the weights were not standard and the ingots were not a currency. The common weight groupings made counting a rough guide only.
- Replacement: "The slabs were also of about the same weight, within a few kilograms. A clerk on the quay could count a shipment and get a rough total, and then weigh a few to check."

**44.** "Slabs of this one pattern have been found from Sardinia to Syria, which means that smiths and merchants across the whole sea had agreed on what a load of copper looked like."
- Verdict: first half correct; second half is inference.
- Sources: Pulak PDF ("as far west as Sardinia and Sicily, and as far east as central and eastern Anatolia, Syria, and Mesopotamia ... southern Germany, Bulgaria, mainland Greece, western and southern Anatolia, Cyprus, the southern Levant, and the Nile Delta") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot
- Evidence: finds span at least Sardinia to Syria, and a good deal further. Pulak thinks the shape was for loading onto pack animals; "agreed on what a load looked like" is a fair gloss but not a documented agreement, and the weight standard behind it is disputed (item 43).
- Replacement: "Slabs of this one pattern have been found from Sardinia to Syria, and as far as Bulgaria and southern Germany. Smiths and merchants right across the sea were casting, trading and recognising the same shape."

**45.** "The name archaeologists use for it is the oxhide ingot, since its outline looks like a hide pegged out to dry."
- Verdict: correct.
- Sources: Pulak PDF, note 2
- Evidence: Pulak: such ingots were "originally termed 'oxhide' when it was believed, erroneously, that they were cast to resemble dried oxhides (Bass 1967)." So the outline is the reason for the name, though the resemblance is not why the shape was made. Optional addition: "The name is a guess about the shape, not the reason for it."

**46.** "The shape went out of use when the palaces fell."
- Verdict: imprecise.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot ; Pulak PDF
- Evidence: oxhide ingots were in use from the 16th century to the early 12th century BCE, with late examples in Sardinia dated to about 1000 BC. The Late Bronze Age palaces and the Cypriot copper trade collapsed around 1200-1177 BCE. The pattern thinned out after that, but did not stop at once.
- Replacement: "The shape faded out over the two centuries after the palaces fell."
