# The cassette tape

| | |
|---|---|
| **First made** | Philips, at Hasselt in Belgium; shown in Berlin in 1963 |
| **Made of** | A plastic shell, two hubs, and plastic tape coated with iron oxide |
| **What it does** | Stores sound as patterns of magnetism on a moving tape |
| **Before it** | Reel-to-reel recorders, whose tape had to be threaded by hand |
| **After it** | The compact disc of 1982, from Philips and Sony |

## Why it mattered

Philips meant the cassette for dictation, and at first its sound was poor: the tape crept past the head at under five centimetres a second. What made it the world's format was a business decision. From 1966 Philips let any manufacturer use the design free of charge, so every cassette fitted every machine. By 1968, 85 companies had sold 2.4 million players.

A cassette needed no studio, no licence and no printing press. Anyone with two machines could copy one, and the copies could be copied again. A government that owned the radio stations could not follow a tape from hand to hand. In 1979, the year of the revolution in Iran, Sony put the Walkman on sale, and the cassette went into people's pockets.
