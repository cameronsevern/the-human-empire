**[01 — The hand axe](toolbox/01-hand-axe/profile.md) — 360 words**

1. **Doubtful claim; chapter tension — high confidence.** “Somebody hit one stone with another and used whatever sharp piece came off. Any shape would do.” Earlier flake-making involved controlled strikes and selection, not simply accepting whatever happened. Chapter 1 explicitly says there were “right and wrong places to strike.” The early toolkit also included cores and hammerstones, making “The oldest stone tools were flakes” incomplete. [Smithsonian overview](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/behavior/stone-tools/early-stone-age-tools)

2. **Unproven cognitive claim — high confidence that qualification is needed.** “The maker has to hold a picture of the finished tool in mind,” and “It is the oldest thing we know of that was made to a design.” A mental template is an interpretation, not something the shape proves. Experiments can produce handaxe-like forms without a predetermined finished design. The defensible point is deliberate, repeated shaping of both faces; priority for *planning itself* is not established. [Experimental study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4938430/)

3. **Design.** “Its true heir is the habit it began, of deciding what a thing should be before starting to make it.” Apart from repeating the unsupported priority claim, this leaves “Where it went” abstract: the brief asks for a connection to something the reader owns or has seen.

4. **Design.** “Why it mattered” has three paragraphs. “The real thing” is absent despite the licensed hand-axe photograph already recorded in chapter 1’s [image ledger](chapters/01-what-is-a-human/images/ledger.md).

**[02 — The needle](toolbox/02-needle/profile.md) — 359 words**

1. **Wrong arithmetic — very high confidence.** “In ten thousand generations, the only real change to the design came in the nineteenth century…” Forty to fifty thousand years represents roughly **1,600–2,500 generations**, using 20–25 years per generation. Ten thousand generations would imply roughly 200,000–250,000 years.

2. **Misleading technological necessity — high confidence.** “Tiny, close stitches are what make fitted clothing possible: sleeves, trousers, hoods, mittens and boots, cut to the body and worn in layers.” In context this makes the eyed needle the prerequisite for fitted clothes. Tailored clothing could be made using awls before eyed needles; needles facilitated finer sewing and more elaborate garments. Chapter 2 shares the simplification, so this is a factual issue rather than a contradiction between the two texts. [Research review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212769/)

3. **Design.** “Why it mattered” has three paragraphs. “The real thing” is absent although chapter 2’s [image ledger](chapters/02-everywhere/images/ledger.md) already supplies a licensed photograph of a surviving eyed needle.

**[03 — The lamp](toolbox/03-lamp/profile.md) — 355 words**

1. **Overgeneralized performance claim — high confidence.** “A torch gives more light than a lamp, but it has to be held, it smokes heavily, and it burns out in under an hour.” A torch can be propped or mounted, and burn time depends on construction and fuel. Experimental prehistoric torches have lasted about an hour; “under an hour” is not a general limit. The useful distinction is steadier, longer-lasting stationary light from a suitably fuelled lamp. [Experimental study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250497)

2. **Design.** “Why it mattered” has three paragraphs. “The real thing” is missing despite an available [licensed photograph of the Lascaux lamp](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lampe_a_graisse_-_Lascaux.jpg).

No clear contradiction with chapter 3 or numbered-label mismatch.

**[04 — The bow](toolbox/04-bow/profile.md) — 397 words**

1. **Unsupported factual claim; chapter contradiction — high confidence.** “Most of the oldest known victims of war, on the Nile and beside Lake Turkana, have arrow points among their bones.” This conflates evidence of wounds, embedded projectiles and specifically arrowheads. Nataruk includes substantial blunt-force trauma and embedded stone points in only some individuals. Chapter 4 describes clubs at Nataruk and “arrows and light spears” at Jebel Sahaba; it does not support this majority claim. [Nataruk findings](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/evidence-of-a-prehistoric-massacre-extends-the-history-of-warfare), [Jebel Sahaba reassessment](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-89386-y)

2. **Wrong mechanics — very high confidence.** “When the string is let go, the stave snaps straight and gives all the stored effort back in a fraction of a second.” A strung bow returns toward its **braced curve**, not a straight stave. Only part of its stored energy reaches the arrow; energy also remains in moving limbs and string or becomes vibration and heat. This also conflicts with step 2’s statement that the bow is already bent.

3. **Wrong explanation of throwing — high confidence.** “An arm can throw a spear about as hard as it can push.” Throwing uses coordinated body motion and elastic energy storage, particularly in the shoulder. It is not limited to a simple arm push. The bow’s explanation should stand on its own storage-and-release mechanism. [Biomechanics research](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12267)

4. **False safety claim — very high confidence.** “With a bow, he could kill from a distance at which he was in no danger.” Distance can reduce exposure; it does not remove danger from an opponent’s missiles. The relevant advantage is attacking beyond an opponent’s effective reach, not immunity.

5. **Partial prompt mismatch.** “The arrow is light: a thin shaft, feathers to keep it straight, and a small point of stone.” Step 5 describes the entire arrow, while label 5 specifies **“A point of stone, glued and bound.”** The point corresponds, but its fastening—the label’s substantive detail—is missing.

6. **Design.** “Why it mattered” has three paragraphs. “The real thing” is missing; [Holmegaard bow photographs](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Holmegaard1.JPG) are available. Keeping the schematic as the main illustration correctly follows the weapons exception.

**[05 — The sickle](toolbox/05-sickle/profile.md) — 411 words**

1. **Missing causal step; chapter contradiction — very high confidence.** “Year after year, that small difference picked out the wheat that does not drop its grain, and wild wheat slowly turned into a crop.” Harvesting alone does not produce the described domestication: the retained grain must also be **sown**, allowing its traits to become more common in subsequent crops. Chapter 5 explicitly identifies re-sowing as “the step that matters.” Without it, removing non-shattering ears does not increase their descendants in the wild stand.

2. **Wrong earliest date — high confidence.** “First made — About 15,000 years ago…” Evidence for composite cereal-harvesting tools at Ohalo II dates to about **23,000 years ago**. Around 15,000 years ago is appropriate for their becoming common in Natufian assemblages, not their earliest known appearance. [Ohalo II study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0167151)

3. **Misleading contrast — high confidence.** “Pulling a plant up by hand takes the whole plant, loose seed and all.” Grain already shed onto the ground does not come up with the roots. Both uprooting and cutting can lose shattered grain; harvesting time, handling and subsequent sowing matter.

4. **Unsupported exclusivity — high confidence.** “It is useful only to people who harvest grass seed in bulk…” Sickles can also cut stems for fodder, thatching and other uses. Consequently, “A sickle is the oldest tool made for one crop” is not justified: the implement is neither confined to one crop nor necessarily to collecting seed.

5. **Overcertain identification — high confidence.** “Any flake with that shine was used for harvesting.” Similar-looking polish can result from other activities, including cutting reeds; identifying cereal harvesting requires examination of the wear pattern, not merely a visible shine. [Experimental use-wear evidence](https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789088900334.pdf)

6. **Design.** “Why it mattered” has three paragraphs. “The real thing” is missing. The open-access Ohalo II paper includes photographs of surviving harvesting blades under a CC BY licence, although these are components rather than a complete sickle.

**[06 — The clay tablet](toolbox/06-clay-tablet/profile.md) — 370 words**

1. **False universal ancestry — high confidence.** “After it — Every written record since.” Writing arose independently in multiple regions; not every written tradition descends from Mesopotamian tablets. A narrower connection to the Mesopotamian record-keeping tradition would be sound. [Library of Congress overview](https://wwws.loc.gov/exhibits/exploring-the-early-americas/language-and-context.html)

2. **Chronological conflation with the chapter — high confidence.** “First made — About 5,300 years ago, in the city of Uruk…” Chapter 6 distinguishes earlier **numerical tablets** from the addition of drawings at Uruk around 5,300 years ago. The card dates the latter development as though it were the invention of the tablet itself. Numerical tablets belong earlier in the late fourth millennium BCE. [Research overview](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/8f6a2e30-b5c0-4812-af6d-999d115dda53/Numeracy%20at%20the%20dawn%20of%20writing_Ferrara.pdf)

3. **Incomplete preservation explanation — high confidence.** “When cities burned, their records were fired and preserved. That is why hundreds of thousands of tablets survive.” Accidental firing preserved some archives, but many tablets survive unfired; some were deliberately baked. Clay’s durability is the broader explanation, not burning cities alone. [Library of Congress collection description](https://www.loc.gov/collections/cuneiform-tablets/about-this-collection/)

4. **Design: substantial repetition.** “Counting begins with tokens,” through “A small drawing beside the numbers says what was counted,” repeats chapter 6’s complete tokens → envelopes → impressions → tablets → drawings sequence. Some overlap is necessary for explaining the object, but this largely retells the chapter passage despite the design’s instruction against doing so.

5. **Design.** “Why it mattered” has three paragraphs. “The real thing” is missing although chapter 6’s [image ledger](chapters/06-taxes/images/ledger.md) already records a licensed photograph of a surviving tablet.

**[07 — The sledge](toolbox/07-sledge/profile.md) — 397 words**

1. **Wrong absolute — high confidence.** “Nothing in Egypt could lift it.” Egyptians could raise heavy stones using mechanical advantage, including ramps and levering; the precise construction arrangements remain debated. “Too heavy for the haulers to lift directly” would express the intended point without denying their lifting capabilities. [Egyptological discussion](https://gizamedia.rc.fas.harvard.edu/documents/lehner_treasures_032-045.pdf)

2. **Incorrect account of the demonstrated mechanism — high confidence.** “The principle is unchanged: spread the weight, and make the ground slippery.” In the sand experiment, water primarily **stiffens the sand through capillary bridges**, reducing the heap pushed ahead of the runners. That differs from lubricating a ship’s slipway. Step 4 explains the sand mechanism correctly; the ending blurs it. [University of Amsterdam research](https://iop.uva.nl/content/news/2014/00/prl-egyptian-pyramids.html)

3. **Design: chapter connection missing.** “The sledge.” Chapter 7 discusses pyramid construction but does not actually mention a sledge or ramp. This fails the requirement to profile a tool that appeared in the matching chapter; it is an omission in the pairing, not a factual contradiction.

4. **Design.** “Why it mattered” has three paragraphs. “The real thing” is missing despite the Met’s [public-domain photograph of a surviving Egyptian sledge](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545485).

The numbered labels match. Separately, the prompt’s scene requests about **six** visible haulers while its own label 3 says **twenty**: that inconsistency is inside the prompt, not between the steps and labels.

**[08 — The cylinder seal](toolbox/08-cylinder-seal/profile.md) — 374 words**

1. **Wrong replacement material — high confidence.** “When people began to write on parchment in place of clay, the cylinder gave way to a flat seal pressed into wax.” The relevant Near Eastern transition included stamp seals impressed into **clay bullae securing parchment or papyrus documents**. Substituting wax makes the transition misleading. [Metropolitan Museum account](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Ancient_Art_in_Miniature_Ancient_Near_Eastern_Seals_from_the_Collection_of_Martin_and_Sarah_Cherkask.pdf)

2. **Overstated security — high confidence.** “Nobody could get in without breaking the clay, and nobody without the seal could replace it.” A sealing provided evidence of interference and authority, not an unforgeable lock. Experimental research demonstrates ways to defeat clay sealings using materials available in antiquity. The sound claim is that opening normally broke the sealing and an unauthorized replacement risked detection. [Security study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/were-ancient-seals-secure/28C74293459BCB5270F602CF6EB432A1)

3. **Design: chapter connection missing.** “The cylinder seal.” Chapter 8 contains no cylinder seal or explanation of its use. As with the sledge, the selected tool has not appeared in its matching chapter.

4. **Design.** “Why it mattered” has three paragraphs. “The real thing” is missing despite available [public-domain photographs of surviving cylinder seals](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327630).
