I found four factual issues, one label imprecision, and several design/style issues. No files were edited.

1. **The rock and pillar edicts are not the same texts.** [Card, line 8](toolbox/20-edict-pillar/profile.md:8):

   > “The same orders cut into boulders and cliffs, wherever the rock happened to lie”

   The major rock and pillar edicts are distinct series issued at different times, although their concerns overlap. For example, the Kalinga account is Rock Edict XIII; the three-day reprieve for condemned prisoners is Pillar Edict IV. “Earlier royal edicts cut into boulders and cliffs” would be accurate. [Translations of the edicts](https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/dhammika/wheel386.html).

2. **Buried surfaces were not uniformly rough.** [Step 5](toolbox/20-edict-pillar/profile.md:19):

   > “Nobody was going to see this part, and so the masons left it rough, still covered in the marks of their tools.”

   Some pillars were polished below ground; others had rough buried portions. Falk identifies polished underground surfaces at Gotihawa and Kumrahar. The sentence converts one construction practice into a universal explanation. This also breaks the style rule against absolutes with known exceptions. Label 5 and the prompt repeat the generalisation. [Harry Falk, *Aśokan Sites and Artefacts*, pp. 146–147](https://dokumen.pub/aokan-sites-and-artefacts-1nbsped-3805337124.html).

   This is **not a direct contradiction of the chapter’s wording**: the chapter avoids describing the buried finish. However, its [lookup item 46](chapters/20-ashoka/lookups.md) explicitly records this correction.

3. **“Reading height” is misleading as a general description.** [Step 4](toolbox/20-edict-pillar/profile.md:18):

   > “The king's words are cut into the shaft in neat rows, low enough for a person standing beside it to read them.”

   At Topra, the lowest line of Edicts I–VI was approximately 2.25 metres above the original ground, with the inscription extending another 1.2 metres upwards. These were not uniformly convenient eye-level inscriptions. Label 4 and the prompt’s head-height band repeat the problem. [Falk, *Aśokan Sites and Artefacts*, p. 216](https://dokumen.pub/aokan-sites-and-artefacts-1nbsped-3805337124.html).

4. **Iron pillars were made more than once.** [Final paragraph](toolbox/20-edict-pillar/profile.md:31):

   > “Indian kings also went on raising columns of their own, in stone and once in iron.”

   Besides Delhi’s iron pillar, there is the substantial iron pillar at Dhar, now broken into three pieces. Replace “once in iron” with “sometimes in iron,” or identify Delhi as one example. This also violates the rule against exception-breaking absolutes. [Archaeological report, “The Iron Pillar at Dhār”](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/13956.pdf).

5. **Label 2 identifies the whole capital with only its lowest part.** [Step 2](toolbox/20-edict-pillar/profile.md:16):

   > “The capital is carved from a second block, and a copper bolt holds it on.”
   >
   > “Its lowest part is a bell shaped like a lotus flower turned upside down.”

   The prompt and illustration instead label it:

   > “The capital: a bell shaped like a lotus turned upside down”

   The prose correctly distinguishes the bell from the complete capital. The [chapter](chapters/20-ashoka/chapter.md:129) explicitly includes bell, platform and animal in the capital. “The capital’s bell-shaped lower part” would make the label precise. **There are no swapped numbers:** all five callouts point to the corresponding parts.

6. **The chapter comparison is weakened by a misleading contrast.** [First “Why it mattered” paragraph](toolbox/20-edict-pillar/profile.md:23):

   > “A king who writes on boulders must therefore put his words where the rock is, which may be a hillside that few people pass.”

   This is possible in the abstract, but the [chapter specifically places Ashoka’s rock inscriptions](chapters/20-ashoka/chapter.md:125) “beside roads, near towns and along the frontiers.” It creates an implication of poorly situated rock edicts that the chapter does not support. The defensible distinction is greater freedom to choose a pillar’s location, rather than an established improvement in audience size. This is a misleading implication, **not a literal contradiction**.

7. **An unexplained historical term and compressed chronology.** [Final paragraph](toolbox/20-edict-pillar/profile.md:31):

   > “One of them, now at Allahabad, carries Ashoka's edicts, then a long poem in praise of a conquering king who lived six centuries after him, and then some lines added for a Mughal emperor about 420 years ago.”

   “Mughal” is neither explained here nor introduced in the preceding chapter, contrary to **Plain words**. The sentence also packs three rulers and two different ways of measuring elapsed time into one sentence, working against **Give the reader room**. The underlying sequence is not the problem.

8. **The ending repeats the chapter’s ending.** [Last sentence](toolbox/20-edict-pillar/profile.md:31):

   > “The four lions from the top of the pillar at Sarnath, meanwhile, are printed on every Indian banknote and stamped on the cover of every Indian passport.”

   The chapter has just ended with the lions on Indian coins, banknotes and passports. This supplies the required familiar descendant, but repeats the same concluding image despite the toolbox instruction that the page should not retell the chapter.
